# Research: For children specifically aged 9-12, does perceived and/or actual competence predict durable, sustained voluntary interest in an activity better than free choice / autonomy-based self-selection? Seeking longitudinal or experimental evidence with effect sizes located at the 9-12 age band specifically (not adolescent 12-17, not early childhood 6-9), on whether competence-building versus giving children free choice better predicts who continues an activity over months or years.

_2026-08-01_

## Summary
For children aged 9-12 specifically, the evidence does not support a clean claim that competence predicts durable voluntary interest better than free choice/autonomy — the two most direct, high-quality tests point in opposite directions. Controlled experiments in the band (Mabbe et al. 2018, mean age 10.71) show felt competence, not felt autonomy, was the proximal driver of voluntary persistence/challenge-seeking (competence satisfaction b=.31 vs autonomy b=.01; competence manipulation eta-sq up to .65 vs autonomy .07-.08), yet an equally strong meta-analysis (Deci/Koestner/Ryan 1999) finds positive competence feedback does NOT enhance children's free-choice intrinsic motivation (d=0.11 ns vs d=0.43 for adults), and choice/autonomy reliably raises intrinsic motivation at elementary age (Patall meta d=0.51). Real-world longitudinal and RCT tests are sobering for the competence-building hypothesis: an 11-week competence-oriented soccer program did not raise competence, interest, or value and interest declined in both groups (Skjesol 2025, n=175), and in the Childhood-and-Beyond longitudinal cohort a child's own perceived competence predicted the DEPTH of later participation but NOT whether they continued at all — only parental influence predicted continuation. Where competence does matter for durability, it appears to act as a mediator of persistence and dropout (declining perceived competence predicts sports dropout more than actual skill loss; perceived competence mediates childhood motor skill → adolescent activity) rather than as a lever that outperforms autonomy. The most defensible synthesis: at 9-12, competence and autonomy operate as parallel, jointly necessary mechanisms, not as a hierarchy where competence beats free choice.

## Findings

### 1. In experimental tests within the 9-12 band, felt competence (not felt autonomy) was the proximal driver of voluntary persistence and challenge-seeking, and a competence manipulation produced far larger effects on competence satisfaction and intrinsic motivation than an autonomy manipulation.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2018_MabbeSoenensVansteenkiste_ExperimentalChildPsych.pdf>

Mabbe, Soenens, De Muynck & Vansteenkiste (2018), J. Experimental Child Psychology 170:134-160 (n=110, mean age 10.71, Flanders elementary schools). Competence satisfaction predicted behavioral challenge-seeking (b=.31, p=.000, CI[.14,.47]) whereas autonomy satisfaction did not (b=.01, p=.89). Only the feedback-valence (competence) manipulation had a main effect on challenge-seeking (eta-sq=.06); the communication-style (autonomy) manipulation did not. The competence manipulation produced large effects on competence satisfaction (partial eta-sq=.65) and intrinsic motivation (eta-sq=.33) vs small autonomy effects (.08, .07). NOTE: outcome is 'behavioral challenge seeking,' paraphrased as 'voluntary persistence.'

### 2. An 11-week competence-oriented (skill-training) soccer program for 9-12 year-olds did not increase perceived competence, interest/enjoyment, or value versus a no-program control; interest declined significantly in BOTH groups; effects were very small with limited practical relevance.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint7040101>

Skjesol et al. (2025), Psychology International 7(4):101, RCT of 175 children aged 9-12 who voluntarily applied, randomized 100 intervention / 75 control. The program 'did not affect the children's perceived competence, interest/enjoyment, or value/usefulness.' Interest/enjoyment decreased significantly pre-to-post in both groups; the control group actually reported HIGHER perceived competence at both timepoints; 'effect sizes are very small... practical relevance is small.' CAVEAT: the program was a traditional soccer-skill program explicitly NOT designed around SDT/competence-support, so 'competence-building intervention' overstates what was tested.

### 3. In the Childhood-and-Beyond longitudinal cohort, a child's own perceived competence/motivation in middle childhood did NOT predict WHETHER they continued sports into adolescence (only parental influence did); it predicted only the DEPTH (weekly hours) of participation among continuers.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/305043768_A_Longitudinal_Comparison_of_Parent_and_Child_Influence_on_Sports_Participation>

'A Longitudinal Comparison of Parent and Child Influence on Sports Participation' (J. Youth Development, 2013), CAB dataset. 'Only parent influences were predictive of whether the child continued.' Child motivation was a significant predictor of amount of time spent, not of continuation. LIMITATIONS: middle-childhood wave sampled Grades 3/4/6 (~8-12, overlapping but not confined to 9-12); the child predictor is a composite conflating perceived competence with task value (alpha .93-.95), so competence cannot be isolated and autonomy is not measured.

### 4. Positive competence feedback does NOT enhance children's durable (free-choice) intrinsic motivation, unlike in adults — a significant age difference the authors attribute to children experiencing the feedback as controlling.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf>

Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin 125(6) meta-analysis. Verbal-reward/feedback effect for children d=0.11 (ns, CI -0.11 to 0.34) vs college students d=0.43 (0.27-0.58); significant age difference Qb(1)=5.14, p<.02. 'Positive feedback did not enhance the free-choice intrinsic motivation of children,' with the interpretation that children experienced it as more controlling. Directly contradicts the competence-over-autonomy framing for durable motivation.

### 5. Providing free choice/autonomy reliably increases intrinsic motivation at elementary age, and where directly tested at 9-12 the choice effect was mediated JOINTLY by autonomy AND competence — not competence over autonomy.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2008_PatallCooperRobinson_PsychBulletin.pdf>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022096519301110>

Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008), Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis: choice raises intrinsic motivation overall d=0.30 (FE, 46 effect sizes; CI 0.25-0.35) and d=0.51 for primary-school students (k=10), with no significant age-band differences. Direct experiment (J. Experimental Child Psychology 2019, N=126, mean age 10.8): choice provision enhanced intrinsic motivation and vitality BECAUSE children experienced more autonomy AND competence need satisfaction — parallel mechanisms, undercutting a clean 'competence beats free choice' hierarchy.

### 6. Where competence bears on durability, it acts largely as a mediator: perceived competence links early motor skill to later activity, and DECLINING perceived competence predicts dropout more consistently than actual skill loss.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2569960/>, <https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jmld/8/3/article-p457.xml>, <https://www.ideafit.com/why-kids-drop-out-of-sports-and-how-coaches-can-keep-them-engaged/>

Barnett et al. PASS longitudinal cohort (PMC2569960, childhood mean 10.1 → adolescence): perceived sports competence mediated childhood object-control skill → adolescent physical activity and fitness; mediated model explained 18% of PA and 30% of fitness variance. Peer-reviewed motor-development work: children who underestimate physical abilities show lower motivation, higher anxiety, and withdrawal (JMLD 8(3), though that cohort spans grades 2-4, ~7.8-9.8, mostly below the band). A secondary practitioner source states declining perceived ability predicts dropout more consistently than skill loss. Perceived competence matters for who quits, but this frames it as a protective mediator, not as outperforming autonomy.

### 7. Actual competence and motivation/enjoyment dissociate at ages 9-12: the majority of children have low actual motor competence yet medium perceived competence and high motivation/enjoyment, and authors recommend weighting affective factors equally with competence.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1080/17408989.2023.2265399>

Carcamo-Oyarzun et al. (2023), Physical Education and Sport Pedagogy (DOI 10.1080/17408989.2023.2265399), cross-sectional person-centered LCA of 730 Chilean schoolchildren (mean age 11.22, grades 5-6, no longitudinal follow-up). Largest profile (67.5%, 'nonaligned') had low actual motor competence but medium perceived competence and high motivation/enjoyment; a 'high aligned' profile (21.9%) was more active with lower BMI concurrently. Authors recommend PE emphasize psychological/affective factors equally alongside physical development, implying competence is not the dominant engagement driver. CAVEAT: cross-sectional (cannot establish durable prediction) and full text paywalled, so exact figures unverified; the 'recommendation' interpretation split 1-1.

## Caveats
Design mismatch dominates: almost none of the strongest sources directly pit competence-building against free choice for the SAME children over months/years in the 9-12 band. Controlled experiments (Mabbe 2018; the N=126 choice study) measure short-term persistence/intrinsic motivation, not durable continuation over months/years. The two studies that DO track real continuation (CAB longitudinal sports; Skjesol RCT) find competence weak or null for continuation. Direct conflicts remain unresolved: (a) Mabbe finds competence a stronger short-term driver than autonomy, while Deci/Koestner/Ryan and Patall find autonomy/choice robust and competence feedback null for children's free-choice motivation; (b) the CAB study finds child perceived competence does NOT predict continuation, while dropout and mediation studies treat perceived competence as a key predictor of withdrawal — reconcilable if perceived competence is a mediator/protective factor rather than a stand-alone lever, and if 'depth' vs 'whether-continued' are distinct outcomes. Source-quality flags: the Skjesol RCT is MDPI (modest reputation flag) and its intervention was not actually SDT/competence-designed, so it is weak evidence about deliberate competence-building; the dropout claim [14] rests on a practitioner blog; the Chilean LCA and JMLD articles were paywalled (403), so exact figures and 'cross-sectional' wording were not independently re-verified. Age-band fit is imperfect throughout: CAB spans grades 3-6 (~8-12), JMLD spans grades 2-4 (~7.8-9.8, mostly below 9-12), and Barnett measures outcomes in adolescence. One claim was refuted for transparency: the assertion that 'sustained engagement narrows to children who repeatedly experience success' was voted down 0-3.

## Open questions
- Is there any study that experimentally or longitudinally compares a competence-building condition against a free-choice/autonomy condition for the SAME 9-12 cohort and tracks voluntary continuation over months/years — the exact contrast the question asks — or does all current evidence only measure short-term motivation or single-mechanism designs?
- Does perceived competence function as a mediator/gatekeeper (necessary to prevent dropout) rather than a driver that outperforms autonomy — i.e., are competence and autonomy jointly necessary, with neither sufficient alone, at 9-12?
- Why does competence feedback enhance durable intrinsic motivation in adults but not in children (Deci/Koestner/Ryan): is the 'experienced as controlling' mechanism specific to externally delivered feedback, and would internally experienced mastery/competence satisfaction behave differently for durability?
- Do these patterns hold outside sports/physical-education contexts (which dominate the located evidence) — e.g., in cognitive, artistic, or self-selected hobby domains more relevant to a discovery/passion product?
