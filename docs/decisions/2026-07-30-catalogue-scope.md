# What stays in the catalogue, and why prestige is the wrong axis

**Status:** **Applied, then added to.** The cut described here took the catalogue to 37. It is now **44**: PR #267 added four county-fair pursuits, #269 added acting, reciting and public speaking, and #268 ruled sport out of scope. The cuts below still stand; the number does not. The seven that failed the second test on a
structural fact are gone; the fifteen marginals stay, because the owner chose to keep both tests
rather than replace one with the other (§4c).
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Why now:** we proposed cutting low-status pursuits (Pokémon Cards, Comics & Manga, Speedcubing,
Scrabble, Backgammon) on the grounds that top universities would not credit them. Three independent
literature sweeps say that premise is unsupported, that the opposing argument is *also* unsupported,
and that the real mechanism is something neither position anticipated.

---

## 1. Both sides of the original argument lose

### The cut is not supported

Harvard's internal **Reading Procedures for the Class of 2023**, produced under court order in
*SFFA v. Harvard* (HARV00097939, Trial Exhibit P633), grade the extracurricular rating **purely by
level of attainment**. The scale never enumerates a qualifying domain:

> **1.** Unusual strength in one or more areas. Possible national-level achievement or professional
> experience. A potential major contributor at Harvard. Truly unusual achievement.
> **2.** Strong secondary school contribution in one or more areas such as class president,
> newspaper editor, concertmaster etc. … Possible local or regional recognition.
> **3.** Solid participation but without special distinction.

Read literally, a nationally-ranked Pokémon TCG player and a national science olympian both score a
1. MIT and Princeton say the same thing in nearly identical words — MIT: *"we don't value one type
of activity over another"*; Princeton: *"We don't value one type of activity over another."*

There is no evidence, anywhere in the record, for or against any pursuit on our list. Anyone
claiming otherwise is inventing it. The "Tier 1–4 activity ranking" that circulates online and looks
exactly like the answer we wanted is **consultancy invention with no dataset behind it.**

### The distinctiveness counter-argument is not supported either

This document previously argued that a nationally-ranked Pokémon player is *more* distinctive than a
conventional applicant and therefore reads better. That argument is wrong, and it was rejected by
name by a sitting dean.

Stu Schmill, MIT Dean of Admissions, 2006, in a post subtitled *"You don't need to take up the
crumhorn to look 'different'"*:

> "We don't value one type of activity over another. … **You don't have to set yourself apart from
> your classmates (common advice you might hear from college counselors).** If you love to play the
> violin, play the violin — even if many of your classmates do as well. You don't need to take up
> the crumhorn to look 'different.'"

The base rates agree. From Card's expert report, Exhibit 4, over six years of applicant data:

| Rating combination | Applicants | Admit rate |
|---|---|---|
| Four ratings of 2 | 622 | **68%** |
| Extracurricular 1, no other 1s | 453 | **48%** |
| No ratings of 1 or 2 anywhere | 55,981 | **0.1%** |

Breadth at a high level beat the single extracurricular spike. That is the opposite of what the
distinctiveness theory predicts. Treat it as a raw correlation with severe selection problems, but
it is certainly not support.

**Also worth retiring: the word "spike."** No named admissions officer at any institution uses it.
It is consultant and forum vocabulary. The underlying concept *is* institutional under other names —
Harvard's own FAQ says **"well-lopsided"**, and Yale's internal orientation used **"pointy"** — but
the tiers and formulas built on top of it are marketing.

## 2. The mechanism nobody predicted: verification, not taste

Two sweeps found this independently, and it is the most decision-relevant thing in the research.

**Domain prestige is real. It does not live in the evaluative language. It lives in the machinery.**

| Channel | Exists for | Does not exist for |
|---|---|---|
| **A second rating axis.** Harvard's *academic* rating separately rewards "national or international level recognition in academic competitions" | Olympiads, academic competition | Everything else |
| **Faculty portfolio review.** MIT offers exactly four: Maker, Music & Theater Arts, Visual Arts & Architecture, Research. Harvard routes music/art/dance/academic via SlideRoom or a faculty read | Music, art, dance, research, making | Games, puzzles, word sport |
| **Advocacy inside the process.** A separate athletic rating, coach interest ratings, an ASTAT flag | Recruited athletics | Everything else |
| **A category to be filed under.** The Common App has 29 activity types | Music, dance, debate, robotics, athletics, science/math | Games, puzzles, tabletop — all land in "Other" |

The consequence is concrete. An IMO medalist converts **one** achievement into **two** high ratings
and a faculty endorsement. A World Cube Association world-record holder converts the same effort
into one rating, arriving as roughly 150 characters of self-report in a dropdown labelled "Other",
read by a generalist in a few minutes.

Yale's internal language makes the point exactly. Deresiewicz, who sat on the committee, records
that a "really pointy" applicant meant *"a musician whose audition tape had impressed the music
department, a scientist who had won a national award."* Both validated by a channel the institution
built.

**So the asymmetry is in verifiability, not in stated value.** Nobody says a card game counts less.
The institutions simply built confirmation pathways for some domains and not others.

---

## 3. What that means for this product

This is the part worth sitting with. **The missing channel is the thing we are building.**

A WCA ranking in a free-text "Other" field is weak because a reader cannot confirm it cheaply. The
same ranking arriving inside a tamper-evident record of the work, with a defended provenance trail
and a short human examination behind it, is structurally the *same kind of object* as an audition
tape that impressed the music department: an external party who looked, and stands behind it.

That is `passionBrainlift.md` SPOV 4 and 5 restated in admissions terms, and it argues for keeping
unconventional pursuits rather than cutting them — because they are precisely the pursuits with no
existing channel, and therefore the ones where our record adds the most.

It also sharpens the mastery-scaffold work (`2026-07-30-mastery-scaffold.md` §5): milestone
attestation is not only how the map knows where a child is. It is the artefact that makes an
unconventional climb legible to someone who has never heard of the venue.

## 4. The proposed rule

**Prune on verifiability, not on prestige, and not merely on whether a judge exists.**

A pursuit stays if a child can plausibly reach **regional-or-better external distinction** in it,
and that distinction arrives as something a stranger can check. The rubric's own boundary is the
test: "solid participation but without special distinction" is a 3, and applicants with no rating of
1 or 2 anywhere were admitted at 0.1%. A pursuit where the ceiling is participation is the thing
that fails, whatever it is called.

On the six at risk, the evidence is **not homogeneous**:

| Pursuit | Read |
|---|---|
| **Comics & Manga** | **Keep.** Maps onto an existing faculty-review channel at every school examined (MIT Visual Arts & Architecture; Harvard art faculty read). Cutting it would be the clearest error in the set. |
| **Speedcubing** | **Keep.** WCA rankings are public, externally administered and checkable in thirty seconds. Caltech's own supplement named speed-cubing as a characteristic interest. |
| **Scrabble** | **Keep.** NASPA/WESPA youth ratings are objective and external. |
| **Pokémon Cards** | **Keep.** Worlds has a Juniors division and awards prizes *as scholarships*, which produces an artefact that reads as an award. |
| **Demoscene** | **Keep.** Partially maps onto MIT's Maker Portfolio, and the UNESCO intangible-heritage listing is an unusually legible external marker. |
| **Backgammon** | **Flag.** Not for prestige — for thin youth infrastructure and a gambling association that is a genuine legibility problem. Verify USBGF youth pathways before deciding. |

### Named cases, held loosely

Real and checkable: Isaiah Bradner, 2019 Pokémon TCG North America International Champion (Senior
Division), now at Dartmouth. Mack Meller, youngest Scrabble expert in history at 11, Columbia.
Collin Burns, 3×3 world record at 15, Columbia. Matty Hiroto Inaba, 2022 North American Speedcubing
Champion, Columbia.

**These prove much less than they appear to.** Every one is also academically exceptional, and that
is not sampling failure — the traits that make a child world-class at anything by 17 are the traits
that produce strong grades. There is no denominator: we cannot see the equally-ranked competitors
who were rejected. And the counter-cases are real: Ray Rizzo, the only three-time Pokémon VGC world
champion in history, went to Drake; a top-of-class Michigan senior whose headline achievement was
the *Magic: The Gathering* pro tour was rejected by every Ivy he applied to.

## 4a. The decision actually taken

The recommendation in §4 was **not** adopted. The product owner, carrying a mandate from the
programme manager, directed that the catalogue be cut to pursuits that credibly help a child reach a
top-ranked university, on the grounds that the brainlift is revisable and the product's stated
purpose is admissions outcomes.

The bar as given: **"if it doesn't let you get into a top school, cut it."**

Operationalised for the audit, since the bar as stated is not directly testable, as three questions
per pursuit:

1. **Ceiling.** Can someone under 18 reach national-or-regional external distinction in it, at a
   named venue, with a result a stranger can check?
2. **Channel.** Does it route into a verification channel — Harvard's academic rating, a faculty
   portfolio, recruiting — or does it land in Common App "Other"?
3. **Precedent.** Is there a documented, named individual whose defining pre-college achievement was
   this pursuit and who was admitted to a top-ranked school?

All 44 pursuits are being audited against these, not only the six originally at risk, because
several conventional-sounding pursuits may fail the same test. Songwriting, Sudoku, Sewing and
Woodworking are the ones I expect to be in trouble, and none of them was on the original list.

**Recorded dissent, for whoever reads this later.** The evidence in §1–§3 does not support cutting on
domain, and the named precedents in §4 include admits to Dartmouth and Columbia in exactly the
pursuits proposed for removal. The decision is a judgement call about product positioning under a
manager's mandate, not a finding. It should be revisited if the positioning changes.

## 4b. The audit result, all 44

Four independent sweeps, each asked the same three questions: is there a named venue where someone
under 18 can reach national-or-regional distinction with a publicly checkable result; does it route
into a verification channel; is there a documented admit whose hook was this.

**22 KEEP, 15 MARGINAL, 7 CUT.** Marginal means a real ceiling exists but no named precedent was
found, sometimes with no channel either.

| Verdict | Pursuits |
|---|---|
| **CUT (7)** | Sudoku, Codes & Ciphers, Pokémon Cards, Backgammon, Demoscene, Speaker Design, Weather Watching |
| **MARGINAL (15)** | Speedcubing, Go, Bridge, Scrabble, Hacking Puzzles (CTF), Making Games, Ham Radio, Sewing, Guitar, Making Tracks, Songwriting, Comics & Manga, 3D Animation, Asteroid Hunting, Podcasting |
| **KEEP (22)** | Competition Maths, Chess, Programming, Model Rockets, Woodworking, Designing For Someone, Robotics, Growing Things, Piano, Violin, Drums, Singing, Photography, Drawing, Stop-Motion, Filmmaking, Writing Stories, Finding Wildlife, Birding, Watching Stars Change, Reporting, Debating |

### Why each cut is a cut

- **Speaker Design** and **Demoscene**: no under-18 venue exists *at all*. The AES Saul Walker
  competition names loudspeaker design specifically and requires enrolment in a college programme,
  so a high-schooler is disqualified from entering rather than merely unlikely to win. Assembly's
  only under-18 contest is a logo competition for children.
- **Backgammon**: the 2025 world under-18 event had **11 registrants and 2 recorded placings**.
- **Codes & Ciphers**: no world championship. The Alan Turing competition no longer runs as a ranked
  contest, and the NSA Codebreaker Challenge scores schools rather than individuals.
- **Weather Watching**: there is no ceiling. The two COOP honours require **20 and 25 years of
  service**, which is structurally impossible for a minor.
- **Sudoku** and **Pokémon Cards**: both have a technically real under-18 title that is structurally
  smaller than it sounds. Sudoku's U18 gold is a side-classification inside an adult event and the
  standalone junior championship drew five countries; Pokémon's divisions are age-graded children's
  brackets. Note this contradicts §4's earlier reading of Pokémon: the Bradner case is a
  *continental Senior* title, he was also his school's valedictorian, and no source connects the two.

### Three findings that outrank the table

**1. Every named precedent also had an independently elite academic record.** Burns graduated summa
cum laude and made Phi Beta Kappa in his junior year; Meller was homeschooled through Stanford
Online High School; Tang was a four-time AIME qualifier. Admissions decisions are not public, so a
named case can establish that a person did the thing and got in — never that the thing got them in.
That gap is closed for exactly one pursuit: MIT has stated it enrolled *almost every American IMO
medalist of the last decade*. For nothing else on this list is it closed at all.

**2. Harvard's academic rating does not require a competition, which reopens the science pursuits.**
The verbatim academic-1 criterion (HARV00097938) reads: *"unusual creativity and possible evidence
of original scholarship, often substantiated by our faculty or other academic mentors."* Competition
recognition is a separate, hedged second sentence. A peer-reviewed paper with a mentor satisfies the
operative clause directly. That splits citizen science along a hard line: contributing a **record**
(an eBird checklist, a CoCoRaHS reading, an IASC detection) is participation, while authoring a
**result** opens the academic axis. Three pursuits have a documented under-18 route across it —
Finding Wildlife (a 17- and an 18-year-old are *first authors* of the *ZooKeys* paper naming two
scorpion species), Birding, and Watching Stars Change (the AAVSO reports 26% of its 2024 journal
articles had student first or corresponding authors, half of them high schoolers). Asteroid Hunting
does not: it terminates at a database row, and numbering takes six to ten years, arriving after the
application.

**3. MIT's Maker Portfolio absorbs seven pursuits regardless of whether any competition exists.**
It explicitly welcomes "crafts that might not typically be lumped under engineering," is read by
faculty and alumni, and its launch examples include a chainmail suit and a knitted fractal. That
weakens a blanket cut. It does not produce a Harvard rating, because the Maker Portfolio is a
*channel*, not a distinction, and Harvard has no equivalent.

### The finding that undercuts the whole exercise

**Almost none of these ceilings is reachable by anyone in our age band.** Scholastic starts at grade
7 and age 13. YoungArts requires grades 10–12 or ages 15–18. Presidential Scholar in the Arts
requires being a graduating senior. Cliburn's junior division is 13–17; Menuhin's is under 16.

So for the overwhelming majority of a child's time in this product, **every KEEP above is a claim
about a pipeline that opens after they leave us.** The catalogue was built to the tests in
`docs/research/2026-07-28-discovery-catalogue.md` §1, which asked what a *child* can reach now. This
audit asks what a *seventeen-year-old* can reach. Cutting on the second test while serving the first
population is a category error, and it is the strongest argument against acting on this table
literally.

A related structural point, from the music sweep: the conservatoire is the **training** pipeline,
not an alternative destination. Woo went through Juilliard Pre-College and landed at Yale. The
honest claim is that serious training is acquired in a pre-college division and then *spent* at a
general admissions office through a portfolio — the violin alone opens nothing.

## 4c. What was actually applied

**Both tests stand, and neither replaces the other.** A pursuit needs a venue a child can reach now
(the original test, `docs/research/2026-07-28-discovery-catalogue.md` §1) **and** a documented path
to a distinction an admissions reader can check. Only entries failing both were removed, so the cut
is the seven and not the twenty-two non-keeps.

**The distinction is now in the data, not just in this document.** `Pursuit.ceiling` records the
later distinction — name, url, `opensAt`, and the named precedent where the audit found one — and it
is deliberately separate from `venue` because the two select differently. The numbers that fall out:

- **37** pursuits, **36** with a ceiling, **25** with a named precedent.
- **21 of 37** have a ceiling that opens *later* than the pursuit's own floor. That is the age gap,
  now machine-readable rather than a paragraph.
- **17 of 37** admit a six-year-old at all.

**An absent ceiling is meaningful and is tested as such.** Asteroid Hunting is the one entry with
none: a provisional designation is the venue's own output, and numbering — the terminal honour, and
the right to name what you found — takes six to ten years, arriving after the application. Of
roughly 3,800 to 7,000 provisional detections since 2006, about a hundred have ever been numbered.
Naming the venue twice would have manufactured a ceiling, so the field is left empty and the test
bounds how common that may become.

**Two things the audit got wrong, caught by writing the data down.** The CTF ceiling was picoCTF,
which is already that pursuit's venue, so it recorded nothing; it is now MITRE eCTF, where a
high-school team placed sixth nationally in 2024 ahead of MIT and UCLA. And a ceiling may legitimately
open *before* a pursuit's own floor: Birding sits at 13 because eBird bars under-13s, while the ABA
Young Birder award takes entrants from 10, so a ten-year-old can win the award and still not be
allowed the tool. Both numbers are right and there is a test saying so, because it looks like a bug.

### What the cut cost, recorded because it was not free

**Thirty-five curated resources lost their tile but not their place.** A resource keeps its
`domainPath` and loses only its `pursuits` tag, so Bletchley Park, NOAA, NRICH and UNSW Physics still
answer through the concierge; they simply no longer stock a wall tile. Nothing was deleted. This
works because `EMPTY_PURSUIT` means "a pursuit with no shelf", not "a resource with no tile".

**Cutting Sudoku orphaned three of the fifteen games.** Nonogram, Mirror Maze and Pipes all pointed
at that tile, and Nonogram is one of only five gadgets that can emit `chosen_challenge` — the single
signal that lets a child ask for something harder. They now point at Competition Maths, which is one
cabin-mate wider than the work: these are grid deduction, not olympiad problem-solving. The
alternative was three games reachable from no tile, which is worse for a child and worse for the
signal. **If the wall ever feels thin on logic puzzles, this is why, and restoring Sudoku is the
honest fix rather than stretching Competition Maths further.**

## 5. Two findings that cut against us, recorded because they are uncomfortable

**Chetty, Deming & Friedman (*QJE*, 2025)**, the only credible causal design in this literature,
using waitlist variation across Ivy-Plus colleges: roughly **30% of the admissions advantage held by
top-1% applicants is explained by stronger non-academic ratings**, and conditional on test scores,
private-school students have **no higher academic ratings but much higher non-academic ratings**.
They also find non-academic ratings are uncorrelated or negatively correlated with post-college
outcomes, while academic credentials strongly predict them.

That suggests non-academic ratings partly measure a school's ability to *package* a student. A
product that helps a child genuinely get good at something is not obviously pulling the same lever
wealthy applicants pull, and we should stop short of claiming it is.

**Nothing in this literature speaks to ages 6–14.** Every source observes 17-year-olds at the point
of application. How early engagement converts into later distinction is entirely unevidenced by
these sources, and that conversion is the link the whole product depends on. It is the largest
open claim we have.

## 6. What the evidence cannot tell us

- **Whether any specific pursuit helps.** The record is silent on all six. Silence cuts both ways.
- **Whether a niche ranking converts to a high rating in practice.** The rubric is domain-blind on
  paper; whether a reader with fifteen minutes credits a national Scrabble title the way they credit
  a concertmaster chair is untested. The rubric's three exemplars are all conventional school roles,
  which is weak circumstantial evidence about reader habit.
- **Which activities are crowded.** The number that would most inform this decision is not publicly
  measured anywhere.
- **Anything causal about activities.** No study randomises or instruments an extracurricular
  pursuit. The experiment that would settle it — matched files varying only domain at constant level,
  across several hundred real officers — will not be run, because the finding is reputationally
  catastrophic for the institution in either direction.
- **Whether Harvard generalises.** All the high-quality rating data is Harvard, classes of 2014–2019,
  pre-*SFFA*. Harvard has stated it revised its procedures after trial.
