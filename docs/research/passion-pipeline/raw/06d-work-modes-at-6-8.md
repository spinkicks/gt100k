# Research: A children's learning product measures interest per (domain x work-mode) cell, where the nine 'work-modes' are: build, investigate, compose, perform, debug, explain, persuade, collaborate, care. For children ages 6-8 specifically, what does the developmental and educational evidence say about (a) which of these nine modes of engagement are developmentally available and behaviorally distinguishable at this age -- e.g. can a 6-8 year old meaningfully 'debug' (systematic fault isolation), 'persuade' (audience-aware argument), 'compose' (generative original creation), or 'explain' (metacognitive articulation to another), and at what age does each become observable; (b) whether a child's preference for a MODE of working (making things vs investigating vs performing vs explaining) is a real, stable, measurable individual difference at ages 6-8, or whether it is too unstable and task-dependent to be measured -- cite evidence on the stability and cross-situational consistency of activity/play preferences and learning-approach preferences in early childhood, and on regression to the mean; (c) how such mode preferences have actually been operationalized and observed in young children -- e.g. free-play observation schemes, preschool/primary activity-choice research, Montessori/Reggio work-choice records, learning-center selection studies, and what reliability was achieved; and (d) the critical warning: does the 'learning styles' literature -- widely debunked for adults -- apply here, i.e. is a 'work-mode preference' just a repackaged learning style, and what distinguishes a defensible behavioral preference construct from a debunked one? Be adversarial about (d).

_2026-07-25_

## Summary
For ages 6-8 the evidence base does not support a nine-cell (domain x work-mode) interest measure as a trait-profiling instrument, but it does support a narrower, defensible version: mode preference as a repeatedly re-measured behavioral state estimate. On (a), there is almost no direct developmental evidence on the availability of these specific nine modes; the one surviving finding is negative — 7-10 year olds given only probe questions showed no improvement in unconfounded experimental design or valid inference, so "investigate"/"debug"-style systematic fault isolation is not spontaneously elicited at this age and requires explicit instruction (all stronger age-floor claims about CVS, computational thinking, and metacognitive articulation failed verification). On (b), individual activity-preference hierarchies are real and idiosyncratic enough to be detected above group averages, yet the stability ceiling is low and largely environmental: childhood rank-order consistency of trait-like individual differences is ~.31 over ~6.7 years (~10% shared variance), repeated preference assessments were stable for only 15 of 38 participants (40%) across pooled studies, 40% of single-sitting MSWO assessments shifted rank order within one session, apparent stability is partly an artifact of elicitation format (paired forced-choice 67-70% vs single-item 0-27%), and embedded reinforcement inverted preschoolers' revealed orderings — the three least-preferred zones rose nearly sixfold, the instructional zone from 16% to 72% of intervals. On (c), the one clean operationalization is momentary time sampling of zone occupancy plus engagement across nine concurrently available free-play activities (Hanley et al. 2007): in-zone interobserver agreement 94-98% at a 90 s per-child interval, collapsing to 71.7% at 30 s, with individual allocations spanning 0-50% — but the sample was 3.5-5.5 years, and generalizability-theory work on 6-11 year olds shows the number of occasions needed for dependability varies by behavioral dimension, so one session is not a dependable score. On (d), a work-mode preference is not automatically a repackaged learning style — Pashler et al. debunked the meshing inference (preference implies matched instruction improves learning), not the existence of preferences — but the product crosses into the debunked territory the moment it routes instruction from the measured mode, and it would then owe the crossover (disordinal) interaction design that virtually no learning-styles study has ever produced; the psychometric base rate is brutal (only 1 of 13 flagship style instruments met all four minimum standards; ~46% met one or none), and test-retest in the .3-.6 band makes per-cell labels noise-dominated.

## Findings

### 1. On (a), there is essentially no verified developmental evidence establishing at what age each of the nine named work-modes becomes observable; the only surviving age-specific finding is negative — probe questions alone, without direct instruction, produced no improvement in 7-10 year olds' ability to design unconfounded experiments or make valid inferences, so merely prompting a child to investigate will not elicit systematic fault isolation at 7-8.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00081>

Chen & Klahr (1999), Child Development 70(5):1098-1120, N=87 aged 7-10: "Providing probes without direct instruction, however, did not improve children's ability to design unconfounded experiments and make valid inferences," whereas explicit within-domain training plus probes produced learning and transfer. Two limits carried forward: the paper measures the control-of-variables strategy, not "fault isolation" under that name (a construct extension the source does not label), and only the abstract was retrievable, so no 7-8-specific breakout of the probe-only condition was confirmed. Critically, every stronger companion claim in this area failed verification — that CVS research "starts at age 7" with no data below (0-3), that systematic investigation is trainable-but-unavailable at this age (1-2), that transfer rises linearly across 7-10 (0-3), and the whole computational-thinking / debugging-measurable-in-grades-1-2 line (0-3, 1-2, 0-3, 1-2). Net: the nine-mode taxonomy's developmental availability is, on this evidence set, unestablished rather than established; treat build/investigate/compose/perform/debug/explain/persuade/collaborate as an untested partition at 6-8.

### 2. Mode/activity preference CAN be operationalized purely behaviorally, with no self-report or style questionnaire, as time allocation among concurrently available activity zones during free play — momentary time sampling of proximity (within 1 m) plus engagement (touching or orienting to materials) — and this is an existence proof for measuring mode-of-working as observed choice.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2078569/>

Hanley, Cammilleri, Tiger & Ingvarsson (2007), J Appl Behav Anal 40(4):603-618 (PMC2078569). Nine zones simultaneously available plus an out-of-zone category in a 12x7 m preschool classroom, N=20 (10 boys, 10 girls, 4 with developmental disabilities). Preference = intervals in each zone / total intervals, zones then rank-ordered. Engagement co-occurred with in-zone at 97% of intervals, so in-zone alone was analyzed. Two scope limits that block direct transfer: participants were 3.5-5.5 years (below the 6-8 target band), and the partition is physical activity zones (blocks, books, dramatic play), not abstract work-modes — so this validates the METHOD (choice-as-time-allocation, observer-scored) and not the claim that work-mode is the right partition. The paper reports no test-retest stability of preference rankings.

### 3. That behavioral measure achieves high interobserver agreement — 94-98% for in-zone at a 90 s per-child sampling interval, 98-99% for engagement and in-zone with a single child at 5 s — but collapses to 71.7% at a 30 s interval, so reliability is bounded by observer load rather than by construct fuzziness (an inference, not an authorial claim).
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2078569/>, <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ832374.pdf>

Verbatim: Study 2 (20 children scored simultaneously) "(Ms = 97.7%, 95.7%, and 97.3%, respectively). Agreement was substantially lower for the 30-s intervals (M = 71.7%)" for 60/90/120 s; the 30-s system "was unreliable when used to simultaneously measure the time allocation and engagement of multiple children." Study 4: agreement on 44% of sessions, M=94% (range 85-98%) for in zone. Study 1 (single child, 5 s MTS): 99% in-zone, 98% engagement, second observer on 30% of sessions. Two fidelity corrections for downstream wording: the "~35% of sessions" figure is not in the paper (actuals 30%, 100%, 44%; 35.3% is from the 2009 companion study), and the 94-98% band is for in-zone only — engagement agreement is reported only in Study 1. Recommended phrasing: "in-zone agreement 94-98%; second-observer coverage 30-44% of sessions."

### 4. Individual preference hierarchies over activity types are discernible and idiosyncratic in preschoolers — allocation ranged 0-50% across zones and one child was regularly observed in only two of nine activities — so within-child rank orderings are detectable above group averages, supporting activity/mode preference as a real (measurable) individual difference rather than pure noise.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2078569/>

Same primary source (Hanley et al. 2007). Verbatim: the system "was sensitive to differences in response allocation" and "preference hierarchies are discernible for individual children"; "Although preferences were fairly idiosyncratic across children, some patterns in the data are worth noting"; Tina "was regularly observed in only two of the nine activities." Group-vs-individual divergence is explicit (dramatic play most preferred for 7 of 9 girls but 2 of 9 boys; 12 of 18 never entered the instructional zone). Internal quality checks: 25 daily 18-min sessions, 12 observations per child per session, n=18 analyzed; capacity confound checked (rank-order r=.98 true vs equalized capacity; capacity-preference r=.33); short-run convergence (first five observations resembled all 25). Boundary: this establishes measurement sensitivity, interobserver reliability, and within-3-week convergence — NOT test-retest stability over weeks/months or cross-situational consistency. Ages 3.5-5.5 in one university lab preschool. This finding is in direct tension with the manipulability finding below.

### 5. The same free-play zone-choice measure is strongly manipulable by materials, adult presence, and embedded reinforcers rather than reflecting fixed traits: embedding reinforcement raised time in the three originally least-preferred zones nearly sixfold (16 of 20 children shifted; instructional zone 16% to 72% of intervals), so a child's revealed preference ordering can be inverted by redesigning affordances and payoffs.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ832374.pdf>

Hanley, Tiger, Ingvarsson & Cammilleri (2009), J Appl Behav Anal 42(1):33-41. Verbatim: "Sixteen of the 20 children were observed in a target zone more often during the embedded reinforcement phase relative to the preceding baseline phase. Overall, there was a just under a twofold increase and just under a sixfold increase in time allocation to the target zones during the satiation and embedded reinforcement conditions, respectively." Instructional zone baseline M=16% rising to a sustained M=72%, exceeding baseline blocks (48%). Reversal design; second observer on 35.3% of sessions; IOA 97% in-zone, 98% engagement; return to near-zero on withdrawal supports causal attribution. Three limits: ages 3.5-5.5, one classroom, N=20; "inversion" is fully borne out only for the instructional zone (16->72%) and partly for library (7->41%), while science (11->32%) overlapped baseline and collapsed to M=0.6% at follow-up; and the three-part attribution is a package effect — materials, adult presence, and reinforcers were not decomposed. The follow-up collapse (science 32% -> 0.6% once novel activities stopped, while physically permanent enhancements held) is the sharpest product-relevant warning: cross-mode differences in activity production quality are a first-order confound for any per-mode interest score, though that specific claim only reached a 1-2 vote.

### 6. Long-run stability of any trait-like preference measured at 6-8 has a low ceiling: meta-analytic rank-order consistency of trait-like individual differences in childhood is only ~.31 over a 6.7-year interval (~10% shared variance).
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10668348/>

Roberts & DelVecchio (2000), Psychological Bulletin 126(1):3-25, indexed as Meta-Analysis: 152 longitudinal studies, 3,217 test-retest coefficients, interval held constant at 6.7 years; consistency "increased from .31 in childhood to .54 during the college years, to .64 at age 30," plateauing near .74 at 50-70. The ~10% figure is arithmetic (.31^2). Two caveats must travel with the number: the constructs are personality/temperament traits, not activity or work-mode preferences, and "childhood" is a broad aggregated band, not 6-8. The related claims that consistency rises monotonically to a 50-70 plateau (making early childhood the least stable point in the life course) and that temperament-like behavioral-style constructs sit in an even lower-stability class — making .31 optimistic rather than conservative for work-mode preference — each drew only a 1-2 vote and should be presented as argument, not finding.

### 7. In the one literature that has actually measured preference stability empirically, individual preference rankings clear a trait-like bar only about 40% of the time: repeated paired-stimulus assessments over 2-6 months yielded stable hierarchies for 7 of 10 participants, and pooling with three prior longitudinal studies, only 15 of 38 (40%) met the stability criterion.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16813040/>

Hanley, Iwata & Roscoe (2006), J Appl Behav Anal 39(2):189-202 (PMC1479785), open access and verified in full. "Preferences were shown to be stable for 7 of the 10 participants" (Abe and Bo highly unstable; Dana slightly below criterion); "results of the four studies on preference stability show that preferences were stable across assessments for 15 of 38 (40%) individuals." Method: paired-stimulus format (Fisher et al. 1992), nine items, 36 trials per assessment, subsequent assessments across a 2-6 month period. Population is 10 adults aged 26-62 with developmental delay, so this transfers only as a methodological analogy, not as a developmental finding. Note the source's own internal wrinkle, disclosed rather than concealed: the abstract says "relatively stable for the majority (80%) of participants" (8/10 counting borderline Dana) while the body says 7/10.

### 8. Apparent stability is partly an artifact of elicitation format, not a property of the person: paired-item forced-choice formats produced 67% and 70% stability versus 0% and 27% for single-item formats — though format is plausibly confounded with participant disability level, and this is a post-hoc cross-study observation, not a designed manipulation.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16813040/>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bin.1797>

Hanley, Iwata & Roscoe (2006) Discussion, verbatim: paired-item studies "resulted in more stable preference patterns (67% and 70%, respectively) than the studies incorporating single-item preference assessment formats (0% and 27%...)" followed immediately by "This may be a direct function of the assessment format, or it is equally plausible that this difference is attributable to another variable associated with the selection of a particular format (e.g., level of participant disability)." Mapping: Mason 1989 = 0%, Zhou 2001 = 27%, Carr 2000 = 67%, present study = 70%. Independently corroborated by MacNaul, Cividini-Motta, Wilson & Di Paola (2021), Behavioral Interventions 36(4):962-983, a systematic review of 20 single-case studies whose abstract states preference "is most stable at brief IAIs (8-30 days) and when using the paired-stimulus format." Two wording corrections: drop "free-operant" (the source says only "single-item"; free-operant is a distinct third format), and note the review is a secondary synthesis in which format was not randomized, so "stability is a property of format" overstates a descriptive moderator result. Design implication: if a work-mode instrument is built, forced-choice pairings over concurrently available options will look more stable than free-operant time-allocation — some of that gain is measurement artifact. The related interval-dependence claim (most stable at 8-30 days) drew only 1-2.

### 9. Preference rank order is unstable even within a single sitting: only 24 of 40 MSWO assessments across 17 autistic children (60%) showed a stable pattern across consecutive rounds, with the authors describing preferences as only slightly more likely to be classified as stable than not — so a single-session rank order is not a trait-like measurement, and within-session instability was an unmeasured error source in all prior published stability estimates.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37166411/>

Melanson et al. (2023), J Appl Behav Anal 56(3):638-655, doi:10.1002/jaba.988: 17 children, 40 MSWO assessments, Spearman rank-order correlations across consecutive rounds (a round = each reset of the stimulus array); "Patterns of preference stability were observed in 24 out of the 40 assessments (60%)." The novelty framing is the authors' own: "No studies have evaluated shifts in preference across consecutive rounds of a single preference assessment" — prior work examined stability over time and across whole-assessment administrations only. Limits: explicitly a secondary data analysis, so N=17/40 is opportunistic and the 60% carries wide uncertainty; population is autistic children (ages not in the PubMed record) and the objects are edible/leisure stimuli, not activity or work modes; the "before any time interval elapsed" gloss is imprecise since consecutive rounds do take elapsed time; and the inference that prior published estimates were contaminated is the claimant's extension, not source text.

### 10. As of 2021 the temporal stability of behavioral preference remained an open empirical question in applied behavior analysis despite decades of preference-assessment methodology — a reliable measurement procedure does not establish that the underlying preference is a stable trait.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33469909/>

Butler & Graff (2021), J Appl Behav Anal 54(2):684-699, doi:10.1002/jaba.807, a 12-month longitudinal single-case design (monthly paired-stimulus assessments, bimonthly reinforcer assessments, n=4 with developmental disabilities). Authors' own framing: "Behavior analysts have developed an extensive technology of assessing preferences, but little research has evaluated the extent to which preferences change over time." The paper's own numbers instantiate the reliability-vs-stability distinction: month-to-month Spearman stability averaged 0.79 (edible), 0.66 (leisure), 0.50 (social attention), while initial-vs-12-month stability fell to 0.63 (edible), 0.33 (social), 0.19 (leisure). Note that BOTH single-interval framings of these coefficients were voted down as standalone claims (the "leisure is essentially unstable at 0.19" framing 0-3, the "moderately stable month-to-month" framing 1-2) precisely because they conflict unless the interval is stated; the reconciliation is that 0.66 is the month-to-month mean and 0.19 the 12-month figure for the same class. Do not read these coefficients as estimates for a children's mode-preference instrument: n=4, adults with developmental disabilities, consumable/leisure/attention stimuli.

### 11. The operational recommendation from that literature is frequent routine-embedded reassessment rather than one-off profiling, plus reinforcer variation or learner choice among several preferred items — i.e. a preference measure is defensible as a rolling state estimate feeding immediate decisions, not as a stored trait label.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16813040/>

Hanley, Iwata & Roscoe (2006), Discussion, verbatim and contiguous: "Regardless of assessment format (single item, paired item, group array), preferences should be evaluated frequently, if possible, so as to reflect changes as a function of exposure. To that end, brief assessment formats... or assessments embedded into daily routines... may be most desirable. As an alternative, providing varied types of reinforcers... or allowing a participant to select from a set of preferred items during the training session... may also prevent performance decrements as a function of repeated use." Every element of the recommendation is traceable to named sentences. Two honesty notes: the sample is 10 adults with developmental disabilities over 3-6 months, and the "not a stored trait label" gloss runs slightly against this paper's own headline (80% relatively stable) — the recommendation is fully sourced, the anti-trait framing is inference. This is the single most actionable design conclusion in the whole evidence set for a 6-8 product: measure continuously, decide locally, do not persist a profile.

### 12. Any single observation session is insufficient for a dependable behavioral score in elementary-aged children: generalizability-theory analysis of systematic classroom observation found the number of occasions needed for acceptable dependability differs substantially by the behavioral dimension being scored.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ856396>

Volpe, McConaughy & Hintze (2009), School Psychology Review 38(3):382-401 (ERIC EJ856396), peer reviewed, Reports-Research. Direct Observation Form; each student observed 8 times (a.m. and p.m. across four days); analyses "revealed important differences in observed variance and the number of observations required to obtain acceptable dependability" across dimensions. The claim asserts no specific occasion count and no 6-8-specific figure, so it stays inside the source; note that the stronger version — that eight occasions across morning/afternoon on four days is THE design standard — was refuted 0-3, so do not import that number as a requirement.

### 13. That dependability evidence rests on a small clinically referred sample (24 children aged 6-11 across 18 schools, referred for learning and behavior problems) measuring problem behavior and on-task behavior, so its occasion-count estimates cannot be assumed to transfer to typically developing 6-8 year olds or to preference/choice measures.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ856396>

Confirmed on two independent retrievals of the ERIC record: N=24; ages 6-11; 18 different elementary schools; all referred over concerns about learning and behavioral problems; eight occasions (a.m./p.m. across four days); instrument = Direct Observation Form (McConaughy & Achenbach 2009); descriptors include Generalizability Theory, Behavior Problems, Classroom Observation Techniques. Both extrapolation targets sit outside the design: there is no typically developing comparison group and no 6-8-restricted subsample, and the measured constructs are problem/on-task behavior, not activity choice or work-mode. Facts were read from the ERIC bibliographic record, not the full PDF.

### 14. The structure of children's interests is age-dependent rather than invariant — circular (Holland RIASEC) model fit is positively related to age, and elementary and middle-school participants organize interests and competence judgments along dimensions qualitatively different from college students' — so an adult-derived taxonomy of interest 'types' plus one fixed measurement model applied across ages is not psychometrically warranted.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0167.45.3.290>

Tracey & Ward (1998), Journal of Counseling Psychology 45(3):290-303; first author developed the randomization test of hypothesized order relations and authored the definitive RIASEC structural meta-analysis. Abstract findings, verbatim via OpenAlex: "(b) the structure of interests and competence perceptions varied across age, (c) the fit of the circular model was positively related to age, (d) elementary and middle school students evaluated their interests and competencies using different dimensions than did college students." Three qualifications: "less differentiated" is an interpretive gloss (the reported statistic is poorer circular fit plus different underlying dimensions; differentiation is a distinct Holland construct); the "not psychometrically warranted" conclusion is inference, since the methods were randomization tests plus PCA, not multi-group measurement-invariance CFA; and the dimension-difference result is strongest for middle-schoolers, with elementary covered by the weaker fit-improves-with-age finding. The claim that children's structure is therefore "not merely a noisier version of the adult structure" rests on a single closed-access study and should be corroborated before being stated flatly. Related refuted claim (0-3): that interest and competence are structurally inseparable in children — do not use it.

### 15. That interest-structure study cannot be cited as support for measurable stable interest or mode structure at ages 6-8: its youngest group was 'elementary school' students with no age-6-8 breakout, and the wider RIASEC literature it builds on had supported circular structure only from age 14 up.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0167.45.3.290>

Abstract, verbatim: "Two studies were conducted with samples of elementary school, middle school, and college students, who were given the Inventory of Children's Activities... The structure was examined at the scale and item levels using the randomization test of hypothesized order relations and principal-components analysis." Results are reported only at elementary/middle/college granularity, and the paper's own framing is that age groups younger than 14 had not been examined (Tracey & Rounds 1993 supported circular structure from 14 up). Since fit degrades with younger age, extrapolating downward to 6-8 argues against, not for, measurable stable structure there. Residual weakness: the "no 6-8 breakout" element is an absence-of-subgroup claim supportable only indirectly from the abstract, as the article is closed access and the elementary grade range could not be read. US "elementary" nominally spans ~5-11, so the claim correctly stops at "no breakout" rather than asserting no 6-8 year olds were sampled.

### 16. On (d), a work-mode preference is NOT automatically a repackaged learning style: what Pashler et al. debunked is the meshing hypothesis — the inference from a measured preference to the prescription that matched instruction improves learning — not the existence of preferences or of aptitude differences. A purely descriptive behavioral engagement measure is a different claim.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x>, <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/>

Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008/2009), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 9(3):105-119, ~1,400 Crossref citations, a commissioned APS evidence review — the strongest possible source for a statement about the scope of its own conclusion. Verbatim: "The term 'learning styles' refers to the concept that individuals differ in regard to what mode of instruction or study is most effective for them... The most common-but not the only-hypothesis about the instructional relevance of learning styles is the meshing hypothesis, according to which instruction is best provided in a format that matches the preferences of the learner." The debunked proposition is an efficacy claim, with learner preference as its input. IMPORTANT SCOPE STRIP: the sub-clause "children and adults reliably express format preferences" exceeds this source — that is a psychometric/developmental claim, Pashler et al. sampled students/adults with no early-childhood data, and they raise concerns about the psychometric quality of self-report style instruments, so citing them for "reliably" is unsupported and arguably in tension with the text. Full text could not be re-read (SAGE 403; all mirrors 404), so quote fidelity rests on the supplied passages. This finding is in unresolved tension with the (1-2) counter-claim that any product administering a preference assessment and routing instruction from it inherits the paper's resource-allocation verdict against deploying learning-styles assessments in general educational practice, regardless of the instrument's reliability.

### 17. If the product ever routes instruction, content, or a recommendation from the measured mode, it has asserted the meshing hypothesis and owes a specific crossover (disordinal) interaction design: learners grouped by measured mode, randomly assigned to instructional methods, common outcome test, with the best method differing by group. Preference-existence data, reliability data, and stability data cannot substitute.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x>, <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/>

Pashler et al., verbatim: "First, students must be divided into groups on the basis of their learning styles, and then students from each group must be randomly assigned to receive one of multiple instructional methods. Next, students must then sit for a final test that is the same for all students. Finally... the experiment must reveal a specific type of interaction between learning style and instructional method: Students with one learning style achieve the best educational outcome when given an instructional method that differs from the instructional method producing the best outcome for students with a different learning style." "Crossover (disordinal)" is an accurate technical label for that prose pattern, not in the original wording; "preference or stability data cannot substitute" is a one-step entailment of the stipulated design. This is the correct falsification bar to import for part (d). Source identity confirmed via Crossref, Semantic Scholar and EuropePMC; full text not re-readable (403/paywalled). Note the review predominantly concerns adolescent and adult learners in general education — it sets the bar by analogy, not with 6-8 data.

### 18. That bar has essentially never been cleared, and the failure is disconfirming rather than merely absent: the review found virtually no studies producing the required interaction, and several methodologically adequate studies produced results that flatly contradict the meshing hypothesis.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x>, <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/>

Verbatim abstract: "we found virtually no evidence for the interaction pattern mentioned above, which was judged to be a precondition for validating the educational applications of learning styles. Although the literature on learning styles is enormous, very few studies have even used an experimental methodology capable of testing the validity of learning styles applied to education. Moreover, of those that did use an appropriate method, several found results that flatly contradict the popular meshing hypothesis. We conclude therefore, that at present, there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice." The claim adds no quantitative precision or generalization; "flatly contradict" carries the disconfirming-vs-absent distinction. Scope: general education, mostly adolescent/adult samples — supports (d), not any 6-8-specific inference.

### 19. The psychometric base rate for style/preference instruments is dismal and sets the bar a new work-mode instrument must clear: of 71 learning-styles models identified, 13 of the most influential were evaluated in detail against four minimum standards (internal consistency, test-retest reliability, construct validity, predictive validity, all evidenced by independent researchers) — only Allinson & Hayes met all four, six met one or none (~46%), and six 'should not be used as the theoretical justification for changing practice'.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://www.leerbeleving.nl/wp-content/uploads/2011/09/learning-styles.pdf>

Coffield, Moseley, Hall & Ecclestone (2004), "Learning styles and pedagogy in post-16 learning: A systematic and critical review," Learning and Skills Research Centre, ISBN 1 85338 918 8 — verified by downloading the full 182-page PDF (1,301,188 bytes), front matter and Table 44 intact; leerbeleving.nl is only a mirror, so cite authors + ISBN rather than the URL. Verbatim p.139: "Only three of the 13 models - those of Allinson and Hayes, Apter and Vermunt - could be said to have come close"; "only Allinson and Hayes met all four of the minimal criteria and... Riding and Sternberg failed to meet any of them"; "The remaining six models, despite in some cases having been revised and refined over 30 years, failed to meet the criteria and so, in our opinion, should not be used as the theoretical justification for changing practice." Table 44 grouping: none = Jackson, Riding, Sternberg; one = Dunn and Dunn, Gregorc, Honey and Mumford, Kolb; two = Entwistle, Herrmann, Myers-Briggs; three = Apter, Vermunt; four = Allinson and Hayes. Three precision corrections: 71 were identified in the literature but only 13 psychometrically evaluated; Jackson's zero reflects absence of independent evaluation rather than failure (so 2 demonstrably met none, 1 unevaluated); and this is a post-16 FE/HE/adult review, so it sets the bar by analogy for a 6-8 instrument.

### 20. A test-retest coefficient in the .3-.6 band is not good enough to label an individual: 0.60 — the Dunn & Dunn adequacy threshold — is lax because at that level misclassification is more likely than accuracy, and independent retest data put 13 of 20 PEPS variables below .51 with even supposedly stable physiological traits at .31-.60. Assigning children to (domain x work-mode) cells at that reliability yields noise-dominated labels.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://www.leerbeleving.nl/wp-content/uploads/2011/09/learning-styles.pdf>

Coffield et al. (2004), Section 3, both sentences verbatim from the extracted PDF: "It is important to note that the 0.60 criterion for acceptable reliability is a lax one, since at that level, misclassification is actually more likely than accuracy" and "Overall, 13 out of 20 variables exhibited poor test-retest reliability scores of below 0.51." ## Caveats
EVIDENCE-BASE MISMATCH IS THE HEADLINE CAVEAT. Almost nothing in this set is about typically developing 6-8 year olds choosing work-modes. The behavioral-measurement backbone (Hanley et al. 2007, 2009) is preschoolers aged 3.5-5.5 in a single university lab classroom, choosing among physical activity zones. The entire stability literature (Hanley/Iwata/Roscoe 2006; Butler & Graff 2021; Melanson et al. 2023; MacNaul et al. 2021) is applied-behavior-analysis preference assessment in people with developmental disabilities or autism, mostly adults, with edible/leisure/attention stimuli — it transfers as methodological analogy only. The learning-styles critiques (Pashler et al. 2008; Coffield et al. 2004) are adolescent/adult and post-16. The interest-structure work (Tracey & Ward 1998) starts at "elementary" (~9+) with no 6-8 breakout. The observation-dependability work (Volpe et al. 2009) is 24 clinically referred children aged 6-11 measuring on-task and problem behavior, not choice. No source in this set measured any of the nine named work-modes.

PART (a) IS EFFECTIVELY UNANSWERED. Every affirmative developmental-availability claim failed verification: CVS age floors (0-3), spontaneous vs trained investigation at 7-10 (1-2), linear transfer growth 7-10 (0-3), computational-thinking measurability in grades 1-2 (0-3), the kindergarten redesign and floor-effect findings (1-2, 0-3), and item-difficulty rank consistency across ages 5-8 (1-2). Nothing verified addresses "compose," "perform," "explain," "persuade," "collaborate," or "care" at any age. Do not let the single surviving negative finding about probes-without-instruction be read as a positive age map.

UNRESOLVED CONFLICTS (all real, none adjudicated by the evidence):
1. Real individual difference vs environmental artifact — the SAME paradigm, same children, same measure yields both "preference hierarchies are discernible for individual children" (Hanley 2007) and a near-sixfold inversion of those hierarchies under embedded reinforcement (Hanley 2009). Both are true; they are not contradictory if what the measure captures is a joint function of child and affordance. That is the most defensible reading, and it is fatal to trait interpretation.
2. Detectable-above-noise vs ~.31 ceiling — within-child rank orderings are measurable in a session while childhood rank-order consistency over years is ~10% shared variance. Reconciliation: short-horizon state, not long-horizon trait.
3. Format artifact vs genuine instability — paired forced-choice reads 67-70% stable vs 0-27% for single-item, yet a paired-stimulus format still produced 0.19 twelve-month rank retention for leisure items. The artifact story cannot absorb that, so format choice cannot rescue long-horizon stability.
4. Butler & Graff coefficients were reported in two directly opposing framings (0.19 vs 0.66 for leisure; the stimulus-class ordering flips), and both standalone framings were voted down. They reconcile only under explicit interval labeling: ~0.66 month-to-month mean, ~0.19 initial-vs-12-month. Always state the interval.
5. Part (d) core disagreement — whether measuring a preference is innocent (2-1 for) or whether administering a preference assessment that routes instruction inherits the learning-styles verdict regardless of reliability (1-2, refuted but not dismissed). Practically: the measure is defensible; the routing is what is on trial.

SOURCE-ACCESS AND FIDELITY LIMITS: Pashler et al. full text is unreadable (SAGE 403, all mirrors 404) so its quotes rest on supplied passages; Tracey & Ward and Roberts & DelVecchio were verified at abstract/metadata level only; MacNaul et al. full text is Wiley-403 so the explicit paired-vs-free-operant-vs-single ranking is unverified; Coffield et al. was read in full but its pivotal "misclassification more likely than accuracy" sentence is an undeated, uncited aside with no specified classification scheme. Several numeric details in the incoming claims were wrong and are corrected in the findings: "second observer on ~35% of sessions" (actuals 30/100/44%), the 94-98% band applying to engagement as well as in-zone, "systematic review of 71 models" (71 identified, 13 evaluated), "three met none" (2 met none, 1 unevaluated), and "single-item free-operant" (source says single-item only).

Each is defensible argument and should be labeled as such in any product-facing document.

TIME-SENSITIVITY: low. The learning-styles critique (2004/2008) and the stability meta-analysis (2000) are settled canon; the newest inputs are 2021 and 2023 and both point the same direction (stability is an open question, instability appears even within a session). No evidence here is at risk of imminent reversal — but equally, no one has run the study this product actually needs.

## Open questions
- Does a nine-mode partition (build/investigate/compose/perform/debug/explain/persuade/collaborate/care) have any empirical basis as a set of behaviorally distinguishable categories at 6-8, or is it an adult-derived taxonomy? Nothing in this evidence set tested it. The minimum first study is a factor/cluster analysis of observed choice behavior across a mode-balanced activity set — if observed allocations collapse to two or three dimensions (e.g. active-vs-receptive, social-vs-solitary), the nine-cell grid is over-parameterized and the per-cell scores are unusable regardless of reliability.
