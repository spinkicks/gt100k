# Research: When a young child's (ages 6-8) exploration of subject domains is wrapped inside a game — characters, story, points, puzzles, unlockables — how can you tell whether their engagement reflects genuine interest in the underlying DOMAIN versus interest in the GAME WRAPPER itself? What does the research say about: (a) which behavioral-trace signals (time-on-task, voluntary return after pressure is removed, free-choice re-engagement, depth of exploration, self-initiated extension, information-seeking) are least contaminated by the game shell and most predictive of durable domain interest in young children; (b) how to design a game shell that is medium-neutral / equally appealing across domains so that cross-domain comparisons of a child's engagement are valid rather than reflecting which mini-game was more fun; (c) the over-justification and reward-undermining literature specifically for ages 5-9 — do points, badges, characters and narrative rewards crowd out intrinsic interest in the wrapped content, and under what conditions; (d) how practitioners and researchers in stealth assessment / game-based assessment / educational data mining have measured and validated construct validity, discriminant validity and predictive validity of in-game engagement measures with young children, including known failure modes and how wrapper-driven engagement was detected; and (e) whether 'free-choice' or pressure-off probes can be run reliably with 6-8 year olds. Cite concrete study designs and effect sizes where available.

_2026-07-25_

## Summary
There is no validated instrument that separates domain interest from game-wrapper interest in 6-8 year olds, but the literature converges on a workable strategy: hold the wrapper constant and vary only how the domain content binds to the core mechanic, then measure penalty-free re-engagement after all pressure and reward are withdrawn. The single closest experimental analogue (Habgood & Ainsworth's Zombie Division) shows this works — identical graphics, story and progression, with only the math-to-mechanic binding varied, produced a ~7x voluntary time-on-task difference (75.7 vs 10.28 min, t=7.38, p<.001, r=.89) — but that free-choice arm ran at ~10 years; in its actual 7-8 year old sample only the DELAYED post-test discriminated conditions (F(2,48)=7.49, p<.001, partial eta-sq=.24), while immediate engagement and immediate post-test did not. Raw time-on-task is the weakest of the candidate signals: its meaning reverses with the child's learning performance (better learners stay when learning progress is high, beta=-.51; poorer learners stay with the most FAMILIAR option, beta=.96), it rises with epistemic uncertainty alone with no reward present, it partly indexes executive function rather than interest, and in 4-year-olds aggregate session length and switch frequency were flat against actual learning (p=.24, p=.80) while trial-wise choice policy predicted it. On rewards, the child-specific evidence is unfavourable to points-for-play shells: tangible rewards undermine children's free-choice motivation more than adults' (d=-0.39 vs -0.27, Qb=4.18, p<.04), engagement-contingent rewards — the direct analogue of points-for-time — show d=-0.46 in children, the effect grows rather than decays over 1-2 weeks (d=-0.55), and praise does NOT enhance children's free-choice motivation (d=0.11, ns) even though it does for adults (d=0.43). Cross-domain comparison via different mini-games is the least defensible part of the design: methodologists explicitly warn against operationalising different constructs as different games, feature-matching has a documented failure mode (Anderson & Dill matched seven dimensions and were confounded by an eighth), and generic video-game affinity independently predicts voluntary return (beta=0.267, p=.003) unless you threshold on play intensity rather than any-play.

## Findings

### 1. The strongest available design for separating domain from wrapper is to hold the shell literally constant and vary only how the domain content binds to the core mechanic, then offer a cost-free switch: with identical 3D dungeon, characters, story and progression (numbers on skeletons swapped for equivalent symbol combinations to preserve challenge; identical math moved to an end-of-level multiple-choice quiz), children given a penalty-free, position-randomised menu spent 75.7 min (SD 35.5) on the intrinsically integrated version vs 10.28 min on the extrinsic version (paired t=7.38, df=15, p<.001, r=.89).
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/1/Habgood_Ainsworth_final.pdf>

Habgood & Ainsworth (2011), J. Learning Sciences 20(2):169-206, author accepted manuscript in Sheffield Hallam's SHURA repository; quote verified verbatim. Switching was engineered to be non-diagnostic of anything but preference: "When switching versions their exact position was resumed with intrinsic skeletons becoming extrinsic or visa versa... switching between versions neither provided a gameplay penalty, nor an advantage"; menu order randomised left/right. CRITICAL LIMIT: this free-choice arm is Study 2, n=16, ages 9y10m-11y2m (mean 10y4m) — NOT 6-8s. Children were also "reminded several times... that they were expected to try playing both versions," a mild demand characteristic that works against the observed gap. "Identical graphics" is slightly overstated (skeleton chest display differs; extrinsic adds a quiz screen).

### 2. In the actual 6-8 band, engagement and immediate performance did not discriminate — only delayed learning did. With 58 children aged 7y1m-8y10m (mean 8y0m) randomised to intrinsic / extrinsic / no-math-relevance versions plus matched teacher-led reflection over ~2 hours of play, group differences appeared ONLY at delayed test (F(2,48)=7.49, p<.001, partial eta-squared=.24; intrinsic beat extrinsic by ~16.9 and control by ~20.7 points), and only the intrinsic group kept improving after play stopped (post-to-delayed gain 7.04, p<.04).
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/1/Habgood_Ainsworth_final.pdf>

All figures verified verbatim against the accepted manuscript. Analytic n=51 (df=48) after 3 exclusions from 54 completers; the N=58 is the randomised sample (20/20/18). Practical implication for a 6-8 design: build in a delayed probe — an immediate post-session measure would have shown nothing. Note one apparent AAM typo ("p<0.14" for the intrinsic-vs-extrinsic post-hoc).

### 3. Documented construct/predictive-validity failure mode for young-child telemetry: mining 2,500+ log files from 7-8 year olds, in-game performance measures tracked PRIOR ability (pre-test r=.37-.44 for level reached, unique tasks, total tasks) but NONE correlated with learning gain (r=.18, .20, .25, -.14, all ns) — and in-game accuracy was HIGHER in the less effective condition (79.1% vs 70.1%, F(1,32)=7.86, p<.01) because those children attempted roughly half as many content items (198.2 vs 382.2 attempts). Accuracy can point the wrong way.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/1/Habgood_Ainsworth_final.pdf>

Table 5/Table 6 figures verified verbatim. The accuracy inversion is mechanically explained in-source (higher accuracy over far less practice), so the inference that accuracy is a misleading signal is grounded rather than editorial. Caveat: the correlation analysis is n=34 (17+17), so "no relation to gain" is a low-powered null — r=.25 would need n~60 to reach p<.05. A related, more aggressive version of this claim (that shell-progress measures are contaminated while content-interaction counts are clean) was REFUTED 1-2 in verification, because total-task count is itself among the measures that failed to predict gain.

### 4. The canonical free-choice / pressure-off probe is unobtrusive seconds-on-target-activity after the contingency ends (>90% of the free-choice studies in a 128-study meta-analysis), and it converges only weakly with self-reported interest (mean r=0.35, range -0.20 to 0.71; r=0.43 in the 5 child studies reporting both). The meta-analytic authors argue the behavioural measure is the more valid indicator because participants believe nobody is watching, and recommend treating the two as intrinsic motivation only when they correlate.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf>

Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin 125(6):627-668; full text verified from an Ovid print mirrored on a UW server. Two precision notes: the ">90%" denominator is the 101 free-choice studies, not all 128; and the paper says demand characteristics are "not likely to affect" the measure, weaker than "free of" them. This finding is in direct tension with the stealth-assessment convergence data below (r=-.01/-.06 behaviour vs self-report), so do not assume a self-report interest scale will corroborate a behavioural probe in children.

### 5. Dwell time / persistence is NOT a monotonic index of interest: its meaning reverses across children as a function of whether they are actually learning. In a reward-free free-switching touchscreen task, better performers stayed longer as learning progress rose (marginal beta=-.51, p=.025, CI[-0.95,-0.06]) and showed no novelty effect, whereas poorer performers persisted with the most FAMILIAR option (novelty beta=.96, p<.001) — the authors attribute this to attraction to controllable familiar situations. Anchored by significant performance x novelty (z=2.80, beta=.73, p=.005) and performance x LP (z=2.76, beta=.49, p=.006) interactions.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://srcd.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cdev.14158>, <https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article/96/1/192/8255468>

Poli, Meyer, Mars & Hunnius (2025), Child Development 96(1):192-202, doi:10.1111/cdev.14158; verified via PMC11693834 (Wiley 403s). Data/scripts on OSF (osf.io/tsv6q). Caveats: sample is 46-48 months (not 6-8), N=102 recruited / 87 analysed, primarily white middle-class Dutch, single site; analyses were confirmatory but NOT preregistered and the performance-conditioned marginal effects decompose an interaction; the "ignored novelty" arm is a non-significant estimate (beta=-.45, p=.075), i.e. absence of evidence. Practical upshot for a 6-8 product: any dwell-based interest score must be conditioned on the child's learning performance in that content, or it will systematically misread struggling children.

### 6. Aggregate exposure measures are poor proxies while trial-wise choice policy is informative: in the same 4-year-old free-choice game (N=87), switch frequency (t=-.26, p=.80) and total session length (t=-1.20, p=.24) were unrelated to learning, whereas voluntary next-choice was predicted by novelty (z=3.82, beta=.30, p<.001, e^beta=1.34[1.16,1.58]) and EXPECTED learning progress (z=2.03, beta=.35, p=.04, e^beta=1.42[1.02,1.99]) but not expected prediction error (p=.23); an expectation-based model beat a past-performance model (AIC 714 vs 721).
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://srcd.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cdev.14158>, <https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article/96/1/192/8255468>

All statistics verified verbatim. Design detail matters for portability: no extrinsic reward for correct guesses, free character switching, mean 27.8 trials / 4.21 min. Two honest limits: the two exposure nulls are p>.2 tests with no CIs or equivalence tests, so "unrelated" is absence of evidence, and 15/102 children were excluded precisely for playing <10 trials per character, truncating the low end of both exposure variables. Also, novelty (a first-order surface/exposure variable) was the STRONGEST predictor, so "choice tracks learnability not surface appeal" over-reads the result — a companion claim that novelty must be modelled out before treating choice as domain interest was refuted 1-2 on wording, not on the statistic.

### 7. Raw persistence is confounded with epistemic uncertainty independent of any reward: across three studies with 24-56-month-olds searching a sequence of doors for an object they NEVER find, children persisted longer in the higher-uncertainty condition, showing information gain alone sustains exploration with no external reward delivered.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/desc.13411>

Ruggeri, Stanciu, Pelz, Gopnik & Schulz (2023), Developmental Science 27(1), doi:10.1111/desc.13411, open access, no corrections attached; claim core is stated in the publisher abstract. Design implication: a mini-game that happens to be more epistemically uncertain will harvest more time-on-task regardless of domain interest, so uncertainty/learnability must be equated across domains or measured and modelled. Limits: 24-56 months (below the target band), and the paper contains no head-to-head reward-vs-information contrast. Note the tension with the Poli et al. result above, where prediction-error/uncertainty terms were null (p=.23 next-choice, p=.88 leave/stay) — uncertainty-sensitivity of persistence is not a settled parameterisation.

### 8. Time-on-task is only interpretable where difficulty affords displaying the construct: in Physics Playground, easier levels gave high performers fewer occasions to persist, which the investigators use to explain why convergent validity of the persistence trace dropped from r=.51 (low performers) to r=.22 (high performers). The same trace means different things at different skill levels.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/IJGCMS.pdf>

Wang, Shute & Moore, "Best Practices and Lessons Learned of Stealth Assessment," IJGCMS, author-hosted on Shute's FSU page; quote verified verbatim (p.11 of extracted text). Persistence was operationalised as "the average time spent on unsolved problems and the number of revisits to unsolved problems" — genuinely a dwell/return trace. Caveats: the difficulty-affordance mechanism is a post-hoc interpretation with no Fisher's z test of .51 vs .22, the high/low split is partly circular (defined by levels solved), and the sample is 13-15 year olds.

### 9. In-game persistence/switching partly measures self-regulation rather than interest: with age held constant, higher executive function and metacognition predicted Balancer or Exploiter class membership over Explorer in a 7-pond explore/exploit game (N=136, ages 3-7; classes were Explorers 55%, Exploiters 24%, Balancers 21%).
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/desc.13585>

Kim, Berry & Carlson (2024), Developmental Science, doi:10.1111/desc.13585; verified via Crossref-deposited abstract ("Controlling for age, children with higher executive function and metacognition tended to be classified as Balancers or Exploiters rather than Explorers") — Wiley 403s, so no effect sizes recoverable. The construct-validity framing is the analyst's inference; the authors frame the same result as an intervention target. Note that the related claim that the modal young child (55% Explorers) explores maladaptively against payoff was REFUTED 1-2, and it conflicts with the lawful-exploration modelling above; treat the exploration-class distribution as unsettled. Also relevant: age itself predicted class, so within a 6-8 band raw persistence traces carry maturational variance.

### 10. Engagement/dwell measures are not automatically comparable across activities because the latent driver may not operate in each: in 3.5-year-olds free-playing with three purpose-built novel toys (N=56), information-gain-based learning progress predicted play engagement for only 1 of the 3 toys, while performance-based learning progress predicted exploration for all three — the authors themselves call this "the role of context."
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/desc.70229>

de Boer, Poli, Meyer & Hunnius, "Guiding Curiosity: How Learning Progress Shapes Young Children's Exploration of New Toys," Developmental Science 29(4):e70229 (2026), PMID 42233395; verified via PubMed record (Wiley 403). This is the cleanest existence proof that a formal engagement driver is object/context-specific — the exact threat facing a multi-mini-game shell. Limits: 3.5-year-olds, N=56 split across toys so the 1-of-3 pattern is modestly powered and could reflect toy-specific power; the extension from toys to mini-games is the analyst's. A stronger companion claim (that stimulus-level "Exploration Qualities of Objects" must be equated before cross-content comparison) was REFUTED 1-2.

### 11. Tangible rewards undermine children's free-choice intrinsic motivation substantially more than adults': 57 child studies d=-0.39 [-0.47,-0.32] vs 38 college-student studies d=-0.27 [-0.36,-0.19], age moderation Qb(1)=4.18, p<.04 — a child effect about 44% larger.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf>

Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) meta-analysis of 128 experiments; quote and every number verified verbatim from the extracted full text. Two bounds: this is a post hoc moderator run at the aggregate tangible-reward level (the differentiated contingency categories showed no age effects), and the age moderator is coded as a BINARY "children vs college students" — "Most of the children were preschool or elementary students, but 6 studies used high school students." No age-continuous analysis exists, so this cannot be cited as an ages-5-9-specific estimate.

### 12. Engagement-contingent rewards — points given merely for engaging/spending time, the closest analogue to a points-for-play game shell — show the largest child-vs-adult divergence of any contingency: 43 child studies d=-0.46 [-0.55,-0.37] (-0.43 after removing 4 outliers) vs 12 college studies d=-0.21 [-0.37,-0.05], Qb(1)=6.76, p<.01; free-choice composite across all 55 studies d=-0.40, self-reported interest d=-0.15.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf>

All figures verified verbatim. IMPORTANT CORRECTION to how this is often stated: the source does NOT award engagement-contingency the largest undermining effect — contingency categories did not differ significantly (Qb(3)=5.97, p<.12), completion-contingent free-choice was statistically indistinguishable (d=-0.36, -0.44 after outlier removal), and the paper explicitly names less-than-maximum performance-contingent rewards as "the most detrimental type of rewards" (d=-0.80, -0.88 after one exclusion). The defensible statement is: engagement-contingency is the only contingency with a significant age moderator on free-choice, and its child estimate is large. Also, the -0.15 self-report figure is the engagement-contingent subset, not an omnibus (the overall expected-tangible self-report composite is d=-0.07). Practical read for a game shell: the discrepancy between behavioural (-0.46) and self-report (-0.15) undermining means a child can still SAY they like the domain while voluntarily returning less.

### 13. The undermining is not a satiation artifact and does not decay — it grows: immediate assessment d=-0.35 (32 studies; -0.40 after 2 outliers), delayed within a week d=-0.49 [-0.68,-0.31] (12 studies), delayed more than a week (typically ~2 weeks) d=-0.55 [-0.71,-0.40] (14 studies, -0.53 after outlier removal). All delayed-assessment studies used children.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf>

Verified verbatim from the "Is Undermining Transitory?" section, which was written specifically to answer the behaviourist satiation objection (Eisenberger & Cameron, 1996) and concludes undermining "is not merely transitory." Note an internal typo in the source itself ("the three timings of assessment did not affect the results, Qb(2) = 5.26, p < .01") — the claim does not rely on that moderator test. Design implication: a 2-week delayed pressure-off probe is exactly the window in which reward damage is most visible, so the probe that best measures durable interest is also the one most sensitive to your own reward design.

### 14. Praise-based enhancement cannot be assumed for young children: verbal rewards/positive feedback did NOT enhance children's free-choice intrinsic motivation (7 studies, d=0.11 [-0.11,0.34], ns) although they did for college students (14 studies, d=0.43 [0.27,0.58]), age moderation Qb(1)=5.14, p<.02.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf>

Verified verbatim; the paper itself calls this "a particularly noteworthy finding." Bounds: k=7 with CI upper bound 0.34, so this is a failure to demonstrate enhancement rather than proof of no effect; "children" is the same broad binary code; and the null is specific to the FREE-CHOICE measure — on self-report the same 24-study set showed d=0.36 with no age moderation (Qb(1)=0.18, ns). So a praising character may lift what a child reports without lifting what they voluntarily do.

### 15. Games-methodology researchers explicitly warn against the core cross-domain design: "This cautions against operationalising different conditions of constructs as different games, or generalizing findings from one game to another, let alone other game genres," because genre, controls, interface and required learning time "may all present differential barriers to engagement with different games."
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1046878118805515>

Gundry & Deterding, "Validity Threats in Quantitative Data Collection With Games: A Narrative Survey," Simulation & Gaming 50(3):302-328 (2019), CC-BY; quote verified verbatim via text proxy (SAGE 403s); green OA at eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/136864. The paper is self-described as "an unsystematic bottom-up literature review" applying Shadish/Cook/Campbell validity typologies — expert methodological argument, no effect sizes, nothing about children. The application to domain-specific mini-games for 6-8s is the analyst's extension. A stronger companion claim (that equal appeal across game conditions is generally UNATTAINABLE, with resentful demoralisation and compensatory rivalry) was refuted 1-2, so treat medium-neutrality as very hard rather than provably impossible.

### 16. Feature-matching a shell on a checklist has a documented failure mode on off-list dimensions: Anderson & Dill (2000) matched Wolfenstein 3D against Myst on "blood pressure, heart rate, frustration, difficulty, action pace, enjoyment and excitement," yet missed that the games also differed in competitiveness, which proved to be a significant confound.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1046878118805515>

Both sentences verified verbatim via the Semantic Scholar citation-context API for this paper's reference list. Attribution chain is nested: the matched-dimension list is Gundry & Deterding quoting Adachi & Willoughby (2011, Aggression and Violent Behavior 16(1):55-62, p.58), not Anderson & Dill's own methods text; the empirical demonstration that competitiveness rather than violence drove the outcome is Adachi & Willoughby (2011, Psychology of Violence), which is the source to cite if effect sizes are needed — Gundry & Deterding report none. Practical read: publish your matching checklist, but expect an unlisted dimension (competitiveness, social presence, character appeal) to carry the confound.

### 17. Where the game's incentive structure makes one response strategically advantageous, the game confounds the elicited response — the authors' worked example is a game eliciting ice-cream preferences in which there is an in-game advantage to answering "chocolate" even if the player prefers strawberry — and "the focus on winning the game may also override participants' desire to be a good study subject and lead them to cheat."
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1046878118805515>

Quote verified verbatim from the White Rose green-OA PDF (section "Play May Differ from the Target Situation" > "Games Invite Strategic Action"); the internal "Gundry & Deterding (2018)" pointer is a self-citation to their FDG design-guidelines paper. This passage is conceptual — no study, no effect size — and the paper concedes "eight years on, there is still little if any work on this issue." It concerns adult participants; no child-specific inference is licensed. Direct implication: if choosing a domain earns in-game advantage (better unlockables, faster progression), the choice signal is destroyed.

### 18. Generic game affinity is a construct-irrelevant contaminant of both performance and free-choice measures, and thresholding on play intensity rather than any-play attenuates it: pre-intervention liking for video games predicted ANY voluntary home play (beta=0.267, z=2.99, p=0.003) but went non-significant for above-median (beta=0.123, SE=0.098) and above-75th-percentile (beta=0.071) home-match counts, while in-game performance (0.613 then 0.632) and enjoyment (0.588 then 0.486) coefficients held or grew. In adults, self-reported game usage was positively related to game-based-assessment performance but NEGATIVELY related to the external criterion (academic performance) across N=156 and N=92 samples, which the authors interpret as test bias.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/gd6wp_v1>, <https://iaap-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/apps.70038>

Threshold figures verified verbatim in Tables 6 and 7 of the OSF preprint (Seven Spells maths game, 1,930 primary children, 88 classes, 3 months of post-intervention logs). Caution: the paper never formally tests whether the LikeVG coefficient DIFFERS across models and the CIs overlap heavily ([0.09,0.45] vs [-0.07,0.31]), so this is "no longer significant," not "significantly smaller"; the enjoyment coefficient is also non-monotonic (0.458 -> 0.588 -> 0.486); and the above-75th row reports SE=0.592 inconsistent with its own CI (likely typo). The adult test-bias source is Ohlms, Hohner & Melchers (2025), Applied Psychology, verified via Crossref abstract only (Wiley 403); its "applicant" sample is a simulated potential-applicant pool, and the authors say game usage "enhances skills relevant to success in cognitive GBAs but not necessarily to academic performance," softer than "not true ability variance."

### 19. Stealth-assessment practice validates in-game traces against COVERT performance-based external measures, not self-report, and against criteria dimensionally matched to the game's demands. With 154 8th-9th graders in Physics Playground, the trace persistence measure correlated r=-.01 and r=-.06 with an 8-item IPIP self-report but r=.51 (p<.01, low performers) and r=.22 (p<.05, high performers) with behavioural persistence on deliberately impossible items. In Portal 2 (77 undergraduates, 42 playing, 8 hours), in-game performance correlated r=.02 with Raven's and r=.18 with Remote Associates (both null) but r=.38 with insight problems and r=.34 with a spatial-navigation assessment matched to the game's demands.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/IJGCMS.pdf>

Both sets of correlations verified verbatim from the author-hosted IJGCMS text. The covert external measure (PBMP) used picture-comparison items where "The fourth 'difference' was actually only a one-pixel deletion and thus was impossible to detect," plus low-frequency anagrams, with true purpose undisclosed. Caveats: this is a lessons-learned retrospective, not the full empirical report (originals: Shute, Ventura & Kim 2013; Shute, Ventura & Ke 2015, Computers & Education 80:58-67); no per-correlation n, no CIs, only "p<.05", and with n<=42 in the Portal 2 arm the critical r is ~.30 so r=.38/.34 sit just above threshold and the .18-vs-.38 contrast is not itself tested. Samples are 13-15 and undergraduates, never 6-8. The gloss "not on the game being engaging" is an extension — the paper argues only about dimensional alignment.

### 20. The narrative/wrapper literature cannot currently attribute engagement to narrative at all, has no shared isolation model, and its measurement base is unvalidated: across a systematic review of 15 narrative DGBL systems, effects on engagement/motivation/learning were coded from unverified self-report as categorical positive/negative/neutral/N-A with no effect sizes, only 6 of 15 measured both engagement and motivation, and a positive result in ANY one demographic subgroup was liberally coded positive for the whole system. The review also flags that following a game narrative consumes cognitive capacity needed for the academic material and that young children "may be particularly prone to such overload, due to their immature cognitive and attention skills."
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/bjet.13004>

Breien & Wasson (2021), British Journal of Educational Technology 52(1):91-111, doi:10.1111/bjet.13004, CC-BY-NC; the coding-methodology quotes were verified verbatim from the CORE open-access copy, and the overload passage's three cited works (Pilegard & Mayer 2016; Ross, Pye & Randell 2016; Courage et al. 2015) were confirmed in the reference list. Three calibration notes: the overload passage is a BACKGROUND enumeration of counter-hypotheses, not the review's conclusion (the abstract says narratives "may reinforce" positive effects); the source's hedge is "may be particularly prone," not "most vulnerable population"; and the poor-measurement finding is partly about the REVIEW's own coding decisions rather than proof the primary instruments were bad. The only cited primary with an in-range child sample is Ross et al. (2016), 7-year-olds sharing touchscreen storybooks — which found comprehension COST alongside enriched emotional engagement, i.e. engagement and learning dissociating. The eLNVM contribution is an analytic categorisation scheme, not an experimental wrapper-vs-content separation method.

### 21. Reward-free free-choice probes are behaviourally feasible with children as young as 4, with quantified yield limits: N=102 4-year-olds played a 22-inch touchscreen hide-and-seek game with three freely abandonable characters and no points or rewards for correct guesses, producing usable trial-by-trial stay/leave and choice data; 15/102 (~15%) were excluded for not playing all characters for at least 10 trials, 1 for parental interference, leaving 87 modelable, and voluntary sessions averaged only 27.8 trials / 4.21 minutes (SD 1.64).
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://srcd.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cdev.14158>, <https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article/96/1/192/8255468>

Method text verified verbatim: "children were instructed that they could click on another character to switch characters"; "no external rewards were given for correctly guessing the character's hiding spot." The authors explicitly frame the reward-off design as what licenses an intrinsic-motivation reading: "our approach abstains from using extrinsic rewards to assess the balance between explorative and exploitative behaviors... This absence of external motivators positions our study to offer valuable insights into the intrinsic motivations that underpin children's exploration decisions." Two precision corrections: "no praise" is an inference (the paper reports no evaluative feedback but never states praise was withheld), and termination was researcher-prompted, not purely child-initiated — sessions ended at >=35 trials with any character OR when the child declined the researcher's offer to continue. So this validates a ~4-minute probe, not a long free-choice period, and at age 4 rather than 6-8.

### 22. A pressure-off free-choice probe can be run at scale with primary-school children via game-server logs, with a ~2-week initiation window: after a 6-week in-class intervention, home access was unlocked, teachers were told not to assign or reward play, 9 non-compliant classes (245 students) were excluded, and of ~1,590 analysed children 52.1% voluntarily played at home — with all but two home players initiating within two weeks of the intervention ending.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/gd6wp_v1>

All figures verified verbatim in the OSF preprint (Seven Spells, Irish primary schools, TU Dublin REIC-21-104). Six load-bearing caveats: (1) the ~2-week window captures INITIATION only — the authors themselves used 2-4-month sensitivity analyses for dose, and retention was thin (Day 1 31.6%, Day 7 15.8%, Day 14 11.9%); (2) the outcome is binary >=1 home match, i.e. onset not durability; (3) age band is 2nd-6th class, average 7-11, with only ~14% plausibly 7-9 and zero 6-year-olds, and no age-stratified analysis of the window; (4) the pressure-off purification is asserted, not demonstrated — "our results did not change when these nine classes were included," and teacher verbal or parental encouragement is undetectable in logs; (5) ICC=0.176 with classes ranging from 5-10% to >90% home play, so peer contagion and teacher attitude dominate and cross-child comparison is confounded by class; (6) unrefereed preprint, single game and genre, single country, and the authors both delivered the intervention and control the game logs while concluding the game triggers intrinsic motivation.

### 23. Domain competence and wrapper enjoyment contribute non-redundant variance to voluntary return, so a free-choice probe measures both at once: in a fully controlled mixed-effects logistic model (students nested in classes and schools, standardized betas), in-game performance (0.565, z=4.25, p<0.001), game enjoyment (0.458, z=4.90, p<0.001) and game-independent domain skill (national Irish maths STEN, 0.402, z=3.38, p<0.001) all predicted home play (marginal R2=0.27, conditional R2=0.445), with game performance mediating 45.8% of the maths-skill effect (direct 0.248 [0.068,0.427], mediated 0.210 [0.107,0.334]). Maths anxiety was null (beta=-0.011, p=.91).
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/gd6wp_v1>

Every coefficient verified against Tables 6 and 8 of the preprint; the "two non-mutually exclusive paths: a performance-driven one... and an enjoyment-driven one" framing is the authors'. Note the gloss "wrapper enjoyment" is loose — game enjoyment is a single 1-10 self-report of enjoying the maths game (mean 8.05, SD 1.4), never decomposed into shell vs content, and performance predicts enjoyment (beta=0.48, R2=36.7%), so the two routes are correlated rather than orthogonal. This directly tempers the Habgood inference that holding the wrapper constant removes wrapper attribution: even with domain skill measured entirely off-game, game-side affect remains a strong independent predictor of who comes back.

### 24. Durable interest can be operationalised as spontaneous re-engagement outside the study, captured by a delayed parent interview at 32-67 days (mean 51), and young children's sustained interests separate into "focused" interests bound to the specific triggering materials versus "broad" interests that transfer to the topic or practices — mapped by the authors onto maintained situational vs emerging individual interest. Of 7 children, 4 showed broad and 3 focused sustained interest.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://informalscience.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/PattisonDierking2018_EarlyChildSciInterest_PrePub.pdf>

Pattison & Dierking (2018), Science Education 103(2):362-388, doi:10.1002/sce.21486; author accepted manuscript on informalscience.org (NSF/CAISE repository); typology and 4/3 split verified verbatim. This is the closest conceptual analogue in the literature to the wrapper-bound vs domain-level distinction — "focused sustained interests, focused exclusively around the materials and activities presented during the sessions" vs "broad sustained interests, which extended beyond specific materials and activities to more general topics or practices." But it is NOT a measurement instrument: N=7 low-income Head Start mothers and their FOUR-year-old DAUGHTERS, classification from parent-report stimulated-recall interviews (partly prompted, not purely open-ended), no game wrapper, and the authors flag their own key leap as untested — "we made the assumption in this study, which should be tested in future research, that the sustained interests reported by mothers would provide the foundation for longer-term interest." Parent report here also carries demand characteristics from having enrolled in a science-interest study. A companion claim about in-session engagement being non-discriminating at this age (all children highly engaged; interest-LOSS moments coded instead, 0-17/session) was REFUTED 1-2.

The authors argue free-choice behavior is the more valid indicator because it is unobtrusive and thus not subject to demand characteristics, and recommend treating the two as intrinsic motivation only when they correlate within conditions." **vs** "The meaning of persistence-vs-switching reverses across children as a function of their actual learning: better learners stayed longer as learning progress rose (marginal β = -.51, p = .025) and were insensitive to novelty, whereas poorer learners stayed with the more FAMILIAR option (novelty β = .96, p < .001) — so time-on-task/dwell alone is not a monotonic index of interest without conditioning on learning performance." — Deci et al. treat voluntary time-on-target as the most valid (demand-free) index of intrinsic motivation, whereas the preschool free-switching study shows dwell/persistence is non-monotonic and reverses meaning depending on the child's learning performance.

- "Initial in-session engagement was non-discriminating at this age — every child started every session highly engaged, so the authors abandoned in-session engagement level as an interest signal and instead coded moments of situational interest LOSS and whether the adult successfully re-engaged the child. - "Equal appeal across game conditions is treated as generally unattainable, not merely hard: the authors state that two experimental conditions often cannot be equally fun, and that participants who perceive their condition as inferior (too hard or too easy) suffer resentful demoralisation, while control participants may over-exert via compensatory rivalry — both of which bias engagement measures in the direction of wrapper appeal rather than content." **vs** "Stimulus-level properties independent of the child — what the authors term "Exploration Qualities of Objects" (EQO) — measurably drive which items 4–6.5-year-olds choose and how much they explore them, meaning object/material appeal is a confound that must be equated before cross-content comparisons of a child's exploratory engagement can be interpreted as differential interest." — The EQO paper prescribes equating stimulus appeal as the prerequisite for valid cross-content engagement comparisons; the game-methodology survey holds that equal appeal across conditions is generally unattainable, implying that prerequisite cannot be met.
- "Behavioral-trace signals differed sharply in diagnosticity: shell-progress measures (level reached; number of unique math tasks encountered) did NOT distinguish conditions (F(1,32) = 0.36 and F(1,32) = 1.00), whereas volume of actual engagement with the domain content did — extrinsic-condition children performed roughly half as many total math attempts (198.2 vs 382.2) despite equal shell progress. ## Caveats
AGE COVERAGE IS THE BIGGEST WEAKNESS. Almost no confirmed finding is from a 6-8 sample doing the thing being claimed. The behavioural-trace mechanics (dwell reversal, learning-progress choice policy, uncertainty-driven persistence, exploration classes) come from 24-56-month-olds and 3.5-4-year-olds; the free-choice-switching demonstration is 9y10m-11y2m; the stealth-assessment validity work is 13-15-year-olds and undergraduates; the at-scale log probe averages 7-11 with zero 6-year-olds and only ~14% aged 7-9; the reward meta-analysis codes age as a binary "children vs college students" that includes high-schoolers and never runs an age-continuous moderator. The ONLY genuinely in-band primary study is Habgood & Ainsworth Study 1 (n=58, 7y1m-8y10m), and that arm had no free-choice probe at all. Any claim about ages 5-9 specifically, or about running free-choice probes with 6-8s, is an interpolation between a 4-year-old feasibility demonstration and a 10-year-old free-choice demonstration.

UNRESOLVED CONFLICTS THAT AFFECT DESIGN DECISIONS. (1) Is voluntary time-on-task a good signal or a bad one? Deci et al. treat it as the most valid, demand-free index of intrinsic motivation; Poli et al. find total time played and switch frequency statistically unrelated to actual learning (p=.24, p=.80) and show its meaning reverses with learning performance. Best reconciliation: time-on-target is a valid index of motivation-to-do-this-activity but NOT of learning or of domain (vs activity) interest, and it must be conditioned on performance. (2) Does behaviour converge with self-report? Deci et al. report r=.35 overall / .43 in child studies; Shute's stealth data show r=-.01/-.06 against an IPIP scale. Do not plan on a self-report interest scale corroborating a behavioural probe in children. (3) Is persistence uncertainty-driven? Ruggeri et al. say yes (higher-uncertainty condition sustained longer search with no reward); Poli et al. find prediction-error terms null (p=.23, p=.88). (4) Is the modal young child an over-explorer or an under-explorer? Kim et al. find 55% "Explorers" sampling against payoff; Poli et al. find lower performers sticking to the most familiar option. (5) Does holding the wrapper constant remove wrapper attribution? Habgood & Ainsworth say yes by construction; the Seven Spells model finds game enjoyment an independent strong predictor of return even with domain skill measured off-game, and generic video-game liking predicts any-play (beta=0.267). (6) Can shells be made equally appealing at all? The stimulus-equating prescription and Gundry & Deterding's pessimism point opposite ways, and both of the sharpest versions of these claims were refuted in verification.

SOURCE-QUALITY FLAGS. The at-scale log probe (findings on the 2-week window, two routes, and intensity thresholding) rests entirely on an UNREFEREED OSF preprint with developer/deliverer allegiance — the authors ran the intervention, own the game logs, and conclude the game triggers intrinsic motivation. Several Wiley/SAGE sources (BJET narrative review, Developmental Science EF paper, Applied Psychology test-bias paper, the Child Development article at its Wiley URL) returned 403 and were verified via Crossref/PubMed/PMC/CORE metadata and abstracts rather than by reading the full paywalled text; the EF/metacognition finding therefore has no recoverable effect sizes. The Deci et al. text is an Ovid print mirrored on a university server, not the publisher of record, with Table 1 embedded as unreadable images. Two Habgood & Ainsworth findings and the Pattison & Dierking findings come from author accepted manuscripts, and both contain visible typos (extrinsic "sd = 10.28" equal to its mean; "p<0.14"). The Shute IJGCMS paper is a lessons-learned retrospective that re-reports two earlier studies without correlation tables, CIs, exact p-values or per-correlation n.

STATISTICAL FRAGILITY. Several load-bearing results are underpowered nulls presented as absences: the "in-game measures don't predict learning" result is n=34; the "aggregate exposure is unrelated to learning" result is two p>.2 tests with no CIs or equivalence tests, on a sample where the low end of exposure was truncated by exclusion criteria; the praise null is k=7 with CI upper bound 0.34; the 1-of-3-toys context result is N=56 split across toys. The engagement-contingent "largest effect" framing is not what the source says (contingency categories did not differ, Qb(3)=5.97, p<.12; less-than-maximum performance-contingent rewards were the most detrimental at d=-0.80). Neither Poli et al. paper was preregistered.

FOURTEEN CLAIMS WERE REFUTED during verification and should not be relied on, notably: that verbal information-seeking is less shell-dependent than affective signals (0-3); that free-play engagement decomposes into two dissociable validated constructs (0-3); that Rules/Goals + Challenge + Mystery beat points/badges/leaderboards (0-2); that shell-progress measures are contaminated while content-interaction counts are clean (1-2); that learning domain moderates gamification effects (1-2); that a validated gaming-the-system detector failed across contexts (1-2); and that in-session engagement is non-discriminating at age 4 (1-2).

TIME-SENSITIVITY. The core reward-undermining evidence is a 1999 meta-analysis whose child studies predate digital games entirely; nothing found here tests points/badges/character rewards inside a modern touchscreen game with 6-8s. The most directly relevant recent work (de Boer et al. 2026, Ohlms et al. 2025, the Seven Spells preprint 2025) is all within the last 18 months and partly unrefereed, so this area is actively moving.

## Open questions
- Does the intrinsic-integration free-choice paradigm (penalty-free switching between builds identical except in how domain content binds to the core mechanic) actually run with 6-8 year olds? Habgood & Ainsworth deliberately used an older year group for the free-choice arm and never explain whether their 7-8 cohort was judged unable to handle a version-switching menu. This is the single most important untested step for the design in question.
- Do modern game-shell rewards (points, badges, unlockables, character praise) reproduce the 1999 engagement-contingent undermining effect (child d=-0.46) when the reward is ambient and continuous rather than an announced contingency, and is the ~2-week-delayed free-choice probe still the most sensitive detection window in that setting? No study found here tests reward-undermining inside a digital game with 6-8s.
- Can any cross-domain comparison of a child's engagement be made valid, or must the design abandon cross-domain ranking in favour of within-domain contrasts? Given that different mini-games cannot be operationalised as different conditions (Gundry & Deterding), that feature-matching fails on off-list dimensions (Anderson & Dill), that a formal engagement driver operated for only 1 of 3 toys (de Boer et al.), and that uncertainty/learnability alone drives persistence (Ruggeri et al.), a per-domain within-child baseline-versus-probe design may be the only defensible option — but no study has tested it.
- What is the minimum reliable free-choice probe protocol at ages 6-8: duration, number of exposures, whether the probe must be at home or off-platform, and how to distinguish child-initiated return from parent- or peer-driven return? The 4-year-old paradigm yields only ~4 minutes with ~15% unusable, and the at-scale log probe has ICC=0.176 with classroom effects swamping individual signal, so neither establishes a usable per-child measurement protocol.
- How should a dwell/persistence signal be conditioned on the child's learning performance and executive function to be readable as interest rather than as struggle, familiarity-seeking, or self-regulation capacity? The reversal effects and EF-predicts-class findings imply a correction is needed, but no published model specifies one for this age band.

## Refuted claims (transparency)
- "Stimulus/material-level appeal ("Exploration Qualities of Objects") independently drives which items 4–6.5-year-olds choose and how much they explore them, so object/task appeal is a confound that must be equated before cross-content differences in exploratory engagement can be read as differential domain interest." (vote 1-2)
- "In the same 4–6.5-year-olds (n=64), cognitive curiosity indicators (describing phenomena, question-asking) were stable across tasks while emotional and sensorimotor indicators were not — verbal information-seeking is less task-shell-dependent than affective/approach signals." (vote 0-3)
- "In-session engagement level was non-discriminating with 4-year-olds (all children started every session highly engaged), so the authors coded moments of situational-interest LOSS (0–17/session, mean 6, worst during book reading) and adult re-engagement success (38%–100% by family); higher and more activity-focused re-engagement tracked broad rather than focused interest." (vote 1-2)
- "Novelty (how rarely an option had been chosen) was the strongest predictor of which option children switched to (z=3.82, β=.30, p<.001, e^β=1.34 [1.16,1.58]) independent of expected learning progress (β=.35, p=.04) — switching-toward behavior partly tracks shell unfamiliarity and novelty must be modeled out before treating choice as domain interest." (vote 1-2)

(vote 1-2)
- "A validated action-level gaming-the-system detector (KE-GD) failed to track its construct across contexts: no negative association with learning in three classroom datasets, and in the Fall 2019 control condition more detected gaming predicted HIGHER posttest scores (condition interaction b=-0.98, p=.007; overall rho=-.02, p=.86)." (vote 1-2)
- "Shell-progress measures (level reached, unique math tasks encountered) failed to distinguish conditions (F(1,32)=0.36 and 1.00), whereas volume of actual domain-content engagement did: extrinsic children made ~half as many total math attempts (198.2 vs 382.2) at equal shell progress — progress-through-the-game is wrapper-contaminated; count of content interactions is not."
