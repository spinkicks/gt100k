# Research: For children ages 6-8, what does the evidence say about which activity FORMATS actually trigger and then MAINTAIN domain-specific interest, as opposed to producing only a momentary novelty spike? Compare short videos, digital games and puzzles, hands-on making with physical materials, guided multi-session projects, storybooks and read-alouds, live demonstrations by a practitioner, real-world field experiences, and social/collaborative work. How should a multi-medium 'taster' rotation be designed for this age: how many exposures per domain, how long each session, spaced over what span, and in what sequence, to move a 6-8 year old from triggered situational interest toward emerging individual interest (Hidi & Renninger phases)? Which formats produce durable voluntary re-engagement and which only produce novelty? Include evidence on sustained-attention span and session-length norms at ages 6-8, fine-motor and reading-level constraints on what a child can do unaided, screen vs physical medium effects, and critically: is there evidence that interest triggered inside one medium (e.g. a game ABOUT math, or a video ABOUT insects) transfers to engagement with the underlying domain itself outside that medium?

_2026-07-25_

## Summary
The honest answer is that no format has been shown to reliably trigger and maintain domain interest in 6-8 year olds, and almost no evidence speaks to that age band directly: the strongest dosage, format-comparison, and transfer studies were run on 9-12 year olds, university students, or single families, and the only close-to-band studies (Norwegian 4-8 outdoor observation, a 5-7.9 pupillometry vigilance task, a 3-6 Tower-of-Hanoi transfer experiment) are small and descriptive. What the evidence does support is that maintenance, not triggering, is the binding constraint — triggering happens easily in almost every medium (especially first-hand physical/outdoor encounters), but progression requires re-engaging the SAME content repeatedly while an adult or the instructional design brokers connections to the child's own knowledge, and unsupported solo formats predict stalling at the trigger. The one usable dosage benchmark (Rotgans & Schmidt 2017, ~9-10 year olds) is four distinct triggering stimuli over four weeks, two ~1-hour sessions per week; converting that to ages 6-8 means keeping activity blocks near the observed elementary norm of ~10-17 minutes (on-task odds are 1.42x higher for a 10- vs 30-minute block; a measurable vigilance decrement appears within ~6 minutes even with continuous game-style rewards), so plan 4-6 short spaced exposures per domain rather than one long taster. On the critical transfer question, evidence is split by construct: skill transfers across media for isomorphic tasks (iPad Tower of Hanoi to physical puzzle, ages 4-6, even with no prior physical exposure), and repeated triggering does raise voluntary out-of-medium domain activity with 10-month persistence in one adult study, but interest triggered inside a math game and then NOT maintained was associated with a marked DECLINE in domain interest pre-to-post — a badly maintained taster can leave a child worse off than no taster. Design implication: rotate multiple distinct formats per domain, treat the per-child response (frequency, depth, and voluntary/independent re-engagement) as the measurement unit rather than the format, and never trigger a domain you cannot follow up on.

## Findings

### 1. Triggering is not reliable at the format level. The same potential trigger fails for some children because its effect is jointly moderated by the specific activity and the individual learner's readiness, and trigger design features alone do not account for how long engagement lasts — how the adult and child co-construct the interaction around the trigger matters too. A taster rotation must therefore offer multiple distinct formats per domain and be evaluated per-child, not per-format.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2210656118301685>, <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329883677_Triggering_and_maintaining_interest_in_early_phases_of_interest_development>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tea.22028>, <https://works.swarthmore.edu/fac-education/162/>

Renninger, Bachrach & Hidi (2019), Learning, Culture and Social Interaction 23, DOI 10.1016/j.lcsi.2018.11.007: "learners do not perceive and respond identically to potential triggers for interest; and that the triggering process is nuanced by particular activity, and the readiness of the learner to respond." Vivante & Vedder-Weiss (2025), J. Res. Sci. Teaching 62(7):1814-1834, DOI 10.1002/tea.22028: "this difference was related not only to features of the trigger design but also to the way participants co-constructed the interaction around it." LIMITS: the Renninger study is n=8 Black inner-city MIDDLE-school youths in one out-of-school biology workshop — "earlier phases" refers to phases of interest, not younger children, so nothing here is 6-8 evidence. The Vivante study is an insider ethnography of ONE family; the co-construction conclusion rests on interaction analysis of only TWO illustrative episodes, it says nothing about peers, and it never compared activity formats. The prescriptive clause ("must offer multiple formats, evaluate per-child") is the synthesizer's design inference, not a finding of either paper.

### 2. Progression is sequential and the bottleneck is maintenance, not triggering: moving a child from a trigger to emerging individual interest is a two-step transition through a distinct maintained-situational phase, produced by re-engaging the SAME previously triggering content while being supported to connect it to one's own skills, knowledge and prior experience. In Phase 1 the child cannot sustain engagement alone — support from other people OR from the instructional design itself is a necessary condition — and independent re-engagement only becomes an indicator at Phase 3.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://works.swarthmore.edu/fac-education/12/>, <https://stelar.edc.org/sites/default/files/2022-11/Day%202_Ann%20Renninger%20.pdf>

Hidi & Renninger (2006), Educational Psychologist 41(2):111-127, DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4, p.112: "The four phases are considered to be sequential and distinct, and represent a form of cumulative, progressive development in cases where interest is supported and sustained"; and "There are no data suggesting that individual interest emerges without first being experienced as a situational interest." Renninger's own STELAR/EDC deck (7 slides, first-party, captioned "Figure 1: The Four-Phase Model... (Hidi & Renninger, 2006), Revised; Renninger & Hidi (2022)"): Phase 1 learners "Attend to content, if only fleetingly / Need support to engage from others (e.g., group work, instructional conversation) or through instructional design (e.g., software)"; Phase 2 learners "Re-engage content that previously triggered attention / Are supported by others to find connections between their skills, knowledge, and prior experience / Are developing knowledge of the content / Are developing a sense of the content's value"; Phase 3 is the first to read "Independently re-engage content." TWO IMPORTANT QUALIFIERS: (a) the source's own "(e.g., software)" means a well-designed SOLO digital activity can satisfy Phase 1's support requirement — "solo" is not the same as "unsupported," so "screen formats stall at the trigger" would be contradicted by this very source; and "supported by others" includes peers and designed software, not adults only. (b) Sequentiality is a theoretical assumption with thin longitudinal validation — the authors note "Only a few studies have been conducted that have addressed the development of interest over time" — and any phase "can become dormant, regress to a previous phase, or disappear altogether" without support. Nothing in either source is age-6-8 specific.

### 3. The field's own originators stated (2018/2019) that the process by which interest is triggered and then maintained remains poorly understood, so any strong ranking of activity formats attributed to this literature is extrapolation rather than a finding.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2210656118301685>, <https://works.swarthmore.edu/fac-education/162/>

Renninger, Bachrach & Hidi (2019), art. 100260: "relatively little is understood about the process that results in triggering and then maintaining the development of interest." This is an author-declared gap statement by two of the three originators of the four-phase model, i.e. the strongest possible source class for a meta-claim about what the field does not know. The second clause ("so format-ranking claims are extrapolation") is the synthesizer's inference, licensed but not asserted by the source. Use this citation only for the general gap, not for age-specific parameters — that particular study's sample is middle-school-age.

### 4. Interest development has a falsifiable observable test that separates a novelty spike from developing interest: frequency of engagement, depth of engagement, and — given the opportunity — voluntary and/or independent re-engagement, in addition to positive affect. Voluntary re-engagement is therefore the primary outcome a taster rotation should instrument.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://stelar.edc.org/sites/default/files/2022-11/Day%202_Ann%20Renninger%20.pdf>

Verbatim from Renninger's slide "Indicators of interest development": "Positive feelings, in addition to: Frequency of engagement / Depth of engagement / and, given the opportunity, Voluntary and/or Independent engagement." First-party from the model's originator and corroborated in refereed venues — Renninger & Hidi (2021), Theory Into Practice, DOI 10.1080/00405841.2021.1932159, applies exactly these indicators to a case learner and to classroom observation; the ASEE FIDES instrument (peer.asee.org) operationalizes them as independent content engagement, independent questioning, positive feelings, perseverance, and content knowledge. CAVEAT: the "falsifiable test" framing is the synthesizer's, not the source's — the deck lists indicators and separately describes Phase 1 vs Phase 2, and never asserts a falsifiability criterion. Cite the peer-reviewed Renninger & Hidi (2021)/(2016) alongside the deck for that step.

### 5. Triggering is not confined to Phase 1 — it recurs in later phases — which supports repeated re-triggering exposures across a spaced rotation rather than one taster hit followed only by maintenance activity.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329883677_Triggering_and_maintaining_interest_in_early_phases_of_interest_development>, <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lcsi.2018.11.007>

Renninger, Bachrach & Hidi (2019), Learning, Culture and Social Interaction 23: "Triggering interest (the initiation of the psychological state of interest) can occur in both earlier and later phases of interest development. However, in this study we focus on this process in earlier phases of interest development." Crossref-verified as a peer-reviewed Elsevier journal article (DOI 10.1016/j.lcsi.2018.11.007); cite the DOI, not the ResearchGate mirror. LIMIT: this licenses the theoretical statement that re-triggering is expected across phases, but the paper's empirical work is on early phases only and it carries NO evidence on spacing intervals, exposure counts, or rotation sequencing.

### 6. The only concrete dosage benchmark in this evidence base is four distinct interest-triggering stimuli (one novel problem per week) delivered as two 1-hour sessions per week over four weeks (8 hours total), which produced significant growth in individual interest in primary science students — and the effect was on the GROWTH SLOPE of individual interest, not merely its starting level.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2017.02.003>, <https://repub.eur.nl/pub/97987>

Rotgans & Schmidt (2017), "Interest development: Arousing situational interest affects the growth trajectory of individual interest," Contemporary Educational Psychology 49:175-184 (Crossref-verified journal article, ~140-176 citations). Methods: "Students met twice a week for one hour (i.e., 8 hours in total over 4 weeks). Four instructional problems were used as stimuli to repeatedly arouse students' situational interest in the science topic." Study 2 abstract: "the treatment condition received four situational-interest-inducing science problems as part of a course whereas the control condition did not, all other things being equal... only the group receiving problems experienced repeated arousal of situational interest and its related growth in individual interest." Slope evidence, Study 1: "mean situational interest arousal significantly predicted the initial level of individual interest (= .25, p < .001) and had a significant effect on the slope parameter (= .10, p = .02)... empirical evidence in support of the Hidi and Renninger model." Study 2 reported as slope beta=.18 p<.001, intercept beta=.03 p=.78 — those Study 2 figures were NOT in the verified quote and the full text is paywalled, so confirm them against the Results table before publishing. AGE CAVEAT: primary-school science students (this research line uses upper-primary/Grade-5, ~10-11 yrs, Singapore); the "mean age ~9.5-10" parenthetical is the weakest-attested detail and is not recoverable from indexed metadata. Applying the schedule to 6-8 year olds is extrapolation, and the 1-hour block conflicts with the attention evidence below.

### 7. Doing nothing is not neutral: in the control condition that received no deliberate triggering stimuli, individual interest in science measurably DECAYED over four weeks rather than staying flat (control mean slope -.03, p<.001 vs +.03, p<.001 for treatment).
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2017.02.003>, <https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/efb12dd5-eb9a-4aa0-81e5-64e18b36b1a4>

Rotgans & Schmidt (2017): "Although the means for the slopes for both groups were significantly different from zero (treatment Ms = .03, p < .001 and control Ms = -.03, p < .001), growth was negative for the control group... not receiving problems resulted in a significant decrease in individual interest for science over time." Study 2 = quasi-experiment, N=129, four classes randomly assigned, latent growth curve modeling. LIMITS: only four classes as clusters, so cluster-level confounds cannot be separated from condition; the slope magnitude (±.03) is statistically significant but practically slight; sample is upper-primary, not 6-8. The full text could not be opened for verbatim confirmation (ScienceDirect paywall, NTU repository returns an AWS WAF CAPTCHA) — structural elements confirmed via publisher/repository abstract.

### 8. Repeated in-course situational interest can produce durable, not novelty-spike, outcomes: both individual-interest change and increased self-initiated out-of-class domain activity (reading about, talking about, and watching content in the domain) remained relatively stable at a 10-month follow-up after the course ended.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11165-016-9526-x>

Palmer, Dixon & Archer, "Using Situational Interest to Enhance Individual Interest and Science-Related Behaviours," Research in Science Education: "regular experiences of situational interest during the course were associated with positive changes in individual interest in science and increased participation in science-related activities. These changes remained relatively stable over a delay period of 10 months after the end of the course." MAJOR SCOPE CAVEAT: participants were "primary teacher education students" — i.e. ADULTS in a semester-length science course, not children. This source cannot be cited as evidence about 6-8 year olds; it supports the phase-transition mechanism generically and any transfer to ages 6-8 must be flagged as extrapolation. Also correlational: single cohort, no control group, "were associated with"; the claim's firmer "persisted" tightens the original's "relatively stable."

### 9. Deliberately designed triggers are rare in everyday family life (~18% of 397 coded triggering events; 82% undesigned) but elicited LONGER stretches of situational-interest engagement than undesigned/incidental triggers — engineered exposures outperform spontaneous ones on duration.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tea.22028>

Vivante & Vedder-Weiss (2025), "Science Triggers and Situational Interest in Everyday Family Life," J. Res. Sci. Teaching 62(7):1814-1834, DOI 10.1002/tea.22028, CC-BY: "most triggers were direct (87%) and undesigned (82%)... although designed triggers were relatively rare, they elicited longer situational interest than undesigned triggers" / "designed triggers were uncommon but produced longer stretches of situational interest." The 18% is the arithmetic complement of a genuinely dichotomous coding dimension ("whether the stimulus was designed or not"); the third-category residual ("unidentified") belongs to the direct/indirect dimension. LIMIT: N=1 family (24/7 audio/video plus GoPro), 397 events — a descriptive statistic from one family's corpus, not a population estimate, and the outcome is within-episode engagement duration, not maintained situational interest across occasions. Note the tension with the per-child-variability finding above: designed triggers beat undesigned ones on average even though no single trigger works for every child.

### 10. Session-length ceiling for this age is on the order of 10-17 minutes, not an hour. Observed activity-block length in real K-4 classrooms centres on ~10-17 min (range 6.7-39.7, median 12.8, IQR 10.6-17.1); each additional minute of block length lowered the log-odds of on-task behaviour by 0.0174, so a 10-minute activity yielded 1.42x the odds of on-task behaviour versus a 30-minute one, and the authors judge 30-minute blocks suboptimal at these grades.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED568936.pdf>, <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2016.04.003>

Godwin, Almeda, Seltman, Kai, Skerbetz, Baker & Fisher (2016), "Off-task Behavior in Elementary School Children," Learning and Instruction 44:128-143 — the ERIC file is the author-accepted manuscript of the peer-reviewed article (IES grants R305A110444, R305B090023; CMU + Teachers College). Verbatim: "The slope estimate is -0.0174 (95% CI [-0.0301, -0.0047]) log odds units per additional minute of activity length... the estimated OR for on task behavior is 1.42 times higher for the shorter instructional activity (i.e., 10 minutes) compared to the longer"; F(1,954)=7.04, p=.0081, controlling for gender, grade, SES, instructional format and school type. Distribution: "ranged from 6.7 to 39.7 minutes with a median duration of 12.8 minutes, 25%... less than 10.6 minutes and 25%... longer than 17.1 minutes." And: "long instructional activity durations (i.e., 30 minutes) may be suboptimal in elementary grades." Design: live BROMP observation, kappa .79-.84; Study 1 = 22 classrooms/128 sessions, Study 2 = 30 classrooms across 9 schools. LIMITS: observational, not experimental, so "is associated with lower" is more precise than "reduces"; activity duration was DERIVED (session length ÷ number of activity transitions), not stopwatch-timed; sample is K-4, which brackets but is not specific to 6-8, and the paper calls the grade-level effect "equivocal across studies"; the authors themselves hedge that "Further research is needed to provide educators with evidence-based guidelines on the optimal length."

### 11. Attention decline is detectable within minutes at this exact age, and game-style reward scaffolding does not abolish it. Children aged 5-7.9 (M=6.52) showed a significant vigilance decrement inside a ~6-minute task, and the decrement occurred despite continuous per-trial extrinsic reward (stars, stickers, an animated laughing mole) that appears to have kept baseline arousal steady.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9680391/>, <https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence10040107>

Benitez & Robison (2022), "Pupillometry as a Window into Young Children's Sustained Attention," Journal of Intelligence 10(4):107. Child-friendly whack-a-mole psychomotor vigilance task, Tobii X3-120, linear mixed-effects models; N=41, M age 6.52 (SD .77), range 5-7.9 — the only source here squarely inside the target band. RT slowing across trials b=4.02, SE=0.79, p<.001; task-evoked pupil change declined b=-0.0032, SE=0.0007, p<.001 while baseline pupil diameter did not (p=.47). Authors: "children did indeed show a vigilance decrement, even on a rather short task (~6 min)"; and on the reward, "the stars, stickers, and laughing mole may have allowed children to maintain optimal levels of arousal throughout the task." LIMITS: N=41 (recruitment target 82 missed due to COVID closures; correlational analyses "underpowered"), single lab, single task, MDPI venue, no replication cited. Reward was NOT experimentally manipulated (single condition), so the licensed reading is "decline occurred despite reward," not "reward has no effect." Critically, because 6 min WAS the whole task, the study shows a decrement is measurable WITHIN ~6 min — it cannot establish when decline begins or where the ceiling lies — and a low-stimulation vigilance paradigm is the format most likely to elicit rapid decrement, so extending it to interest-driven session-length norms is a separate inferential step. CONFLICT: this sits against a (refuted, 0-3) claim citing ~18 minutes as the normative attention span for first graders.

### 12. Social arrangement is a lever on attention independent of format: small-group/partner work and individual work produced the highest on-task rates and whole-group instruction the lowest (ORs 1.51-1.62 in Study 2; same direction but smaller in Study 1).
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED568936.pdf>, <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2016.04.003>

Godwin et al. (2016), Study 2: F(3,2169)=27.61, p<.0001. "Compared to all other instructional formats, the highest rates of on-task behavior occurred during individual and small-group instruction; all adj. ps <= .0001. The largest OR was 1.62 [1.30, 2.02] for small-group compared to whole-group instruction on the carpet, and the smallest OR was 1.51 [1.28, 1.77], for individual compared to whole-group instruction at desks... On-task behavior was least likely to occur during whole-group instruction." Individual vs small-group did not differ (OR 1.07 [0.86,1.33]); whole-group desk vs carpet did not differ (OR 1.00 [0.83,1.22]). Study 1: F(3,4803)=8.35, p<.0001, small-group OR 1.23 [1.11,1.37] vs whole-group carpet — same direction, smaller. PRECISION NOTES: in Study 1 the INDIVIDUAL format was not significantly different from whole-group (adj. ps >= .08); "highest" for individual is a Study 2 result only. The paper's "small-group" code "includes small-group work, partner work, and centers," which validates the partner framing. Observational design, so "changes attention" overstates the correlational ORs; sample is K-4, not isolated to 6-8.

### 13. Screen vs physical is the wrong cut: what predicts transfer is the activity's demand structure. Children aged 4-6 transferred a 3-disk Tower of Hanoi solution from an iPad app to the physical wooden puzzle, including in a condition with NO prior physical exposure, and physical practice conferred no measurable advantage over touchscreen practice on the final physical trial — whereas passive-observation imitation paradigms show transfer deficits.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5611486/>, <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01586>

Tarasuik, Demaria & Kaufman (2017), "Transfer of Problem Solving Skills from Touchscreen to 3D Model by 3- to 6-Year-Olds," Frontiers in Psychology 8:1586. Randomized to 3D-3D-3D-3D (n=17), 3D-2D-2D-3D (n=16), 2D-2D-2D-3D (n=16); N=49 retained, ages 3.1-6.5 (M=4.8); Krippendorff's alpha .988. "children 4 years and older can transfer learning from 2D to 3D, even without exposure to 3D prior to the 2D exposure." For over-4s: Total Moves trial effect F(1,33)=16.70, p<.001, NO condition effect (F=1.35) and no trial x condition interaction (F=0.62); older median-split group t(24)=-4.01, p<.001, d=0.80 vs younger t(23)=0.96, p=.35. Authors' own framing: "the limits of 'screen time' as a construct. 'Screen time' does not distinguish activities that involve active engagement from those that involve only passive viewing... not all screen time has the same learning value." IMPORTANT PROVENANCE CORRECTION: the frequently-quoted sentence "there was no evidence that practicing on the physical version conferred any advantages over practicing with the 2D version" appears in this paper's INTRODUCTION describing Huber et al. (2016), Computers in Human Behavior 56:56-64, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2015.11.010 — cite Huber for that; this paper independently replicates the null with its own data. Likewise the "app-to-toy-clock time-telling in 5-6 year-olds" leg is a citation to Wang et al. (2017), not data collected here, and the passive-imitation transfer deficit is cited video-deficit literature. So the demand-structure MODERATION was never tested in one design — it is an inference across literatures. OTHER LIMITS: 16-17 per cell, one Croatian day-care centre, one puzzle; the transfer inference rests partly on a null (modality equivalence plus within-subject improvement) rather than a positive transfer contrast; pairing "ages 4-6" with "n=49" overstates the ~35 children behind the 4+ analysis; sample max age 6.5 barely overlaps the 6-8 band (the "older" transferring subgroup averaged 5.6 yrs); and a LIVE EXPERIMENTER delivered instructions in every condition, so this cannot speak to unscaffolded solo screen use.

### 14. SKILL transfer across media does not imply INTEREST transfer, and a poorly maintained taster can backfire: in a multi-session digital math game (Number Navigation, n=212 fifth graders), situational interest was stable within sessions but declined across sessions, and children whose situational interest was triggered but NOT maintained showed a marked DECLINE in individual mathematics interest from pre- to post-test, while stable-high and stable-low groups showed no change.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jcal.12239>, <https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12239>

Rodriguez-Aflecht, Jaakkola, Pongsakdi, Hannula-Sormunen, Brezovszky & Lehtinen (2018), "The development of situational interest during a digital mathematics game," Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 34(3):259-268 (Crossref/Semantic Scholar verified; Academy of Finland award 131490; ~46-53 citations). Verbatim: "Growth mixture model analyses showed that in the whole sample situational interest was stable within sessions but decreased across sessions"; "Students whose situational interest trajectories were stable (either high or low) presented no changes in individual interest, yet the individual interest of students whose situational interest was triggered but not maintained markedly decreased from pre-test to post-test." Also bidirectional coupling: "Situational interest trajectories were predicted by pre-test individual interest. In turn, situational interest had an effect on post-test individual interest" — so measured in-medium engagement partly echoes pre-existing domain interest, a measurement hazard for taster instrumentation. LIMITS: fifth graders (~10-11 yrs), Finnish, single unreplicated study, one game. Trajectory classes are latent/observational with no non-game control, and class membership was itself predicted by pre-test interest, so regression to the mean is not excluded from the abstract alone — "can leave domain interest worse than before" is defensible, "poorly maintained tasters cause decline" is not. The whole-sample across-session decline masks real heterogeneity (three trajectories, including stable-high), so citing the average as uniform novelty decay slightly overstates the paper; the authors' own takeaway is "use game-based learning not because games are believed to be 'motivating'; rather, games with proven learning outcomes should be carefully selected." CONFLICT: this is the interest-side counterweight to the Tower-of-Hanoi skill-transfer finding above — skill crossed media, interest did not.

### 15. Video can match hands-on on knowledge outcomes only under heavy instructional scaffolding, and hands-on advantages on higher-order skills did not persist. In a fifth-grade quasi-experiment, video matched hands-on on scientific knowledge when clips were teacher-edited, task-relevant, preceded by a cognitive-conflict prompt and followed by argumentation; the hands-on advantage on modelling ability was significant immediately but gone one week later on a near-transfer task (F=0.125, p=0.725).
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s43031-024-00103-x>, <https://diser.springeropen.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s43031-024-00103-x>

Shao, Tang & Zhang (2024), "Video watching and hands-on experiments to learn science: what can each uniquely contribute?", Disciplinary and Interdisciplinary Science Education Research 6(17), DOI 10.1186/s43031-024-00103-x, gold OA CC-BY. Two intact fifth-grade classes, 101 students (50 hands-on / 51 video), same teacher, urban China, plus a one-week delayed open-ended near-transfer measure (48/44). Delayed effect: "the group main effect of modeling was not significant (F = 0.125, p = 0.725, eta2 = 0.001)"; authors' interpretation: "the improvement effect of hands-on experiments on modeling ability exists only when reviewing the simulation experiment itself and that the improvement in modeling ability is a long-term and lengthy process of literacy accumulation." Scaffolding conditionality: "Different from those in previous studies that have used video to attract attention, entertain and motivate students (Hobbs, 2006) rather than explaining scientific knowledge, in this study, both the hands-on experiment class and the video watching class adopted thinking teaching"; "the teacher used thinking-based inquiry teaching, which first triggered cognitive conflicts among students then required them to perform simulation experiments or watch videos with questions and, finally, actively construct concepts"; "students would carry out classroom arguments according to the evidence obtained"; videos were "searched for and downloaded by teachers... and edited according to teaching needs" and "finely edited videos with high task relevance should be used"; "Rich video content could lead to information overload and selective exposure... when the quantity of videos is too large or there is a lack of information gatekeepers"; "teachers should use videos as materials to support students' thinking rather than directly using videos as a substitute for teacher guidance." LIMITS: the immediate hands-on modelling advantage held in only ONE of two lessons (Body Movement, not Heart and Blood), and in that lesson the video group started significantly HIGHER at pretest, so the immediate effect is confounded with baseline/regression; 2 intact classes, 1 teacher, no student-level randomization; "equivalence" is a non-significant difference in an n~100 design with no formal equivalence testing; hands-on DID beat video on "perception and application of materials" and health-behaviour awareness; grade 5 (~10-11 yrs), and the paper itself notes "for students in the stage of visual thinking, tactile perception of real objects is necessary" — relevant since 6-8 year olds are further into that stage. A related claim that the hands-on advantage tracked the scientific FIDELITY of the physical model rather than the medium was refuted 0-3.

### 16. Real-world first-hand encounters trigger interest very readily but rarely reach individual interest inside a single session. In ~8 hours of analysed teacher-camera video of outdoor natural-science activities with 4-8-year-olds, 30 trigger episodes and 85 interest episodes were coded, distributed 44 Phase 1, 34 Phase 2, and only 7 Phase 3-4 — the authors attribute the scarcity to the time individual interest requires.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://www.pescholar.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/How-to-support-young-childrens-interest-development-during-exploratory-natural-science-activities-in-outdoor-environments.pdf>, <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2022.103687>

### 17. A momentary novelty reaction is observably distinct from domain-directed situational interest, and conversion is not automatic: in a documented 7-8-year-old case, attention stayed on the surprise (an insect found in a peer's hair) rather than the insect itself, the child ran off, and no situational interest formed. The authors argue conversion depends on the adult first meeting the child's affect about the discovery and only then bridging to the object.

### 18. ## Caveats
AGE COVERAGE IS THE DOMINANT WEAKNESS. Only three sources touch ages 6-8 directly: Benitez & Robison 2022 (N=41, M=6.52, single 6-min vigilance task), Skalstad & Munkebye 2022 (22 of 39 children aged 7-8, ~115 analysed minutes, no inter-rater reliability reported), and Tarasuik et al. 2017 (ages 3.1-6.5, transferring subgroup M=5.6, barely overlapping). Every quantitative dosage, format-comparison, and interest-transfer result comes from older or adult samples: Rotgans & Schmidt 2017 (upper primary ~10-11, Singapore), Rodriguez-Aflecht et al. 2018 (fifth graders, Finland), Shao et al. 2024 (fifth graders, China), Godwin et al. 2016 (K-4 pooled, grade effect \"equivocal\"), Fitzakerley et al. 2013 (grades 4-6), Palmer et al. (ADULT teacher-education students), Vivante & Vedder-Weiss 2025 (one family). Any per-exposure, session-length, or sequencing number applied to a 6-8 year old is extrapolation, and the direction of the age adjustment is not empirically pinned.

NO SOURCE COMPARED THE EIGHT FORMATS IN THE QUESTION. There is no study in this evidence base that puts short videos, digital games, hands-on making, multi-session projects, storybooks, live demonstrations, field experiences, and collaborative work into one design with a common interest outcome. The closest is a two-arm video-vs-hands-on comparison in fifth grade. Storybooks and read-alouds are entirely absent — no claim survived on them. Guided multi-session projects are represented only by the Rotgans four-problem course. So format rankings must be assembled across incommensurable studies, and the field's own originators say the triggering-then-maintaining process is still poorly understood.

NOTHING SURVIVED ON FINE-MOTOR OR READING-LEVEL CONSTRAINTS. The question asks what a 6-8 year old can do unaided; no verified claim addresses fine-motor capability or reading level. Worse, the one screen-to-physical transfer study explicitly notes a live experimenter delivered instructions in every condition, so it cannot speak to unassisted solo use — precisely the case a taster product needs.

SEVEN UNRESOLVED CONFLICTS. (1) Sustained attention at 6-7: ~18 min normative benchmark vs measurable decrement within ~6 min vs a 7-min observed maximum on-task stretch — the 18-min and 7-min claims were both refuted, leaving the ~6-min pupillometry result standing but from a low-stimulation task that maximizes decrement. (2) Whether one ~20-min hands-on session shifts cross-medium voluntary re-engagement — both the positive and the deflationary readings of that study were refuted, so treat single-session cross-medium carryover as unevidenced here. (3) Skill transfers across media (Tower of Hanoi) while interest does not (math game) — these are not actually contradictory, they concern different constructs, and the report should say so explicitly rather than presenting a screen verdict. (4) Designed triggers reliably beat undesigned ones on duration vs no trigger works dependably because effects hinge on learner readiness — reconcilable as average effect vs variance, but the n=1-family basis of the former matters. (5) Ordering of change: voluntary out-of-medium behaviour moving before career aspiration vs the reverse — both underlying claims were refuted, so the ordering is unknown. (6) Magnitude of a single live practitioner encounter: d=0.668 vs d=0.06-0.24 — the large-effect claim was refuted 0-3, so only the small effect stands. (7) Same field study yielding both \"single exposures almost never reach individual interest\" and \"a single encounter produced same-day spontaneous re-engagement\" — the latter was refuted 1-2.

CITATION HYGIENE ISSUES TO FIX BEFORE PUBLISHING. The \"no advantage for physical practice\" sentence commonly cited to Tarasuik et al. 2017 is that paper's summary of Huber et al. 2016 (DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2015.11.010) — cite Huber. The app-to-clock time-telling result belongs to Wang et al. 2017. Cite Renninger et al. by DOI (10.1016/j.lcsi.2018.11.007), not the ResearchGate mirror. Several key texts could not be opened for verbatim verification (ScienceDirect and Wiley return 403; the NTU and EUR repository PDFs return CAPTCHA/HTML), so the Rotgans Study 2 slope figures (beta=.18/.03) and the control-decay quote rest on publisher abstracts and the supplied quotes rather than a re-read of the Results tables. The Rotgans sample's \"mean age ~9.5-10\" is not attested in any reachable metadata. The Renninger STELAR deck is grey literature (a slide deck), acceptable only because it is first-party from the model's originator and corroborated in Renninger & Hidi (2021), Theory Into Practice.

MODAL OVERREACH IS PERVASIVE IN THIS LITERATURE'S SECONDARY USE. Several load-bearing prescriptions are the authors' hedged interpretations, not tested effects: \"the teacher has to be able to keep Jack focused\" (no counterfactual), \"30 minutes may be suboptimal\" (observational, derived durations, authors call for further research), \"support is a necessary condition\" (a phase description, not a tested necessity). Note also that the model itself counts \"instructional design (e.g., software)\" as valid support, so \"solo screen equals unsupported\" is contradicted by the source most often cited for it.

TIME SENSITIVITY IS LOW but the newest and most directly relevant work (Skalstad & Munkebye 2022, Shao et al. 2024, Vivante & Vedder-Weiss 2025) is recent and thinly replicated; the 2013 PLOS ONE and 2006 Educational Psychologist anchors are stable. Two absence-of-evidence notes: the search budget was exhausted before counter-searches could run on the math-game decline finding, so no contradicting source was located rather than none existing; and 16 claims were refuted by lens review, several of which (place-anchored re-engagement, spaced re-triggering necessity, out-of-medium behavioural spillover) would have been the most directly actionable design levers had they survived.

## Open questions
- Does interest triggered inside a medium transfer to the domain outside it for 6-8 year olds, and does the medium's aboutness matter? The evidence splits by construct — skill transferred app-to-physical for an isomorphic puzzle (ages 4-6), but game-triggered math interest that was not maintained tracked a DECLINE in domain interest (age 10-11). No study measured out-of-medium voluntary domain engagement (asking to do the thing again, in a different form, unprompted) after a format-controlled taster in the 6-8 band. This is the single highest-value gap: a rotation could be net-negative for children it triggers and then drops.
- What is the actual session-length and exposure-count envelope for 6-8 year olds in an INTEREST-driven (not vigilance, not instructional) activity? The only in-band attention datum is a 6-minute low-stimulation vigilance task where decline was detectable within the whole task duration — it cannot say where the ceiling is. The K-4 classroom norm (~10-17 min blocks, 10 min beating 30 min at OR 1.42) is observational and pooled across K-4. The one dosage benchmark (4 stimuli, 2x1h/week, 4 weeks) uses hour-long blocks that contradict both. Needed: a dose-response study varying block length (say 8 / 15 / 25 min) and exposure count (2 / 4 / 6) per domain with voluntary re-engagement as the outcome, in-band.
- Can the required Phase-1-to-Phase-2 support be delivered by instructional design alone, without an adult? Hidi & Renninger explicitly allow "instructional design (e.g., software)" as support, and independent re-engagement only becomes an indicator at Phase 3 — but every close-to-band study involved a live adult (the Tower of Hanoi experimenter gave instructions in all conditions; the outdoor study's whole finding is about teacher moves; the family study's mechanism is parental co-construction). For an unattended product this is the load-bearing unknown, and fine-motor and reading-level constraints on unaided 6-8 activity are entirely unaddressed by any surviving source.
- How do you distinguish a pre-interest novelty reaction from domain-directed situational interest in real time, cheaply, per child? Skalstad & Munkebye show the two are observably separable (attention on the surprise vs on the object) and that they diverge WITHIN the same episode for different children, and Renninger's indicators (frequency, depth, voluntary/independent re-engagement) give the outcome definition — but the Rodriguez-Aflecht finding that in-game situational interest is partly predicted by PRE-EXISTING domain interest means naive in-session engagement metrics (time-on-task, clicks) will partly re-measure prior interest rather than detect new interest. What observable, adult-free signal validly separates trigger, maintenance, and echo of prior interest at this age?
