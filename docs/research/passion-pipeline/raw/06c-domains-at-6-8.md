# Research: For children ages 6-8, which domains of serious pursuit are developmentally accessible enough to begin GENUINE talent development (real beginner practice and real first projects), not merely exposure — and what does authentic beginner work concretely look like in each? Cover at minimum: mathematics, programming / computational thinking, music performance and composition, visual art, chess and strategy games, engineering and building (LEGO/robotics/making), natural science and field observation, writing and storytelling, dance and movement, and second languages. For each domain give: the earliest evidence-supported entry age and why, what a real first practice session or first project looks like for a 6-8 year old, what tools and interfaces are actually usable at this age given reading level and fine-motor development, and the evidence on benefits and risks of early entry (including early specialization, burnout, and dropout evidence). Then: how many domains should a child sample before narrowing, over what timeframe, and what does the literature on early identification of domain-specific talent at ages 6-8 say about how reliable and stable such early signals are — do early indicators at 6-8 predict later domain attainment, and how much regression to the mean is there? Include evidence from gifted education (Talent Development Megamodel, SMPY, Renzulli), the sport early-vs-late specialization literature, and music/chess prodigy research.

_2026-07-25_

## Summary
The evidence supports a two-part answer. (1) Domain entry is genuinely staggered rather than uniform: mathematics is the best-evidenced domain for real (not exposure-level) beginner work at ages 5-8 — kindergarteners given a challenging geometry/measurement curriculum that positioned them as practicing mathematicians outperformed comparisons on both a standardized test (d=0.25) and an open-response performance assessment (d=2.68); Western-art-music systematic instruction conventionally starts at 5-7 (but 10-15 for self-taught popular genres, which is why the field's own framework refuses to bind developmental levels to chronological age at all); and movement/dance places a criterion-based readiness floor at roughly 6-7 (emotional maturity to follow directions plus competent balance/postural control), with formal technical dance training beginning at 7-8 — so ages 6-8 sit exactly at the play-to-real-practice transition in the physical domains. (2) Early identification of who is talented at 6-8 is close to worthless as a predictor: cognitive-ability rank order measured at age 7 or below does not reach even group-decision stability (r>.70) until age 16 (meta-analysis of 205 samples, 87,408 participants); ~60% of children in the top 3% of a highly reliable grade-3 achievement composite fall out within one year and 60-65% by grade 8, with single-domain subtests less stable than composites; and in sport, junior competitive performance explains only 2.2% of senior-performance variance, with the correlation falling to zero or slightly negative at ages 11-13 — the youngest ages sampled. On specialization, adult world-class attainment is associated with the opposite profile from early narrowing (more childhood multi-sport practice, later main-discipline start, fewer main-discipline hours, slower early progress), and consensus bodies find no evidence of benefit from pre-age-12 single-domain specialization in most sports while associating it with burnout, dropout, injury, shorter elite tenure, and less enjoyment. The literature therefore supports keeping 6-8 broad and treating early signals as current accomplishment rather than prediction — but it does not supply a validated number of domains to sample, and the verified evidence base covers only mathematics, music, dance/movement, sport, and (thinly) chess, with essentially nothing on programming, visual art, engineering/making, field science, writing, or second languages.

## Findings

### 1. Talent domains have structurally different onset points and distinct developmental trajectories rather than one universal developmental schedule: mathematical precocity is recognizable in the preschool years and certainly in the elementary grades, some performance domains (boy-soprano singing, gymnastics) also begin in childhood, while domains such as psychology and the social sciences cannot be seriously entered until high school, college, or after an advanced degree.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://www.apa.org/ed/schools/gifted/rethinking-giftedness.pdf>, <https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100611418056>, <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619895030>, <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13598139.2022.2139666>

Primary statement of the Talent Development Megamodel: Subotnik, Olszewski-Kubilius & Worrell (2011), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 12(1):3-54, doi 10.1177/1529100611418056 — an invited peer-reviewed integrative monograph in APS's flagship public-interest journal, funded by the James S. McDonnell Foundation. One of its four enumerated arguments, verbatim in the abstract: "different talent domains have different developmental trajectories that vary as to when they start, peak, and end." Quoted text: "precocity in mathematics can be recognized as early as the preschool years (and certainly in the elementary grades) whereas outstanding contributions in psychology do not typically occur until several years after completing an advanced degree... the serious study of some academic subjects, such as the social sciences, is not introduced until high school or college. Therefore, specialization can typically get underway only in college." Later megamodel papers (2021 Springer; 2022 Tandfonline) name mathematics, music, and gymnastics as domains where talent must be nurtured "beginning in preschool or the primary years," with identification starting pre-kindergarten. Calibration: this is a narrative/integrative review that ARGUES the differing-trajectory principle and illustrates it with domain examples — it does not report a dataset measuring onset ages across domains, so "empirically identifiable onset points" is a shade stronger than the source establishes. The psychology/social-science half is partly a statement about U.S. curriculum structure (institutional opportunity) rather than developmental capacity. The apa.org URL is not machine-fetchable (Incapsula bot block) and Sage returns 403; cite the DOI. Note: the same source elsewhere gives a narrower inventory (chess, music performance, subsets of mathematics as the prodigy domains, per Feldman's co-incidence model) — see caveats.

### 2. Mathematics is the strongest-evidenced case for genuine advanced (not exposure-level) work at or below ages 6-8: kindergarteners (5-6) given a challenging geometry and measurement curriculum (Project M2) that explicitly positioned them as practicing mathematicians significantly outperformed a comparison group on both a norm-referenced standardized test and an open-response performance assessment.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1122862>, <https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216671806>

Casa, Firmender, Gavin & Carroll (2017), "Kindergarteners' Achievement on Geometry and Measurement Units That Incorporate a Gifted Education Approach," Gifted Child Quarterly 61(1):52-72, NSF DRL0733189 — the flagship peer-reviewed NAGC journal; ERIC classifies it "Journal Article; Reports - Research." Quasi-experimental, intervention n=210 vs. comparison n=196 across four states (CT, KY, SC, TX), analyzed with hierarchical linear modeling. Authors' own conclusion: "kindergarteners can achieve at high levels of mathematical understanding when given the opportunity to learn from more challenging curriculum." The mechanism phrase "positioning students as practicing mathematicians" is the article's own language, and the outcome measure had performance-based, verbal, and written components — i.e. authentic beginner work in this domain concretely looks like open-ended geometry/measurement investigation plus oral and written mathematical explanation, not worksheet arithmetic. Caveats: quasi-experimental (licenses "outperformed," not causal language); participants were kindergarteners (~5-6), marginally below the 6-8 band; single study.

### 3. The gains from authentic mathematician-style work at this age appear mainly on curriculum-aligned performance/explanation tasks (d = 2.68) rather than on independent standardized tests (d = 0.25 on the ITBS Mathematics subscale) — so the transfer-relevant effect of authentic early domain work is modest, and the very large effect is the signature of measure-curriculum alignment.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1122862>, <https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986216671806>

Both effect sizes are in the source's own abstract, not a secondary gloss (verified via two independent fetches of the ERIC record with byte-consistent figures; Sage full text is paywalled, HTTP 403): ITBS Mathematics subscale p<0.01, d=0.25; researcher-developed open-response assessment (performance-based, verbal, written components) p<0.001, d=2.68. The order-of-magnitude gap between a researcher-built aligned measure and a norm-referenced test is the classic treatment-inherent-measure pattern; any report should lead with d=0.25 as the transfer number. Same population caveat (kindergarten, ~5-6) and single-study caveat as the preceding finding; ERIC confirms control/experimental groups with HLM but does not confirm random assignment.

### 4. For music there is no single evidence-supported entry age: systematic instruction typically begins at 5-7 for Western art music but 10-15 for self-taught popular-genre musicians, and the field's cross-domain framework therefore explicitly refuses to associate windows of chronological time with developmental levels at all.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619895030>, <https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/28316/7/Talent%20Development%20in%20Achievement%20Domains_AAM.pdf>

Preckel, Golle, Grabner, Jarvin, Kozbelt, Müllensiefen, Olszewski-Kubilius, Schneider, Subotnik, Vock & Worrell (2020), "Talent Development in Achievement Domains: A Psychological Framework for Within- and Cross-Domain Research," Perspectives on Psychological Science 15(3):691-722, doi 10.1177/1745691619895030, PMID 32196409. Quote verified verbatim in the green-OA accepted manuscript (Goldsmiths eprint 28316, manuscript ID PPS-19-108.R2, i.e. twice-revised peer review): "For musicians engaged in Western art music, the start of systematic music instruction is often between 5 and 7 years. However, for many self-taught musicians within the popular music genre, the start of systematic music learning can be much later, for example, between 10 and 15 years. Consequently, the TAD framework avoids any general association of windows of chronological time with developmental levels." Independently corroborated elsewhere in the same paper: "within TAD the levels of talent development are not defined in terms of chronological age, but in terms of positions on the developmental trajectory," with age-window links deferred to future research. Author list includes the Megamodel authors plus a music-psychology specialist (Müllensiefen), so the music passage is within declared expertise. Caveat: the 5-7 / 10-15 figures appear as the review's uncited expert summary at that passage — to use them as an empirical finding rather than as the framework's stated rationale, also cite the informal-learning literature (e.g. Green on popular musicians).

### 5. In the movement domain, readiness for formal structured training is criterion-based rather than age-based, with the threshold placed at approximately 6-7 years — emotional maturity to accept and follow directions plus competent balance and postural control — preceded from birth to 5-6 by exploratory and deliberate play developing fundamental motor skills; this gives a defensible developmental floor for real (not exposure-level) practice at ages 6-8.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://www.nsca.com/globalassets/about/position-statements/nsca_position_statement_long-term_athletic_development.pdf>

NSCA Official Position Stand: Lloyd, Cronin, Faigenbaum, Haff, Howard, Kraemer, Micheli, Myer & Oliver (2016), "NSCA Position Statement on Long-Term Athletic Development," J Strength Cond Res 30(6):1491-1509; 268 numbered references; authors include Boston Children's/Harvard (Micheli), Cincinnati Children's (Myer), Ohio State (Kraemer), Cardiff Met Youth Physical Development Unit. PDF downloaded and 19 pages of text extracted; quote verified verbatim under the heading "Starting Age": "Although there is not a single chronological age at which it is deemed acceptable for youth to formally start training, recent guidelines recommended that any child engaging in a form of resistance training is emotionally mature enough to accept and follow directions and possesses competent levels of balance and postural control (approximately 6-7 years of age) (134,141,189). However, children should engage in exploratory and deliberate play from early childhood (from birth to the age of 5-6 years) inclusive of activities designed to develop fundamental motor skills (138) and foundational levels of strength (e.g., gymnastics or similar bodyweight management activities) (143). If children are ready to engage in organized sports, they are ready to participate in developmentally appropriate strength and conditioning." Scope caveat: the 6-7 criterion is stated specifically for RESISTANCE TRAINING; generalizing it to "the movement domain" broadly (including dance) rests on the adjacent organized-sport-readiness sentence, and this document is not a dance-pedagogy authority. It is a consensus position stand (expert opinion tier), though each recommendation is anchored to numbered primary citations.

### 6. Dance uses an explicit staged age ladder in which formal technical training begins at 7-8 years (with no ballet barre before that age), preceded by creative play at 3-4 and 'beginning technique' (body parts, alignment, locomotor patterns) at 5-6 — placing ages 6-8 exactly at the transition from play/pre-technique into genuine technical practice.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://healthydancercanada.org/uploads/4/7/1/3/47130231/hdc_motordevelopment_rp_2021.pdf>

Healthy Dancer Canada / Dance Health Alliance of Canada resource paper (© 2021), Krasnow & Wilmerding — authors of "Motor Learning and Control for Dance" (Human Kinetics, 2015); HDC states its resources are peer-reviewed by field experts and updated biannually, and a sibling paper by the same pair is published as an IADMS resource. PDF downloaded (489,704 bytes, 8 pp., created 2021-09-17); quote verified verbatim on p.5 under "Implications for Technical Training": "3-4 years: Focus on creative play / 5-6 years: Beginning technique (body parts, alignment, locomotor) / 7-8 years: Technical training (no barre before this age) / 10-12 years: More skill acquisition...". The rationale is the perturbation-platform postural-control table on p.4 (4-6 years: "erratic muscle responses, high variability, inter-sensory integration"; 7-10 years: "similar to adults, slightly longer responses"), from which the authors reason "7-8 years old is an ideal time to challenge these mechanisms." Two important nuances: (a) the age ladder carries no inline citations — it is expert pedagogical inference (~level-5 evidence) from the empirical motor-control section, so it must not be upgraded to "the evidence establishes 7-8 as the entry age"; (b) the "no barre" rationale is the OPPOSITE of a capability gate — the concern is that physical support removes the balance challenge and makes dancers "too visually dependent," and teachers are told to "challenge young dancers by having them work away from the barre." The same document warns directly against talent-ID inference: "simply because a child demonstrates a skill early in development does not necessarily correlate with natural abilities in sports and dance."

### 7. Renzulli supplies an operational open-endedness test for distinguishing authentic talent-development work from exposure or drill: if an agreed-upon solution, existing right answer, or prescribed solving strategy exists, the activity is by definition not Type III enrichment but a teacher-assigned training exercise.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://gifted.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/961/2022/05/Anatomy-of-a-Type-III-Enrichment-Project.pdf>

Joseph S. Renzulli, "Anatomy of a Type III Enrichment Project," University of Connecticut, hosted on gifted.uconn.edu (Renzulli Center, Neag School) — the originator of the Enrichment Triad Model describing his own construct, i.e. primary authority for a definitional claim. The PDF had no text layer; the verifier decoded its Flate content streams and subset-font ToUnicode CMaps locally to recover all 3 pages. Quote verbatim (including the original's grammatical slip): "2. Open-endedness of the problem. A second essential element of real problems is that they do not have a preexisting or unique solutions for the groups or individuals addressing the problem. If an agreed-upon solution or already existing right answer is present, or if a prescribed strategy for solving the problem exists, then it is more appropriately classified as a teacher-assigned training exercise." Type III is defined on p.1 as "individual and small group investigations of real problems" with four criteria: personalization; open-endedness; authentic methodology and advanced content; work intended for authentic audiences — confirming the quote is not cherry-picked from a contrary context. Anchored in peer-reviewed antecedents it cites: Renzulli (1982), Gifted Child Quarterly 26(4):147-156; Reis & Peters (2020), Gifted Education International 37(2):109-141. Caveats: the document is an undated practitioner white paper, not peer-reviewed itself (adequate for a definitional claim, too weak for an outcome claim); Renzulli contrasts real-problem work with a "teacher-assigned training exercise," not with "exposure," so using this as an exposure-vs-talent-development boundary is the report's extension of his distinction. A dissenting verifier noted the document's worked exemplar is a middle-school group (~11-14) with no 6-8 guidance — see caveats.

### 8. Ages 6-8 sit in the steepest part of the instability curve for ability measurement: rank-order stability of cognitive ability follows a negative-exponential age trend, and scores obtained at age 7 or below do not reach even group-decision-usable stability (r > .70) until age 16, whereas age-11 measures stay above .80.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://labs.la.utexas.edu/tucker-drob/files/2024/02/Breit-et-al.-2024-Stability-of-Cognitive-Abilities.pdf>, <https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000425>

Breit, Scherrer, Tucker-Drob & Preckel (2024), "The Stability of Cognitive Abilities: A Meta-Analytic Review of Longitudinal Studies," Psychological Bulletin, doi 10.1037/bul0000425 — APA's flagship quantitative-review journal; author copy on the co-author's UT-Austin lab page carrying the APA copyright footer (42 pp., ~287 refs). 205 longitudinal studies, 87,408 participants, 1,288 test-retest correlations (all three figures verbatim from the abstract). Functional form verbatim: "best described by a negative exponential function, with low stability in preschool children, rapid increases in stability in childhood, and consistently high stability from late adolescence to late adulthood... This same functional form continued to best describe age trends in stability after adjusting for test reliability." Quote verified from the Discussion, p.30, "Implications for Applied Assessment": "Measurements between the ages of 8 and 10 are still somewhat relevant (i.e., stability >.70), whereas measurements from age 7 or below are not stable above .70 until age 16." The .70/.80 thresholds are correctly attributed by the paper to Watkins & Smith (2013) (.70 for group decisions, .80 for individual diagnostic decisions) and self-flagged as "chosen somewhat arbitrarily." Figure 8 is coarser than the prose: r>.70 spans at most 5 years from age 6, 12 years from age 9, 18 years from age 11; r=.80 "is not obtained in children younger than 6" and holds ~2 years for 8-year-olds. Reference anchor: a 20-year-old at a 5-year interval, rho = .76. CRITICAL SCOPE LIMIT: the construct is general cognitive ability (g, fluid reasoning, comprehension-knowledge, working memory, processing speed), NOT domain-specific talent; the paper notes effortful-processing measures (working memory, learning efficiency) are markedly less stable "especially in young children." Applying this to music/chess/math talent signals is an inference beyond what was measured.

### 9. Early top-percentile identification fails at a high rate within a single year even with near-perfect test reliability, and single-domain signals are less stable than multi-domain composites: only ~40% of grade-3 top-3% scorers on a composite (K-R 20 = .98; grade 3-to-4 r = .91) were still top-3% one year later, versus ~50% fallout for the Reading, Language, and Mathematics subtests taken separately.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746292.pdf>

Lohman, D. F., & Korb, K. A. (2006), "Gifted Today but Not Tomorrow? Longitudinal Changes in Ability and Achievement During Elementary School," Journal for the Education of the Gifted 29(4):451-484 (Prufrock Press). Lohman is a CogAT co-author and a primary authority on this identification literature. Verifiers extracted the PDF text locally (WebFetch could not parse it). Verbatim, p.464: "Only about 40% of the students who had composite scores in the top 3% in third grade also scored in the top 3% in fourth grade. Note that this occurs in spite of the fact that Composite ITBS scores are highly reliable (K-R 20 rxx' = .98) and show substantial stability across years (r = .91 for grade 3 to grade 4)." And: "As would be expected, regression effects were greater for the Reading, Language, and Mathematics subtest scores than for the Composite score that combines them. For each of these content scores, the fallout was approximately 50% in the first year." Figure 2 plots subtests below Composite at every grade comparison; a second independent dataset (ITBS Form K + CogAT Form 5, N=2,363) shows the same steep first-interval fallout. Caveats: percentages are MODEL-BASED estimates from Martin (1985) — an unpublished Iowa doctoral dissertation, n=6,321 tested annually grades 3-8 — assuming bivariate normality, not observed cross-tabulations. One verifier reproduced the paper's own model (top-3% cut z=1.8808) and got 45.3% overlap at r=.80 (matching the paper's published 45%) and 62.4% at r=.91, i.e. ~38% composite fallout — so the direction holds while the paper's "~40%" prose sentence appears mis-scoped. The interval is grade 3 to 4 (~ages 8-10), at or just above the 6-8 band. "Identification failure" is a gloss; the authors call it "fallout" from regression to the mean and measurement error. Generalizing "single-domain" beyond ITBS academic subtests to music/chess/sport signals exceeds the source.

### 10. The instability is not merely first-year measurement error: regression continues at a slower rate across grades, so by grade 8 only 35-40% of children who scored in the top 3% at grade 3 would still score in that range.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746292.pdf>

Same peer-reviewed source (Lohman & Korb 2006, JEG 29(4):451-484); quote verified verbatim from extracted PDF text at the Figure 2 discussion (local artifacts /tmp/ej746292.pdf and /tmp/ej746292.txt, passage ~char offset 31,000): "As the figure shows, however, regression continues at a slower rate across grades. This means that regression effects reflect more than errors of measurement. By eighth grade, the correlations indicate that only 35-40% of those who scored in the top 3% at grade 3 would still score in that range." Empirical basis stated immediately prior: Martin (1985) longitudinal ITBS analysis of 6,321 students tested every year from third to eighth grade, reanalyzed by Lohman & Korb. Both halves of the claim are the authors' own statements, not inference. Caveats: model-based estimates explicitly assuming bivariate normality, not counted retention; the underlying correlation matrix is secondary (Martin 1985, reweighted to approximate Iowa grade-5 composite distributions). The source attributes the single largest drop (grade 3-4) "largely" to measurement error while concluding the continued slower decline exceeds it — consistent with the claim.

### 11. In sport — the only domain with large-scale quantitative evidence on this question — early competitive performance has near-zero predictive value for adult attainment, and predictive value declines with younger assessment age to zero or slightly negative at pre-adolescence: pooled r = 0.148 (2.2% of reliable variance) across 129 effect sizes from 13,392 athletes, with r = 0.215 at ~17-19 years, 0.128 (Junior B), -0.024 (Junior C), and -0.052 at ~11-13 years. The meta-analytic authors conclude this raises serious questions about using junior performance for talent selection, since such programs cannot be confident they are selecting the highest future senior performers.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10799111/>, <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01906-0>

Barth, Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick (2024), "Quantifying the Extent to Which Junior Performance Predicts Senior Performance in Olympic Sports: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis," Sports Medicine 54(1):95-104, PMID 37676619, PMCID PMC10799111 (open access). PRISMA 2020-guided with an OSF-registered PRISMA-P protocol, eight databases searched Jan-Apr 2022, MMAT quality appraisal, three-level robust variance estimation; 21 reports (2006-2021), 21 Olympic sport types, 61.9% male, 72.1% international-level, mean senior age 25.7 yr; 7 unpublished reports (k=23) included alongside 14 published (k=106) to mitigate publication bias. Every number matches Table 2 and the abstract exactly, including pooled 95% CI 0.114-0.182 and r̄²=0.022, and per-category CIs (A: .174-.257, k=62; B: .100-.156, k=45; C: -.106 to .057, p=.515, k=13; D: -.101 to -.002, p=.045, k=9). Verbatim: "junior performance explained only 2.2% of the reliable variance in senior performance"; "the younger the junior age category, the lower the correlation between junior and senior performance"; "The results suggest that junior performance has very little, if any, predictive value for senior performance"; "raises serious questions about the use of junior performance for talent selection purposes"; and that selection programs "cannot be confident they are selecting the highest future senior performers." Between-category contrasts A-B, A-C, A-D, B-C, B-D all p<0.001. Calibration: (a) C vs. D did not differ significantly (F=0.042, p=0.839) and Junior C's CI spans zero, so the bottom two steps are statistically indistinguishable rather than a demonstrated further decline, and age effects explain only 0-4.6% of variance; (b) the authors "raise serious questions" and say the practice "may be misguided" — they do not prohibit, so "should not be used" hardens their hedge; (c) THE YOUNGEST CATEGORY SAMPLED IS 11-13 YEARS, so ages 6-8 are unsampled and any extension is extrapolation (the age trend runs in the direction the extrapolation needs); (d) correlational, male- and Western-European-over-represented, with survivorship gaps (juniors who dropped out; seniors who never competed as juniors) and truncated performance range; (e) Olympic-sport performance, not academic, musical, or chess talent.

### 12. Adult world-class attainment is associated with the opposite developmental profile from early specialization — more childhood/adolescent multi-sport practice, later main-discipline start, FEWER accumulated main-discipline hours, and slower early progress relative to national-class athletes — while the early-advantage pattern (earlier start, more main-sport practice, faster early progress) holds only among junior/youth performers.
**Confidence:** high
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620974772>, <https://www.nsca.com/globalassets/about/position-statements/nsca_position_statement_long-term_athletic_development.pdf>

Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick (2022), "What Makes a Champion? Early Multidisciplinary Practice, Not Early Specialization, Predicts World-Class Performance," Perspectives on Psychological Science 17(1):6-29 (Epub 2021-07-14; PMID 34260336; doi 10.1177/1745691620974772), verified via Semantic Scholar and PubMed, which lists publication type "Meta-Analysis" and keywords "early specialization; meta-analysis; performance; practice; skill acquisition." 51 international reports, 477 effect sizes, 6,096 athletes including 772 world-class; ~119 citations. The abstract's findings list matches each element of the claim, including that adult world-class athletes "initially progressed more slowly," and that the reverse pattern held among youth/junior athletes. In-lens caveat: the design is cross-sectional group comparison (world-class vs. national-class cohorts), so "athletes who specialized earlier experienced less success as they aged" is inferred from between-group contrasts rather than a directly tracked within-athlete trajectory; pair with longitudinal junior-to-senior transition studies for a strong developmental statement. Independently, the NSCA position stand's Section 4 ("Long-Term Athletic Development Pathways Should Encourage an Early Sport Sampling Approach") links early specialization to injury risk, motor-skill blunting, lower later performance, and dropout across ~20 cited references.

### 13. Later rather than earlier specialization is associated with higher adult performance even holding sport-specific deliberate practice constant — successful senior elites sampled more organized involvement in other sports while not differing in sport-specific deliberate practice — and, as of the 2020 cross-domain review, no equivalent early-vs-late specialization evidence exists for any non-sport achievement domain.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619895030>, <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620974772>

Preckel et al. (2020), Perspectives on Psychological Science 15(3):691-722, doi 10.1177/1745691619895030, PMID 32196409 — peer-reviewed invited cross-domain review by 11 senior talent-development researchers including the Megamodel authors. Verbatim: "research from the domain of sports suggests that in senior elite sports, highly successful athletes were characterized by later specialization and greater amounts of organized involvement in other sports than less successful athletes, although both groups did not differ on the amount of sport-specific deliberate practice (Güllich, 2014, 2017, 2018)... To conclude, for the domain of sports and independent of the general amount of deliberate practice, later specialization seems to be associated with higher adult performance compared to early specialization; comparable research for other achievement domains is missing." Traceable to primary matched-pairs studies (Güllich 2014; 2017 "International medallists' and non-medallists' developmental sport activities"; 2018 J Sports Sci 36(19):2256-64) that equate groups on sport-specific practice, and corroborated at higher tier by Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick (2022). Caveats: retrospective/quasi-experimental — "holding practice constant" means group equivalence on a retrospectively recalled variable, so no causal upgrade; and the negative half is time-bounded — word it "as of Preckel et al. (2020)" rather than as a standing 2026 fact. This is the single most important limitation for the whole research question: the entire early-vs-late specialization evidence base is a sport literature being borrowed.

### 14. Early single-sport specialization has an agreed operational definition — intensive organized training/competition >8 months per year, to the exclusion of other sports with limited free play, in prepubertal (roughly age 12 or younger) children — so year-round exclusive single-domain training at ages 6-8 falls squarely inside the risk-flagged category; the expert panel found no evidence that young children benefit from it in the majority of sports, and specializing before 12 is associated with increased burnout and dropout and decreased athletic development over time.
**Confidence:** medium
**Sources:** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4853833/>, <https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967116644241>

LaPrade, Agel, et al. (2016), "AOSSM Early Sport Specialization Consensus Statement," Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine 4(4):2325967116644241, PMID 27169132 — 17-member panel (Baker, Côté, Jayanthi, Myer, Hewett, Brenner, Engebretsen, Hainline, Gould, Kocher) from a think tank held 2 Oct 2015, Rosemont IL. The three criteria are verbatim and in order: "(1) Participation in intensive training and/or competition in organized sports greater than 8 months per year (essentially year round); (2) Participation in 1 sport to the exclusion of participation in other sports (limited free play overall); (3) Involving prepubertal (seventh grade or roughly age 12 years) children." Introduced as "the following 3 criteria" — "joint" is a fair gloss, not literal. Also verbatim: "there is no evidence that young children will benefit from early sport specialization in the majority of sports" and "youth specialization before the age of 12 years is associated with increased burnout and dropout rates and decreased athletic development over time." ## Caveats
SEVERE DOMAIN-COVERAGE GAP. The question asks about ten domains; the verified evidence base covers only mathematics (one quasi-experiment, kindergarten), music (a framework-level entry-age statement plus one small violin replication), dance/movement (two expert-opinion guidance documents), sport (the bulk of the evidence, including all the meta-analyses), and chess (two secondary citations inside a meta-analysis discussion). NOTHING survived verification for programming/computational thinking, visual art, engineering/building (LEGO/robotics/making), natural science and field observation, writing and storytelling, or second languages. Any downstream statement about entry ages, first projects, tools, or risks in those six domains is unsourced by this round and must be labeled as such or researched separately. The question's request for concrete tool/interface recommendations given reading level and fine-motor development is likewise not addressed by any surviving claim.

CROSS-DOMAIN BORROWING IS THE CENTRAL WEAKNESS. Nearly all quantitative evidence on specialization, burnout, dropout, and predictive validity comes from sport, and Preckel et al. (2020) state explicitly that \"comparable research for other achievement domains is missing.\" The Barth et al. (2024) meta-analysis's youngest sampled age category is 11-13, so applying it to 6-8 is extrapolation (though the age trend runs in the direction the extrapolation needs). The Breit et al. (2024) stability meta-analysis measures general cognitive ability, not domain-specific talent, and notes effortful-processing measures are less stable in young children. The Lohman & Korb regression figures are ITBS academic achievement at grades 3-8. Do not present sport-derived risk figures or ability-test stability coefficients as direct evidence about 6-8-year-old music, chess, or art talent.

EVIDENCE-GRADE MIX. Three load-bearing documents are expert consensus/position statements: AOSSM 2016 self-describes as \"Think tank, roundtable discussion\" (Level 5; no search strategy or evidence grading; some reliance on personal communication and unpublished data), the NSCA 2016 position stand, and the Healthy Dancer Canada resource paper whose 7-8 age ladder carries no inline citations. One (SMPY) is a funding solicitation, not peer-reviewed. One key stability finding (Lohman & Korb) rests on model-based bivariate-normal estimates from an unpublished 1985 dissertation, and a verifier found the paper's own \"~40%\" prose sentence internally inconsistent with its reported r=.91 (recomputation gives ~38% fallout — directionally the same). Two verifiers could not fetch primary text directly: the APA-hosted Rethinking Giftedness PDF is bot-blocked and Sage returns 403 for several DOIs, so cite DOIs rather than the apa.org and ResearchGate URLs.

UNRESOLVED CONFLICTS. (a) Early-start advantage: the chess literature (Gobet & Campitelli 2007; Howard 2012, via Macnamara et al. 2014) finds earlier start predicts higher rating net of practice, while the violin replication finds no start-age difference between attainment groups and the sport meta-analyses find LATER main-discipline start associated with world-class attainment. These may be reconcilable — domain differences; survivorship in rating databases; \"age of serious entry\" and \"exclusive early specialization\" are different constructs — but present the tension rather than picking a side. (b) Which domains are early-accessible: the same Megamodel source gives two incompatible inventories, one including gymnastics and boy-soprano singing alongside math, another restricting children's physical/conceptual reach to chess, music performance, and subsets of mathematics (Feldman's co-incidence model); the narrower inventory was refuted 0-3, so the broader reading stands, but the inconsistency is real. (c) Authentic work at 6-8: Renzulli's Type III criteria require learner-selected topic AND method and his canonical exemplar is a middle-school group, which sits awkwardly against the claim that genuinely creative mathematical work is available in the early school years. Both the \"learner-selected method is necessary\" objection (1-2) and the \"no 6-8 guidance exists\" objection (0-3) failed verification, so the conflict is soft — but Project M2 was adult-designed curriculum, not child-initiated inquiry, and that distinction should not be blurred. (d) Dance/movement entry age: 7-8 (HDC ladder) vs. 8+ for ballet technique (IADMS-attributed claim, failed 1-2) vs. ~6-7 for structured resistance/S&C readiness (NSCA). The sub-domains differ, so these partly talk past each other.

REFUTED MATERIAL THAT MAY LOOK PLAUSIBLE — do not reuse: the 21-26% music/games, 18% sports, 4% education deliberate-practice variance figures (0-3); the retrospective Olympian multi-sport statistics including 11.4 vs 10.2 years and 17% single-sport NCAA D-I women (0-3); the 6-12 sampling / 13-15 narrowing / 16+ investment stage model (0-3); the \"3+ sports between 11-15\" breadth-dose finding (0-3); the multi-sport gross-motor-coordination result (0-3); the \"pointe after 16 without career handicap\" dance datum (0-3); the Renzulli middle-school-exemplar objection (0-3); the retest-eligibility-every-1-3-years recommendation (1-2); the neurological (visual/vestibular/proprioceptive integration) explanation for the 7-8 dance threshold (1-2); and the general \"early identification has poor predictive validity because few identified children become eminent adults\" framing (0-3) — note the last is refuted AS SOURCED even though the regression-to-the-mean and junior-senior meta-analytic findings support a similar conclusion on much firmer ground.

TIME-SENSITIVITY AND ATTRIBUTION HYGIENE. Preckel et al.'s \"no comparable research in other domains\" is accurate as of 2020 and should be dated. AOSSM (2016) and NSCA (2016) are a decade old; the Healthy Dancer Canada paper is updated biannually so a newer version may exist. Cite Breit et al. as 2024 (one internal table self-labels it 2023, a pre-publication artifact). Several sources were mis-tagged \"primary\" when they are consensus statements or narrative reviews (NSCA, AOSSM, Jayanthi); two meta-analyses were tagged primary, which understates rather than overstates their strength.

## Open questions
- What is the entry-age and authentic-first-project evidence for the six uncovered domains — programming/computational thinking, visual art, engineering/making (LEGO/robotics), natural science and field observation, writing and storytelling, and second languages? Second-language acquisition has a large, well-developed critical-period literature and early computing has a substantial K-2 computational-thinking literature (ScratchJr, Bee-Bot, unplugged CS); their absence here is a gap in this round, not in the field, and a targeted second round should close it.
- Does the sport early-vs-late specialization finding transfer to cognitive, artistic, and craft domains at all? Preckel et al. (2020) say the comparable research simply does not exist. The mechanisms that plausibly drive the sport result — physical maturation timing, overuse injury, physical burnout, motor-skill transfer between sports — are partly absent or differently structured in math, chess, music, and writing, so the transfer is an open empirical question, not a safe default.
- How should the chess early-start finding (earlier start predicts higher rating net of practice hours) be reconciled with the violin null and the sport late-start advantage? Candidate explanations to test: sensitive-period effects genuinely differ by domain (pattern-recognition/chunking domains vs. physically maturing domains); survivorship bias in chess rating databases excludes early starters who quit; and 'age of serious entry' is a different construct from 'exclusive year-round specialization,' so both findings can be true at once. Resolving this determines whether starting a 6-8-year-old seriously in a chess-like domain is a real advantage or a selection artifact.
- What are the concrete tool, interface, and reading-level constraints for authentic beginner work at 6-8 — text-free or icon-based interfaces, manipulatives vs. screens, fine-motor demands (pencil grip, instrument size, mouse vs. touch), and attention-span-appropriate session length? No surviving claim addresses this, yet it is decisive for whether a 'real first project' is actually executable at this age rather than adult-scaffolded.
- What are the appropriate NON-predictive uses of early signals at 6-8, given that prediction is demonstrably unreliable? The evidence supports treating a 6-8 signal as current accomplishment plus an indication of what to offer next, rather than as selection — but no surviving claim specifies how to operationalize that (e.g. re-testing cadence, opportunity-to-learn-based rather than score-based placement, or interest/engagement measures as the tracked variable instead of performance).
