A six-year-old reads ~600 words and hears ~4,000

Wolf's novice-reader stage puts a typical 6–7-year-old at roughly 600 words readable against 4,000 understood when heard. The map below is the built one: cabin identity carried by text alone. Find the clickable thing

This is the memo's central finding made playable. Feature search, where the target is the only thing with some property, is mature by age 6. Conjunction search, where the target is defined by a combination, is still developing at 6–7 and is where distractors bite. A painterly room full of props is a conjunction display. Time yourself in each mode, then remember you are an adult with a fully developed serial search strategy.

The number that matters is the wrong-click count, not the clock. A wrong click on scenery is a child probing a thing that does nothing. In the engine that is not neutral: per memo 06 §8.4 P2, an option that was surfaced and not engaged is scored as a decline. So a child who cannot find the hotspot produces evidence that they rejected the topic. Every miss you just logged is a potential sign-inversion in the interest model, which is why this is a measurement bug rather than a polish item.

Credit where it is due. MapScreen.css already does this correctly on the map: the playable cabin carries animation: node-breathe plus an ember glow, i.e. motion and luminance exclusive to the interactable. D2 is mostly a request to keep that and extend it into the cabin interiors, not to invent it.

Sources. Feature search peaks as early as age 6 with little change into adolescence; conjunction search improves through childhood, and 6–7-year-olds performed worse than adults (Donnelly et al. 2007). 6–8-year-olds are susceptible to distractors in conjunction but not feature search. Foveal crowding is elevated in children (maturity ~5–9), so spacing matters alongside size. Colour is a weaker channel at this age than luminance or motion, and is further degraded by irrelevant motion, which is why the recommended marker is motion plus luminance rather than a hue.

Ambient motion is bought out of the hotspot budget

The PRD asks for a cursor-driven parallax so the frame "breathes", plus drifting dust motes. Motion is the strongest involuntary attention cue at this age, which is exactly why it is the best hotspot marker we have, and exactly why spending it on ambience is subtractive. Turn ambience on and try to keep track of the three interactable props.

The sign flips, and that is the point. To an adult reviewing a screenshot, ambient motion reads as "the room is alive". To a 6-year-old's attentional system it reads as noise in the one channel the hotspots need. In eye-tracking with 4–5-year-olds, low-relevance animation lowered comprehension versus static, and children looked less at the relevant elements and more at the irrelevant ones. High-relevance animation was merely equal to static, so ambience has no upside to trade against.

This is the one recommendation in the memo that removes work. The reduced-motion code path already renders a still frame, so it is a default change, not new engineering.

Sources. Motion is what attracts children's gaze in illustrations (eye-tracking, 4–6). Low-relevant animation lowered picture-book comprehension and redirected gaze (eye-tracking, 4–5, three conditions). Rey (2012), 39 studies: seductive details harmed retention d = 0.30 and transfer d = 0.48. Contextual animation added for interest in 9–11-year-olds produced another null. Limit: both eye-tracking studies are below band and measure story comprehension rather than hotspot discovery. The extrapolation to parallax versus hotspots is the memo's, and it is testable in our own build.

Point-and-click accuracy by target size and age. Hourcade, Bederson, Druin & Guimbretière, Accuracy, Target Reentry and Fitts' Law Performance of Preschool Children Using Mice, UMD HCIL. Age F(2,36) = 20.744, p < .001; target size F(2,35) = 34.924, p < .001; distance to target had no effect. The authors: "to achieve the same level of accuracy as adults at 16 pixels, 5 year olds require 32 pixels, and 4 year olds 64 pixels". And 64 px beat 32 px significantly for children while giving adults nothing.

The finding is not that the token is too small. It is that the right token exists and is unreachable. contract.css already defines [data-mood="child"] --control-min: 56px and a .u-primary-target at 76 px, under a comment citing the child-UX literature. Both are set by nobody (they appear in the repo only at their own declaration sites), and passion/apps/mvp-jul24 never imports @gt100k/design-tokens at all. So the child surface renders in adult mode: 38 px cabin nodes, 15.2 px labels, 10.9 px badges, against the contract's own "never below 16px for any user".

Smallest useful PR: import the token package and set data-mood="child". Two lines, no art, no audio, and every control goes 44 → 56 px with all body type to ≥16 px at once.

Which band is this? The sizes above are argued for 6–8, the band the PRD's evidence and the discovery-surface doc's rule 6 concern. But mvp-jul24's own PROJECT.md targets 9–12, where NN/g rates fine motor "well developed". So the 38 px node is a much smaller problem for a competent 11-year-old than this panel implies. What holds at any age: the 10.9 px badge and 15.2 px label break the token contract's own "never below 16px for any user" rule, and the token-adoption gap is a plumbing defect regardless of band. See the memo's §2.7 note.

And the platform choice compounds it. DISCOVERY-APP-PRD.md:258 targets school Windows machines, chosen for GPU headroom, which silently also chose mouse/trackpad over touch, the harder modality at this age. Hourcade's numbers above are mouse numbers, so they are the applicable ones. Honest limit: Hourcade tested 4–5-year-olds and the paper's own opening notes they had not seen the problem in children 7 and older. So read 64 px as "the floor is capability-dependent and far above 44 px at the bottom of our band", not as a measured 6–8 requirement.

Show it once, animated. Don't explain it, and don't make the tutorial a game.

A within-subjects study of a tablet counting game (n = 32) compared a baseline, an animated demonstration, and an interactive demonstration. The animated one beat both, which is why the paper is titled "When Less Can Be More". Added tutorial interactivity costs load without buying comprehension. Children under ~8–9 also rely predominantly on visual feedback to correct movement.

An undiscovered affordance is identical to an absent one. NN/g's sharpest negative case: a PBS Kids game offered a click-only alternative to a hard drag, and "none of the kids in our study discovered that function." Flip the fallback switch above: "available only" is the shape our A5 accessibility mirror is currently specified in.
