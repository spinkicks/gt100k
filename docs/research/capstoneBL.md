## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree



### Category 1: Reading the child

#### Subcategory 1.1: What a child picks versus what they come back to

- **Source: Quested et al. (2013), youth-sport motivation across five countries**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - N ≈ 8,000 youth players, mean age 11.56, five countries.
      - The free-choice "this is my choice" item was endorsed by more than 88% of children in every country and was dropped for non-discrimination.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2013_Quested_et_al.pdf

- **Source: Hidi & Renninger (2006); Harackiewicz et al. (2008); O'Keefe, Dweck & Walton (2018)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Hidi & Renninger (2006), four-phase model of interest development: the later phases are defined by self-generated re-engagement rather than by enjoyment.
      - Harackiewicz et al. (2008), N = 858: situational interest measured during a course predicted subsequent course choice across 7 semesters. Enjoyment of the lectures did not.
      - O'Keefe, Dweck & Walton (2018): students holding a "fixed" theory of interest, that a passion is found rather than developed, disengage when the material becomes difficult.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4 ; https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.1.105 ; https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618780643

- **Source: Mabbe et al. (2018); Patall et al. (2008); Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Skjesol et al. (2025); Childhood-and-Beyond cohort (2013)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Mabbe et al. (2018, n=110, mean age 10.71): competence satisfaction predicted challenge-seeking (b = .31, p < .001) while autonomy satisfaction did not (b = .01, ns); competence manipulation partial η² = .65 on competence satisfaction and .33 on intrinsic motivation, vs .07–.08 for the autonomy manipulation.
      - Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999): positive verbal reward/feedback effect on free-choice intrinsic motivation d = 0.11 (ns) for children vs d = 0.43 for college students; age difference Qb(1) = 5.14, p < .02.
      - Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008): choice raises intrinsic motivation d = 0.30 overall and d = 0.51 for primary-school students; a 2019 experiment (N=126, mean age 10.8) found the choice effect mediated *jointly* by autonomy and competence.
      - Skjesol et al. (2025, RCT n=175, ages 9–12): an 11-week competence-oriented soccer program raised neither perceived competence nor interest/value; interest/enjoyment declined significantly in both arms.
      - Childhood-and-Beyond (2013): child perceived competence predicted the *amount* of later participation, not *whether* the child continued; only parental influence predicted continuation.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2018_MabbeSoenensVansteenkiste_ExperimentalChildPsych.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint7040101

- **Source: Habgood & Ainsworth (2011); Rotgans & Schmidt (2017)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Habgood & Ainsworth (2011), n=16, ~age 10: free-choice play totalled 75.7 minutes for the intrinsically integrated version against 10.28 minutes for the extrinsic version.
      - The same work at ages 7–8 (n=58) found that only the *delayed* test discriminated between versions (partial η² = .24). In-session engagement did not.
      - Rotgans & Schmidt (2017): four situational-interest triggers across four weeks produced a treatment interest slope of +.03 against a control slope of −.03. Untriggered interest actively declines.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2010.508029 ; https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2017.02.003

- **Source: Actual versus perceived motor competence (PubMed 29016237)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - N = 201, ages 6–9: actual motor competence explained 9–30% of activity variance against 0–5% for perceived competence.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29016237/

   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100619832930

#### Subcategory 1.3: Why behaviour cannot name a motivational state

- **Source: Guay, Vallerand & Blanchard (2000); Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991); Fredricks, Blumenfeld & Paris (2004); Skinner et al. (2008)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - On the free-choice measure, SDT's own authors: it "is unidimensional and consequently cannot assess other important motivational concepts derived from self-determination theory, namely identified regulation, external regulation, and amotivation."
      - Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991), three experiments: after **positive** feedback, ego-involved participants persisted **less** than task-involved ones; after nonconfirming or no feedback they persisted **more**; in neither case did they report interest, enjoyment or perceived choice.
      - Fredricks et al.: "no distinction is made between effort aimed merely at fulfilling behavioral expectations and that aimed at understanding the material and mastering the content."
      - Skinner et al. (2008, N = 805, grades 4–7): emotional components of engagement drove later changes in behavioural ones; feedback from behaviour to emotion was not as consistent.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2000_GuayVallerandBlanchard_MO.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00995170

- **Source: Curran et al. (2015); Raedeke (1997); Barnett, Smoll & Smith (1992)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Meta-analysis of the passion literature, 94 studies and 1,308 effect sizes: harmonious and obsessive passion correlate with deliberate practice to a statistically indistinguishable degree (Hotelling's T = .48, ns); obsessive correlates **slightly more** with hours per week; obsessive passion is **unrelated to objective performance**. The only large clean separation is on activity dependence, itself a questionnaire.
      - Curran et al. (2011, n = 149 junior soccer players, mean age 16.2): harmonious and obsessive passion correlated **r = .73**, and obsessive passion was **unrelated to athlete burnout**.
      - The child version of the Passion Scale (Mageau et al. 2009, n = 163, mean age 11) ran α = .60 harmonious and .66 obsessive.
      - Raedeke (1997, 236 age-group swimmers): attraction ("I want to be here") and entrapment ("I have to be here") produce the same attendance record; entrapped clusters carried elevated burnout.
      - Barnett, Smoll & Smith (1992), Coach Effectiveness Training, 18 Little League coaches, ~ages 10–12: next-season attrition **5% in the trained group versus 26% in control**, with no difference in win-loss records; the largest self-esteem gains went to the children who started lowest.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-015-9503-0 ; https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.19.4.396 ; https://doi.org/10.1123/tsp.6.2.111

- **Source: Achenbach, McConaughy & Howell (1987); Südkamp, Kaiser & Möller (2012); Dizon-Ross (2019)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Cross-informant agreement about a child sits at r ≈ .28, stable across 119 studies and confirmed by later syntheses.
      - Teacher judgment accuracy runs about r = .63 for achievement and markedly lower for interest specifically.
      - Dizon-Ross (2019), N > 2,700: parents are roughly 30% wrong about which of their own two children is academically stronger, and correcting the belief causally reallocates real household investment.
      - The spread *within* adult report exceeds the gap between kinds of adult: in-the-moment parent report against device telemetry reached r = 0.35, while a single global retrospective question against a week of accelerometry reached r = −0.11, ns. **[VERIFY: locate the two telemetry/accelerometry studies before using these two numbers]**
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.101.2.213 ; https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027627 ; https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20171172

### Category 2: Sustaining the child

#### Subcategory 2.1: What a burnout instrument can and cannot do

- **Source: Salmela-Aro et al. (2009), School Burnout Inventory; Maslach Burnout Inventory threshold withdrawal**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 10 items, 3 factors (exhaustion, cynicism, inadequacy); original N = 1,418 Finnish adolescents; three-factor fit RMSEA .06, CFI .96.
      - School burnout is associated with roughly 4× higher dropout risk.
      - The sleep item reads "I often sleep badly because of matters related to my schoolwork," which requires the child to notice poor sleep *and* attribute it to the activity.
      - Validated age floors: the SBI was constructed on 16-year-olds and up; the BAT-C student version covers ages 9 to 13.
      - Mind Garden withdrew all Maslach Burnout Inventory cut-off scores in 2016 for lack of diagnostic validity; WHO ICD-11 classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon rather than a medical condition. **[VERIFY: cite the MBI manual edition and the ICD-11 entry directly]**
      - The best published attempt to detect emotional exhaustion from behavioural traces reached ROC AUC 0.56 against a chance level of 0.50 (239 adult workers, nine months, richer sensing than session logs). **[VERIFY: locate this study or drop the claim]**
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/abs/10.1027/1015-5759.25.1.48

- **Source: Yerkes-Dodson critique (Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2024); Wilson et al. (2019), the 85% rule**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - The inverted-U "optimal arousal" law rests on a 1908 study using 2–4 mice that never measured arousal.
      - Both challenge and hindrance stressors relate negatively to psychological strain and physical health.
      - Wilson et al. (2019): for a broad class of learning systems the optimal training error rate converges on ≈15.87%, that is, about 85% success.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4 ; **[VERIFY: add the Trends in Cognitive Sciences 2024 DOI]**

- **Source: Luthar & Becker (2002)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Affluent and high-pressure youth run 2–3× population norms for anxiety, depression, and substance use.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00492

#### Subcategory 2.2: The early start holds, the early lock-in does not

- **Source: Güllich et al. (2023); Barth et al. (2023); Brenner / AAP (2016)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Güllich et al. (2023) review: predictors of rapid junior performance and of long-term senior performance are largely opposite, and most elite athletes specialize in mid-to-late adolescence.
      - Barth et al. (2023) meta-analysis: junior-to-senior performance correlates at a pooled r = 0.148, roughly 2.2% of variance. At ages ~11–13 the correlation is approximately −0.05.
      - Brenner / AAP (2016) clinical report: roughly 70% of children drop organized sport by age 13. Prescribes at least 1–2 rest days per week and around 3 months per year away from a given sport. This report associates early specialization with burnout and overuse injury, which runs against the Güllich review's finding of no direct link.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1175718/full ; https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01906-0 ; https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2148

- **Source: Arkes & Ayton (1999); Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Arkes & Ayton (1999): sunk-cost reasoning is largely absent in young children and in non-human animals, and increases with age. The bias belongs to the adult, not the child.
      - Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017), 88 samples and 66,807 participants: grit correlates with conscientiousness at ρ ≈ .84, and the perseverance facet carries essentially all of the predictive weight while consistency-of-interest carries almost none.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.5.591 ; https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000102

- **Source: Music-school dropout and motivation study; autonomy-supportive coaching SEM; Saarinen (2025); Isoard-Gautheur et al. (2016); Raedeke & Smith (2001)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Controlled motivation raised dropout (β = 0.19); autonomous motivation was protective (β = −0.17); encouraging parental involvement was the strongest protective factor (β = −0.39).
      - Autonomy-supportive coaching predicted psychological resilience (β = 0.522).
      - ~50% of musical participants drop out by age 17; parental over-involvement showed a U-shaped harm pattern (Saarinen 2025).
      - Isoard-Gautheur et al. (2016), n=458, ages 14–18: the higher-burnout profile carried 2.2–2.4× the dropout rate six years later.
      - Raedeke & Smith (2001) Athlete Burnout Questionnaire triad: exhaustion, reduced sense of accomplishment, and sport devaluation. Devaluation is the earliest and quietest of the three.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1469029225000305 ; https://doi.org/10.1123/tsp.2014-0140

### Category 3: The record the child builds

#### Subcategory 3.1: How children experience a record kept about them

- **Source: Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Butler (1988); Balci & Morris (2026) badge RCT; 38-study dashboard review (2023); 2025 learning-analytics meta-analyses**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Expected, contingent tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, and the effect is *more* severe for children than for college students (Deci et al. 1999).
      - Butler (1988): fifth and sixth graders randomly assigned to receive grades, comments, or both. Interest was highest after comments, and grades-plus-comments performed like grades alone. The number did not dilute the comment, it erased it.
      - A digital-badge RCT (n=210) found badges reduced intrinsic motivation and satisfied no competence need. Sample was college students.
      - A 38-study systematic review found *no evidence* that dashboards improve academic achievement; 2025 meta-analyses find ES ≈ 0.36 (23 studies, 9,710 participants), heavily moderated by developmental level.
      - Motivational "task-value" prompts increased anxiety among already-anxious learners.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1988.tb00874.x ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506824 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042

- **Source: MyReadscape (Nature Humanities & Social Sciences Communications); Molenaar et al. (LAK 2020); Wolf (2008); ICO Children's Code; COPPA**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - MyReadscape (N=30, ages ~7–9): a public leaderboard motivated 79% of interviewed children but fostered only rank-based competition; badges had the weakest motivational impact of all features tested.
      - Molenaar et al. (2020): personalized trace-data visualizations improved accuracy and transfer for ages ~7–9.
      - Wolf (2008): at ages 6–7 a child understands roughly 4,000 words heard against roughly 600 read, a gap of about 7×.
      - COPPA governs under-13; the UK Children's Code (Age Appropriate Design Code) sets age-appropriate defaults. The COPPA 2025 amendments bar indefinite retention and require a written retention policy, with a compliance date of 2026-04-22.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-06750-x ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3375462.3375465 ; https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/

- **Source: Ragone et al. (CHI 2026) child-trust study; Agesilaou & Kyza (2022); Stoilova, Livingstone & Nandagiri (2020)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - No located study tests a learning-record, digital-credential, data-provenance, or authorship-tracking UI with children aged 8–14 on legibility, motivation, or stigma. The one AI-non-disclosure-shame study (survey n=97 plus interviews) used college students.
      - Ragone et al. (2026, N=289, ages 9–11): children engage with consent only when it is framed concretely ("asking before taking," not "data sharing") and object to systems "taking things without asking."
      - Agesilaou & Kyza (2022, n=63 fifth and sixth graders): children misunderstood shared and platform ownership of their own self-generated data.
      - Disclosure UI: a simple AI-use label outperformed a detailed one in an adolescent sample; anxiety about being accused of cheating rises with grade level.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790765 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212868922000058 ; https://www.cogitatiopress.com/mediaandcommunication/article/view/3407

#### Subcategory 3.2: Verifying authorship in the AI era

- **Source: Weber-Wulff et al. (2023); Liang et al. (2023); Krishna et al. (2023); OpenAI / Turnitin / Vanderbilt**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 14 detectors across 754 cases were "neither accurate nor reliable"; none exceeded 80% accuracy.
      - Seven GPT detectors misclassified non-native (TOEFL) English at an average 61.3% false-positive rate; all seven flagged 19.8% of TOEFL essays.
      - Paraphrasing dropped DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% accuracy at a fixed 1% false-positive rate.
      - OpenAI's own classifier (26% true-positive, 9% false-positive) was discontinued; Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector in August 2023, estimating ~750 wrongly-flagged papers per year at the vendor's claimed rates.

### Category 4: Proving the spike was worth building

#### Subcategory 4.1: What elite admissions rewards

- **Source: *SFFA v. Harvard* discovery. Reading Procedures, Class of 2023 (HARV00097939); Card expert report; Findings of Fact (D. Mass. 2019)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Verbatim extracurricular rating: "**1.** Unusual strength in one or more areas. Possible national-level achievement or professional experience… Truly unusual achievement. **2.** Strong secondary school contribution in one or more areas such as class president, newspaper editor, concertmaster… **3.** Solid participation but without special distinction." The scale enumerates **no** qualifying activity anywhere.
      - Card Exhibit 4, six years of domestic applicants: academic 1 with no other 1s → 68%; **extracurricular 1 with no other 1s → 48%**; four ratings of 2 → 68%; athletic 1 with no other 1s → 88%; **no rating of 1 or 2 on any dimension (n ≈ 56,000) → 0.1%**.
      - Card Exhibit 5: ~42% of applicants earn an academic 1 or 2, but fewer than 25% earn a 1 or 2 on each of the other three dimensions; only **7%** earn a 2 or better on at least three.
      - A separate **academic** rating rewards "possible national or international level recognition in academic competitions", so one academic-competition result scores on two axes.
      - Supplementary faculty review exists for **music, art, dance and academic** work only; MIT offers exactly four portfolios (Maker, Music & Theater Arts, Visual Arts & Architecture, Research). The Common App has 29 activity categories and none for games, puzzles or tabletop.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
   - **Link:** https://samv91khoyt2i553a2t1s05i-wpengine.netdna-ssl.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Doc-421-1-Card-Report.pdf

- **Source: Arcidiacono, Kinsler & Ransom (2022); Chetty, Deming & Friedman (2025); Stu Schmill, MIT Admissions (2006)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Logit coefficients on admission: academic rating 1 → **4.094**; extracurricular rating 1 → **4.232**; academic 2 → 1.425; extracurricular 2 → 1.990. At the bottom of the scale the asymmetry reverses, and a weak academic rating is roughly three times as damaging as a weak extracurricular one.
      - Chetty et al., using waitlist variation across Ivy-Plus colleges: ~**30%** of the admissions advantage held by top-1% applicants is explained by stronger **non-academic** ratings; conditional on test scores, private-school students have **no higher academic ratings but much higher non-academic ratings**; non-academic ratings are uncorrelated or negatively correlated with post-college outcomes, while academic credentials predict them.
      - MIT's Dean of Admissions, in a post subtitled "You don't need to take up the crumhorn to look 'different'": "we don't value one type of activity over another… You don't have to set yourself apart from your classmates (common advice you might hear from college counselors)."
