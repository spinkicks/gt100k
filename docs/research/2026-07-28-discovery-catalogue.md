# The discovery catalogue: what a child can actually do, and who will tell them they are good at it

**Date:** 2026-07-28 · **Status:** draft for review · **Supersedes:** nothing yet; if adopted, replaces `SEED_SUBTOPICS` as the child-facing menu

This memo turns eight umbrella topics and twenty-nine abstract subtopics into roughly forty-five
concrete pursuits, and records what verification found when every one of them was checked against a
real venue. It exists because "Board games" is not something a child can specialise in and "Chess"
is, and because the difference turned out to be nameable rather than a matter of taste.

Every entry below was fact-checked against a primary source. Where a source could not be found the
memo says so rather than guessing, because a catalogue whose venues do not exist is worse than a
short one.

---

## 1. The three tests

An entry earns a place by passing all three. The first two came out of the research; the third came
out of the verification, and it is the one that does the most work.

### Test 1 — the action program

Is there a shared motor sequence common to doing this? There is a way you play chess and a way you
bow a cello. There is no common action across Scrabble and Catan, or across cello and drums.

This is Rosch's operational criterion for the basic level, and it is the reason abstract subtopics
feel wrong without being obviously wrong: a superordinate category has no single image and no shared
action, so a child cannot picture doing it. Rosch et al. (1976) found children sort basic-level
categories like adults by age three, while only about half of kindergarteners and first-graders sort
superordinates taxonomically at all.

The test is not about specificity. "Sicilian Defence" is more specific than "chess" and is worse,
because it is a move inside an activity rather than the activity.

### Test 2 — external validation

Five questions, each answered with a name, a number or a URL, never a judgement:

1. **Named standard** — a written, publicly obtainable rubric, syllabus, rating system or
   measurement procedure that defines quality independently of any particular judge.
2. **Named venue** — a specific body, event, publication or database that will render a verdict on
   a particular thing this child made or did.
3. **Non-captive judge** — someone other than the child's parent, teacher, coach or this software.
   The sharp form of the question is *would this judge reject the work?*
4. **Age of admission** — the stated minimum, and any documented under-14 route.
5. **Cadence and cost** — how often a fresh verdict is available, and what each attempt costs.

The original version of this test asked for a credential ladder, and that was wrong. It excluded
audio engineering, where a child could become a serious speaker designer, while admitting nothing
better. The evidence says the property that matters is not whether a credential exists but what the
marker is *referenced to*: criterion-referenced feedback against a published standard behaves
differently from norm-referenced ranking against other children. Fong et al. (2019), meta-analysing
78 studies, found negative feedback had no demotivating effect at all relative to neutral feedback,
and was less demotivating still when it carried instructional detail and used criterion rather than
normative standards.

### Test 3 — the unaffiliated child

Can a child with no money, no club and one supportive-but-uninformed adult reach the verdict?

This test was added after verification, because it failed more entries than the other two combined.
It is not a nice-to-have: the product exists for children who lack exactly the family access that
every school-gated competition assumes.

## 2. What failed, and why it is worth knowing

Seven candidates failed outright. Each failure is more informative than a pass, because each marks a
place where the world has no route for a child.

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **Machine learning** | Kaggle bars under-13s entirely; 13–16 needs per-competition verified parental consent; prize rules require 18. No under-14 ML venue was found anywhere. |
| **Catan** | Every level of the US championship series, down to the local game-shop qualifier, requires entrants to be 18. There is no junior division. Catan Junior is a product, not a competition. |
| **Electronics** | Hackaday and Tindie publish and sell. Neither renders a verdict, and no venue was found that would reject a child's circuit against a published rubric. |
| **3D printing (as published)** | Thingiverse and Printables publish; they do not judge. Passes only when narrowed to a judged design challenge. |
| **Fossils and rocks** | myFOSSIL was sunsetted on 20 June 2025 and has no successor. The AFMS Uniform Rules are a genuinely rigorous rubric with a Junior exhibitor group, but entry requires membership of an affiliated club that hosts competitive exhibits. |
| **Public speaking (Gavel Clubs)** | Toastmasters proper is 18+. Gavel Clubs serve under-18s but must be chartered by a group with an adult counsellor; an individual child cannot join one that does not already exist nearby. |
| **Mahjong** | No youth division and no age-restricted ranking found in either the European Mahjong Association or the American Riichi Association. |

Three more were kept but with their venue replaced, because the venue named first was wrong:

- **Rocketry.** The American Rocketry Challenge is grades 6–12, requires a school or incorporated
  non-profit to sponsor the team, and costs $800–1,200. NAR contest flying is the real route: the US
  Model Rocket Sporting Code sets A Division at ages 7–13, junior membership is $30 a year including
  liability insurance, and judging is against the Sporting Code by contest officials.
- **Debate.** NSDA membership is school-based, and the Middle School National Tournament explicitly
  disqualifies students competing through non-school clubs. The Coolidge Foundation online opens are
  free, run a Middle School division, supply volunteer citizen judges so a child need not bring one,
  and have a **separate registration form for independent entrants and homeschoolers**.
- **Electronics → amateur radio.** The FCC Element 2 question pool is published free, the exam is
  **$5 for under-18s**, and the FCC bars close relatives from serving as your volunteer examiners —
  so the judge is non-captive by regulation, not by convention.

Umbrella is a **filter facet**, not a navigation step. All entries sit at one level.

Cost is per year for a child doing this seriously, excluding the instrument or computer itself.
"Alone" answers Test 3.

The fear that graded exams would require a teacher was wrong, and this is the best news in the

Music production moved from fail to pass mid-verification: ABRSM launched Creative Musicianship with
assessments live from May 2026. Songwriting is the lowest-equipment entry in the whole catalogue — a
voice and a phone.

**Speaker design carries a correction that matters.** The standard and the venue are two unconnected
paths, not one. The Ohio competition judges by subjective listening to three one-minute clips, using
a calibrated microphone only to level-match entries; it does not use CTA-2010. The standard is now
**-C**, published July 2024, and costs $102. Midwest Audiofest no longer exists — Parts Express
rebranded it in 2024. No age policy was found in either direction, and entrants must physically
deliver speakers to Ohio.

CoCoRaHS report or an individual AAVSO visual estimate. The standards and venues are excellent; the
rejecting judge only appears higher up.

## 4. Five ways this catalogue sorts children by something other than interest

Each of these is a confound that would be read as preference. They are listed in descending order of
how well evidenced they are — except the last, which is a property of how the catalogue is drawn
rather than of what is in it, and is recorded here because it is the only one this project creates
for itself.

### 4.1 Sex

The strongest data is Hallam, Rogers & Creech (2008), which surveyed all 150 English Music Services
and matched pupil names against the national dataset. Girls predominate at harp 90%, flute 89%, voice
80%, oboe 78%, clarinet 73%, with violin also girl-skewed. Boys predominate at electric guitar 81%,
bass 81%, tuba 77%, kit drum 75%, trombone 71%.

The figure that reframes those: **60% of all children learning any instrument are girls**, so the
baseline is already skewed and the boy-heavy instruments are more extreme than they look.

Four of this catalogue's five instrument entries sit near the extremes. Offering drums, guitar,
violin and singing as parallel choices will sort children by sex and return that sorting as measured
interest. The mitigation the data supports is to present the **low-skew** instruments — saxophone,
French horn, cornet, hand percussion — and never to show an instrument beside a photograph of a
player.

Elsewhere: US Chess is 12.7% female; speedcubing runs roughly 90% male by competitor count (from
user-run queries against the public WCA export, not an official publication); FIRST's own 2013 study
put FLL at 31% girls and noted boys had been 70% since 2003, and the near-parity figure in
circulation describes a grant subprogramme rather than the programme.

Debate is the case where the activity manufactures the gap rather than inheriting it. Female
participation in Public Forum averages 42% but female finalists 6%, and a study of 125,087 rounds
found female-female teams 17.1% less likely to win — **with no gender gap among novices** — while
girls were 30% more likely to quit.

### 4.2 Money

Free and reachable alone: iNaturalist, USACO, WPF Sudoku, Stone Soup, Scholastic Kids Press, Coolidge
debate, backgammon, photography, drawing, stop-motion, Blender. Amateur radio at $5 and bridge at $5
are next.

Against that: FIRST LEGO League at $500–900, graded music exams at £50–59 *per attempt* plus the
instrument, speaker design at $102 + $79 + roughly $300 of drivers plus travel to Ohio.

Every genuinely free entry in the Art & Animation group and none in Music. A child with no money can
reach the art leaves and not the music ones, and the catalogue would report that as taste.

### 4.3 Geography

UK-only: Young Animator of the Year, the Royal Academy show, Into Film, BBC Young Composer, and the
National Cipher Challenge's prizes. US-only: Scholastic, MTNA, NAR, 4-H, US Chess, ACBL.

Confirmed international: ABRSM Performance Grades and Creative Musicianship, NFFTY, Young Wildlife
Photographer, Toyota Dream Car, iNaturalist, eBird, IASC, the WPF Grand Prix, the WCA, Stone Soup.

If the user base is geographically mixed, that last list is the real catalogue and everything else is
a regional bonus.

### 4.4 Age, at the bottom of the band

This is the most serious structural problem and it was not anticipated. The product targets 6–14, and
most venues start at 10 to 13:

- eBird's terms bar under-13s outright, and the ABA's youngest division is 10–13 — so **birding has
  no route at all for ages 6–9**.
- picoCTF, Ludham Dare and itch.io are 13. BandLab, the obvious free DAW, is 13.
- Coolidge debate starts at grade 6; Scholastic Kids Press at 10; NPR at grade 4.
- 4-H Cloverbuds (5–7) are frequently **barred from competitive judging**, which is the whole point.

What genuinely works at 6–9: iNaturalist on a parent account, Pokémon TCG (hard floor 6), speedcubing
(no minimum), Math Kangaroo from kindergarten, Toyota Dream Car (7 and under), Stone Soup (no lower
bound), NAR rocketry from 7, and every ABRSM grade (any age).

That is eight entries out of forty-five. **The catalogue is thin below ten and should say so rather
than offer a six-year-old a wall of doors that are locked.**

### 4.5 The pictures, which are ours rather than the world's

The other four are facts about the world that the catalogue inherits. This one the product would
manufacture, so it is the one it can fully remove.

Javora, Hannemann, Stárková, Volná & Brom (2019), BJET 50(4) 1942–1960, ages 9–11, N = 53: the same
game at two aesthetic treatments, a strong preference for the prettier one (d > 0.86), chosen 62% of
the time in free choice, and no more learned either way. The sample is small and the manipulation is
between two versions rather than across a set, but the direction is unambiguous and the mechanism
transfers: on a wall where a click is read as interest, a tile that is merely nicer than its
neighbours is recorded as a passion.

Two possible responses, and the first one was tried first. **Remove the pictures**, which was done
for a day and was wrong: 44 word-tiles is 44 reading tasks for a six-year-old, and the flat interface
that outperformed the hierarchy in every grade of the Hutchinson study was itself a ring of category
icons, not a list of labels. The supporting citation for removal was also misapplied — Magner et
al.'s seductive-details effect concerns decoration placed *beside* material a child is trying to
learn, and nothing is being learned on a chooser.

So: **keep the pictures and remove the variance.** All 44 renders come from one scaffold — a single
object, one camera angle, one key light, one muted palette, one dark ground, no people or hands, no
text — and are then normalised to a common mean luminance, which took the raw set from a **3.84:1**
brightness spread down to **1.06:1**. The label strip carries the cabin hue at constant OKLCH
lightness and chroma; nothing tints the picture itself, because that would reintroduce the same
confound as a per-domain brightness difference. `passion/apps/design-lab/test/art.test.ts` fails the
build if the spread reopens past 1.1:1 or if any pursuit loses its file.

What this does **not** fix: subject appeal. A telescope is a more romantic object than a spiral
notepad and no amount of colour-matching changes that. It is unmeasured, it is probably real, and it
is the reason the model weights voluntary cross-day return rather than first click.

## 6. What this changes in the code

Nothing in the inference model. The eight cabins remain the coordinate system a belief is keyed to,
and the surfacing engine still pays maintenance debts per domain, which needs cells coarse enough to
have debts. What changes is what a child is shown: the catalogue above becomes the menu, umbrellas
become filter chips rather than a navigation step, and `SEED_SUBTOPICS` stops being rendered.

The specialisation ladder is unaffected and already correct — `mastery-map` holds a piano map keyed
to ABRSM and Trinity grades, which is exactly the shape the "named standard" column describes.

One asset obligation follows from §4.5: **every pursuit needs a tile render, and adding one to the
catalogue without one breaks the build.** `passion/apps/design-lab/scripts/build-art.mjs` holds the
generation scaffold and does the normalisation; the wall derives the path from the pursuit id, so
there is nothing to wire up beyond producing the file.

### 6.1 A second obligation, found by building it (2026-07-29)

The paragraph above says the inference model is untouched, and that held. What it missed is that
**the curated library was filed by the coordinate system too**, and a menu that is no longer the
taxonomy cannot be answered from a taxonomy key.

The symptom: a resource carries a `domainPath`, and the library's coverage check counted per
subtopic. All 29 were stocked, so it reported clean. But 22 of the 44 pursuits had no subtopic that
was *about* them — nothing in `SEED_SUBTOPICS` is sewing, birding, podcasting, speedcubing or
comics — while seven cells (perception, statistics, maths foundations, general physics) name no
pursuit. Lacking a shared key, the wall resolved a tap against the pursuit's **cabin**, so all eight
Music & Sound tiles returned one shelf and a child who tapped Speaker Design was handed four links
about orchestral instruments.

This is the same shape as the finding in §1: the umbrellas are not coarse pursuits, they are a
different kind of thing. Once that is true of the menu it is also true of everything keyed to the
menu, and the library was the first thing to find out. A crosswalk cannot repair it, because the
partitions are not nested — so `CuratedResource` now carries `pursuits` alongside `domainPath`, both
written by hand, and coverage is asked twice.

**The general rule, since it will recur.** A coverage check is only as good as its choice of
partition, and the partition to check is the one the child's tap lands on. Anything else reports
success while the surface fails. Worth applying to the surfacing engine's maintenance debts next:
those are paid per domain, which is right for a belief and may not be right for a promise made to a
child about a tile.

The seven orphaned cells are a catalogue finding rather than a library one. The perception set —
optical illusions, the Exploratorium's science snacks, BrainFacts, Frontiers for Young Minds — is
seven strong resources with no tile, and "find out how seeing works" is a plausible forty-fifth
pursuit that the three tests in §1 would probably pass. Left visible rather than forced onto
Reporting, which is not what any of it is about.

### 6.2 A fourth test arrives from outside this research (2026-07-30)

§1's three admission tests ask whether a pursuit is real, reachable and judged. A fourth has been
imposed on top of them by the product owner, and it does not come from anything in this document:
**does the pursuit credibly help a child reach a top-ranked university?**
See `docs/decisions/2026-07-30-catalogue-scope.md`.

It is worth being clear about how the two relate, because they can disagree. The tests here were
built to ask whether a child can genuinely get good at something and be told so by someone outside
their family. The new one asks whether that achievement is legible to an admissions reader, which
is a different question with a different answer for at least six entries. The admissions research
found that the leaked Harvard rubric grades level and never domain, and that prestige operates
instead through verification infrastructure — a second rating axis for academic competition, faculty
portfolio review for music, art and making, recruiting for athletics, and for games and puzzle
sports no channel at all, since the Common App has no category for them.

**That finding does not invalidate anything in §1–§5.** A pursuit that passes the three tests here
is still a pursuit a child can get genuinely good at. What the cut removes is entries where that is
true and the fourth test fails anyway, which is a decision about product positioning rather than a
correction to this research.

**Applied 2026-07-30: the catalogue is 37.** Both tests were kept rather than one replacing the
other, so only entries failing *both* were removed: Sudoku, Codes & Ciphers, Pokémon Cards,
Backgammon, Demoscene, Speaker Design and Weather Watching. The fifteen that have a real ceiling but
no documented precedent stayed.

The fourth test now lives in the data as `Pursuit.ceiling`, separate from `venue` on purpose, and
the separation immediately paid for itself: **21 of the 37 have a ceiling that opens later than the
pursuit's own floor.** The gap this document worried about in §4.4 — that the product targets 6–14
while most venues open at 10 to 13 — turns out to be *wider* than measured here, because the
