# GT100K BrainLift: making it real by reading, sustaining, and proving the spike

## Owners

- Felipe Caicedo
- David Ordoñez


## Purpose

Based on our previous brainlifts, for top school admissions, we need three things. Every child clears an academic floor. Above that floor they build a "spike", and we measure it by whether they come back to it on their own. They prove that thing by showing their work and defending it in person, not by handing in a finished product.

Then you start building, and the harder question shows up. How do you run all that with real kids and real privacy law without wrecking the thing you set out to do?

This document answers the *how*. How we tell what a child actually likes. How we push hard without breaking them. How we make the record of their work theirs instead of a file we keep on them. There is a shortcut on sale for each one. Read the kid's face. Add badges and points. Assume hard work sorts itself out. Trust the activity log to tell you why a child did something. All four are wrong, and most of them cost the school as much as the child.

Every SPOV below goes against something the field currently believes, and holds up anyway. We threw out the ones that were only edgy, and the ones that were only true.

### In scope

- Telling whether a child's interest will last by watching what they can do and what they keep doing, never by asking them and never by reading their face, voice, or pulse. Also what watching alone cannot tell you (SPOV 1, SPOV 2).
- What top colleges actually reward, taken from court records instead of from what admissions consultants say (SPOV 6).
- Treating burnout as something we measure and design against, instead of the price you pay for pushing kids hard.
- Making the record of a child's work something they can read, want, and never get punished by.

### Out of scope

- The academic floor and how we teach it, the case for building a spike at all, and how we pick families.
- Reading emotion or engagement off a child's face, voice, or heart rate. We rule it out on the evidence (SPOV 2) instead of exploring it.
- Final legal calls. We point out the rules that constrain the design.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)


### On reading the child

- **Spiky POV 1: "Let them choose and watch what they pick" does not work on 9 to 12 year olds, and the usual fix, "just build their skill," does not work either. Build skill because skill is the part you can build, and watch one thing: the child coming back on their own.**
   - **Elaboration:** The obvious move is to let a child pick and watch what they choose. Both that move and the neat fix for it fall apart when you read the studies. One study asked about 8,000 kids across five countries whether they were playing their sport by choice. More than 88% in every country said yes. The researchers threw the question out, because it could not tell a kid who loved the sport from one who was about to quit.

  Building skill instead does not hold up either. The studies on 9 to 12 year olds point both ways. Skill does make a child want harder work. But telling a child they are good at something does not make them want to do it more, and letting them choose does. An 11-week skills program moved neither their skill nor their interest. So skill and choice sit at about the same level of importance, and they differ in what you can do with them. Skill you can build and measure. Choice you can only protect. Build the skill, protect the choice, and watch whether the child comes back on their own. Skill feeds that. Control kills it.

  We still give a child a menu. Our product is built on one. The discovery wall shows dozens of things to try and asks the child to pick, which is exactly what this SPOV sounds like it is against. The 88% finding says do not read the pick. It does not say stop offering the menu. A first click only means the child saw the thing, and it changes nothing on our end. What counts is the child coming back days later without anyone asking them to, and they can only come back to something the menu showed them first. Treat the thing they clicked as the signal and the whole system stops working.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** the belief that what a child picks tells you what they love, and the follow-up belief that building skill is the main lever for interest that lasts.
   - **Backing:** Quested et al. (2013); Mabbe et al. (2018); Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008); Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Skjesol et al. (2025); the Childhood-and-Beyond cohort (2013); Review of Educational Research (2022) SDT meta-analysis. Honest limit: the two best studies on 9 to 12 year olds point opposite ways. So we are on much firmer ground saying free choice is a bad signal than saying skill beats choice.

- **Spiky POV 2: Watching what a child does is the only thing worth tracking, and it still cannot tell you *why* they did it. So the software reports what it saw and asks the guide to fill in the rest.**
   - **Elaboration:** The sales pitch is that a camera or a wristband can pick up whether a child is interested or stressed. They cannot. Emotion "cannot be reliably read from facial configurations," and since February 2025 the EU AI Act has banned guessing emotion in schools. So we watch what a child does instead: what they come back to, how far they go, how long they stay. You can measure that, and it predicts learning.

  Watching gets you less than it looks like, and that limit shapes the whole design. It tells you what a child keeps going back to and nothing about why. The people who built these measures say so themselves. The authors behind self-determination theory admit their own free-choice measure cannot tell the different kinds of motivation apart.

  That is a real problem for a product built on kids coming back. Praise can flip the signal backwards. In one set of experiments, adults who felt their ego was on the line went back to a task *less* after being told they did well, and *more* after being told they did badly. They did not enjoy it either way. Nobody has run this on children, so we are guessing it carries over. If a coach praises a child on Tuesday, that child's return on Thursday can mean the opposite of what we think. We do not record what an adult said next to the return, so we cannot see it happening. Recording it is the cheapest useful fix left.

  "Over-motivated" is the state every operator wants to catch, and it does not show up in hours either. Across 94 studies, mostly of adults, healthy and unhealthy passion predict the same amount of practice. Hours are the one thing an adult can see, and hours cannot tell the child who needs more from the child you should worry about.

  What you get instead of a detector is a short list of things to try. Take the activity away and watch what the child does. Offer a real way out that costs them nothing: a child who is there because they want to be will turn it down, and a child who feels stuck cannot even hear the offer. And work on the adults, which is the only place with solid evidence from actual children. A workshop that changed how coaches behaved cut next-season dropout from 26% to 5%, and the teams won just as much.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** the wearable and camera pitch that you can sense what a child feels from their body. The harder one: that a detailed enough log of what a child did can tell you why they did it, which is the assumption under most learning analytics. Also the folk idea that "over-motivated" is one state you can spot.
   - **Backing:** Barrett et al. (2019); npj Digital Medicine (2025); EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f); Guay, Vallerand & Blanchard (2000); Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991); Fredricks, Blumenfeld & Paris (2004); Curran et al. (2015); Raedeke (1997); Smith, Smoll & Curtis (1979) and Barnett, Smoll & Smith (1992); Volpe, McConaughy & Hintze (2009). Honest limit: refusing the sensors rests on the strongest evidence here, but the sensors do work for narrow jobs in a controlled lab. What breaks is the jump from that to emotion, and from the lab to a real classroom. Leaving out self-report is our own design choice rather than something a study told us, and it is what forces the software to say "here is what we saw, you decide."

### On sustaining the child

- **Spiky POV 3: Burnout is something we measure and design against. "Good stress" is a myth. Starting early matters, and locking a child into one thing early does not.**
   - **Elaboration:** The argument about pushing kids hard is stuck between two bad answers: push hard and damage them, or keep them safe and mediocre. Neither one is right.

  "Early specialization" is two claims jammed together, so pull them apart. Starting early works, and we keep it. In chess, about 1 in 4 players who started serious study by age 12 reached International Master, against 1 in 55 of those who started later. Music runs the same way, since the graded ladders assume years of practice before the junior competitions open at 13.

  Locking in early is the other claim, and it is wrong. What predicts fast success as a junior is close to the opposite of what predicts success as an adult, and most elite performers do not narrow down until their mid to late teens. Nothing supports "pick one thing early or fall behind." So we start early and refuse to lock in. That reverses a position we used to hold, and it is why the product keeps several live guesses going for each child and why parking one is always reversible.

  The damage does not come from hours. It comes from pressure, and from a child whose sense of worth depends on how they perform. In music schools, pressure raises dropout (β ≈ .19) and doing it for your own reasons protects against it (β ≈ −.17). A parent who encourages is the strongest protection of all (β ≈ −.39), and coaching that leaves a child real choice predicts resilience (β ≈ .52).

  "Good stress" is a myth too. The famous inverted-U curve comes from a 1908 study on a handful of mice that never measured stress at all, and both the good kind of stress and the bad kind track *worse* health. Hard-but-motivating still costs something.

  What we do about it, tracked like any other program metric: real burnout questionnaires (the School Burnout Inventory and others like it), a floor of 9 to 12 hours of sleep for 6 to 12 year olds, practice spread out over time, scheduled light weeks, and teaching that leaves the child real choices. School burnout makes a child four times more likely to drop out, and high-pressure schools run two to three times the normal rates of anxiety and depression.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that the damage from pushing hard is either built in (so cap ambition) or trivial (so push harder), and that "good stress" is real.
   - **Backing:** Frontiers (2023) specialization review; Gobet & Campitelli (2007); Salmela-Aro et al. (2009); Luthar & Becker (2002); Curran & Hill (2019); the CBT/mindfulness burnout meta-analysis; the Yerkes-Dodson critique (2024). Honest limit: the chess numbers come from a club full of titled players, so they are not the odds for a normal child. No burnout questionnaire is validated at age 8, so for the youngest kids we need a teacher or parent to report instead. Borrowing "light weeks" from sports and applying them to schoolwork is reasoning, not tested fact. And one review finds no direct link between specializing and burning out, which we read as the harm running through pressure rather than through workload.

### On the record the child builds

- **Spiky POV 4: A child has to be able to read their own record and want it to exist, or it is just surveillance. Badges, points, and dashboards make it worse.**
   - **Elaboration:** A tamper-proof record of how a child actually worked is only worth having if the child can read it and wants it there. The standard EdTech answer is to gamify it: badges, points, a dashboard. That backfires, and it backfires harder on children than on the college students most of these features were tested on. Rewards a child expects for doing a task cut their interest *more* than they cut an adult's. One controlled trial found digital badges *lowered* interest and did nothing to make students feel more capable. A review of 38 studies found *no evidence* that dashboards improve achievement, and the 2025 meta-analyses find small effects that depend heavily on the child's age. Even encouraging "this matters" messages *raised* anxiety in children who were already anxious.

  One thing helps: showing a child their own progress against their own past, never a leaderboard. That rules out running totals, streaks, reward tokens, and any comparison between children.

  Nobody has tested a record like ours on kids this age, but nearby research gives us something to work with. Children 9 to 11 understand data and consent when you put it in plain, personal terms, like "asking before taking" rather than "data sharing," and they think about their own records in terms of who has power over them. Older students left with the school's default framing describe being tracked as *surveillance* and give up on having any say in it. That last finding comes from focus groups with high schoolers, so it may not reach our age group. On owning up to AI use, a simple label beat a detailed one, and the fear of being accused of cheating grows as kids get older.

  So we build the record to be understood and owned: plain words, comparison only against yourself, disclosure that is easy and carries no shame, and nothing designed to keep a kid in the app. Same rule we use on the signal side.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that badges, points, leaderboards, and progress dashboards are safe, motivating defaults for children, and that a richer, more detailed record is automatically a more trustworthy one.
   - **Backing:** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Balci & Morris (2026); the 38-study dashboard review (2023) and the 2025 learning-analytics meta-analyses; MyReadscape; CHI-2026 child-trust study; Agesilaou & Kyza (2022); Stoilova, Livingstone & Nandagiri (2020); the Australian school-datafication focus groups; the ICO Children's Code and COPPA. Honest limit: we could not find a single study testing a learning record, credential, or authorship screen on 8 to 14 year olds. Everything above is borrowed from nearby research, so this is the one pillar the program will have to prove itself.

- **Spiky POV 5: Every piece of the EvidenceGraph already exists off the shelf. What we own is the way the pieces fit together, plus the in-person defense an AI-only competitor cannot run. The hard problem is proving we deleted something, not proving nobody tampered with it.**
   - **Elaboration:** Two opposite hunches, both wrong.

  The first is that somebody must have built this already. Nobody has. No product on the market combines content-addressed provenance, signed attestations, transparency logs, and verifiable credentials into one tamper-proof record of how a child learned. Putting them together looks new.

  The second is that the hard part must be proving the work is real. That part is already solved, and it is free. W3C Verifiable Credentials, Open Badges 3.0 and CLR 2.0, in-toto and SLSA attestations, Rekor and Trillian transparency logs, RO-Crate mapped to W3C PROV, C2PA content credentials, and sentence-by-sentence authorship replay from Grammarly are all mature and reusable. None of them proves a human did the work without help, and C2PA says so in its own spec. So trust still has to rest where a real school already stands: a five-minute defense in person, on a sample of the work. A remote competitor holding nothing but the finished file cannot do that, and it gets more valuable every year as AI makes files easier to fake.

  The genuinely hard part is the reverse of what the crypto framing suggests. It is proving you deleted a child's data from a log built never to change. Our answer is to keep only fingerprints on the permanent log and put the erasable child data in a separate store with its own keys.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that this is either already built, or that the hard part is proving the work is authentic.
   - **Backing:** Open Badges 3.0 / CLR 2.0 / W3C VC; in-toto, SLSA, Sigstore; Workflow-Run RO-Crate ↔ PROV; C2PA 2.1; EDPB Guidelines 02/2025; Weber-Wulff et al. (2023); Liang et al. (2023); Zhang et al. (2024). Honest limit: we call it new because we could not find it, which does not rule out somebody building it privately. And the evidence for oral defenses mostly comes from universities, not schools.

### On proving the spike was worth building

- **Spiky POV 6: The spike separates children who have already cleared the academic floor. It never substitutes for clearing it.**
   - **Elaboration:** The pitch-deck version says a spiky profile beats good scores. The Harvard court records say something narrower and more useful.

  Harvard scores applicants on four separate things, and the discovery data lets you compare them. Applicants who scored a 2 on all four (solid at academics, activities, personal qualities, and sports) got in 68% of the time. Applicants with a top score in activities and nothing else top got in 48% of the time. Skim that and it looks like being well-rounded wins.

  It does not, because every row in that table only counts applicants with no other top scores. So it compares a spike from a kid *without* the grades against a well-rounded kid. The profile we build, strong academics plus one exceptional thing, is not in the table at all. For that profile the two ratings add up instead of trading off. A top academic rating is worth 4.094 in the model and a top activity rating 4.232, both huge, and only 7% of applicants score a 2 or better on three of the four.

  Two things matter more than the admit rates. First, the rubric grades how good you are and never what you did. It defines a 1 as "national-level achievement… truly unusual achievement" and never lists a single approved activity, so the usual ranking of prestigious hobbies is not in the instrument at all. Second, the subject does matter, but only for getting your claim checked. Academic competitions trigger a second rating. Music, art, and making get a faculty member to review a portfolio. Athletes get recruited. That machinery exists for some pursuits and not others. Prestige is really about who can verify what you say, and the EvidenceGraph fills that gap.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** the admissions-consulting line that a spike beats a strong all-round profile, and the opposite reflex from school counselors that activities are just decoration on a transcript. Also the widespread assumption that a spike only counts if the subject is prestigious.
   - **Backing:** Harvard Reading Procedures, Class of 2023 (HARV00097939); Card expert report Exhibit 4; Arcidiacono, Kinsler & Ransom (2022); Chetty, Deming & Friedman (2025); Stu Schmill, MIT Admissions (2006). **Honest limit:** every number here is a correlation. The rating is one reader's judgment of a whole application, not a fact about the child, so getting better at something and getting a better number written down are two different things. No study proves any activity causes admission, and nothing in this literature looks at anyone under 17. Nothing here backs the link from starting at six to standing out at seventeen, which is what the whole program rests on.

## DOK 3: Insights

What we concluded after working through the sources. These connect the Knowledge Tree to the SPOVs.

### Reading the child

- **Insight 1:** Almost every child says they chose this. When more than 88% agree with "this was my choice" whether or not they are about to quit, what a child picks cannot tell you what they will stick with, so a program built on "watch what they pick" is measuring noise. Skill and coming back on their own are the two things we can build and measure. (→ SPOV 1)
- **Insight 2:** A child needs both skill and choice, and at 9 to 12 neither one outranks the other. They do differ in what we can do about them. We can build skill and measure it, and we can only protect a child's sense that this was their idea. So we aim at skill plus return, with choice protected, rather than drilling skill on its own. Building skill does not create interest by itself, and an 11-week skills program proved it by moving neither. Skill "wins" only because we can build and measure it. (→ SPOV 1, 3)
- **Insight 3:** In the youngest kids, what a child can actually do predicts future engagement better than what they think they can do. So asking a 9 or 10 year old to rate their own skill is weak, and we should lean on what we can watch instead. (→ SPOV 1, 2)
- **Insight 4:** You cannot read what a child feels from their body, and in EU schools it is illegal to try. What they do is readable and does predict things. The field throws out "engagement detection" as one idea when it is really two: reading faces and pulses (does not work) and watching what a child does (works). We stay on the side that works. (→ SPOV 2)

### Sustaining the child

- **Insight 5:** The case for locking in early is weaker than people assume, because what makes a fast junior is close to the opposite of what makes a great adult. So we keep the early start, drop the early commitment, and give up nothing in achievement. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 6:** Burnout comes from pressure and from a child tying their worth to results, not from hours. Pressure raises dropout and doing it for your own reasons protects against it, so we can keep expectations high and still attack the thing that does the damage. The same choice-versus-control split decides whether a parent's involvement helps or hurts. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 7:** We cannot assume stress is good for kids. The inverted-U curve is folklore, and both kinds of stress track worse health, so nothing lets us excuse pressure by calling it motivating. Sleep, spacing, and light weeks are part of the design, not extras. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 8:** Real burnout questionnaires work from about age 9 or 10 up, and none work by self-report at 8, so for the youngest kids a parent or teacher has to report instead. Any single cutoff score only applies to the group it was built on, so we watch several sources rather than waiting for one alarm to go off. (→ SPOV 3)

### The record the child builds

- **Insight 9:** Gamifying is a trap at this age. Rewards cut interest *more* in children than in adults, badges can cut it outright, and dashboards do not raise achievement. So our record holds back on motivational features instead of piling them on. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 10:** One kind of progress view helps: the child against their own past, specific enough to act on, never a leaderboard. Encouraging messages can raise anxiety in kids who are already anxious, so encouragement is not free either. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 11:** We went looking and found no study at all testing a learning record, credential, or authorship screen on 8 to 14 year olds, so this is still an open design problem. Nearby research on kids and data points the way. Talk in plain, personal terms ("asking before taking"). Expect the school's default framing to feel like *surveillance*, because kids stop believing they have a say. Keep disclosure simple, since a plain AI-use label beat a detailed one. And treat readability as the designer's job rather than something a teacher can fix later. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 12:** The proof machinery is a commodity, and putting it together is the new part. Every layer already exists and is well tested. What is ours is combining them for a child and pairing them with an in-person defense that a competitor holding only the finished file cannot run. (→ SPOV 5)
- **Insight 13:** The hard problem is the opposite of what the marketing says. Proving nobody tampered with the record is a commodity. The hard part is *proving we deleted a child's data from a log built never to change*, which our design aims at and we have not built yet. The competitive scan confirms it is the frontier. (→ SPOV 5)

### Proving the spike was worth building

- **Insight 14:** The comparison everyone quotes is the wrong one. "Four 2s beat a single top activity rating" sounds like being well-rounded beats going deep, but every row in that table only counts kids with *no other top ratings*. So it is really comparing a spike from a kid without the grades against a well-rounded kid. Our profile, grades plus one exceptional thing, is not in the table at all, and the model says the two ratings add up rather than trade off. The spike sorts kids who already have the academics. (→ SPOV 6)
- **Insight 15:** The rubric grades *how good you are, never what you did*, and never lists an approved activity. Prestige by subject is real, but it lives in *who can check your claim* rather than in what the reader is told to value: a second rating for academic competitions, a faculty portfolio review for music, art, and making, and recruiting for athletes. That turns the EvidenceGraph from a nice-to-have into the direct answer, since it gives a way to verify pursuits that have none. (→ SPOV 6, SPOV 5)

### Reading motivation

- **Insight 16:** Refusing the sensors bought us less than it looked like. What we kept instead, watching what a child does, *also* cannot name why they are doing it, and the authors of the theory say so about their own measure. So the honest answer is to split the job rather than to guess better. The log holds what happened, the guide holds the rest, and the software asks instead of deciding. (→ SPOV 2)
- **Insight 17:** Adult praise can flip our main signal. For a child who feels pressure from inside, coming back goes up after a setback and down after success, so the same return means opposite things depending on what an adult said last time. We cannot read a return without knowing what was said and what else the child could have done, so we log both next to every return. (→ SPOV 2, SPOV 1)
- **Insight 18:** "Over-motivated" is not one thing, and the number an adult would use to spot it carries no information. Healthy and obsessive passion track practice hours the same, with obsessive a little higher. So the dashboard a stakeholder asks for, a grid based on intensity, cannot be built, and it would fail in a predictable direction by flagging the absorbed kids and missing the pressured ones. What replaces it is tests the guide runs and work aimed at the adults, which is also where the only controlled evidence on children sits. (→ SPOV 2, SPOV 3)

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

The sources, plus the facts and summaries the insights are built on. DOK 1 facts come straight from the source. DOK 2 summaries are in our own words.

### Category 1: Reading the child

#### Subcategory 1.1: Competence versus free choice as a durable-liking signal

- **Source: Quested et al. (2013), youth-sport motivation across five countries**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - N ≈ 8,000 youth players, mean age 11.56, five countries.
      - The free-choice "this is my choice" item was endorsed by more than 88% of children in every country and was dropped for non-discrimination.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - When almost every child says yes to the same question, that question cannot tell a kid who will stay from a kid who is about to quit. This is the core reason we treat free choice as a weak signal.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2013_Quested_et_al.pdf

- **Source: Meta-analysis, Review of Educational Research (2022), SDT needs and motivation**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 144 samples, 79,079 participants (sample mean age 16.55).
      - Relative weights for self-determined motivation: competence 43.21%, autonomy 34.49%, relatedness 22.31%; competence-IM and autonomy-IM correlations near-equal (ρ .58 vs .57).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Of the three needs, skill carries the biggest share, which supports leading with skill while keeping choice right behind it.
   - **Link:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8935530/

- **Source: Finnish 6-year longitudinal (J. Science and Medicine in Sport, 2015); Barnett et al. (2008); actual-vs-perceived competence (PubMed 29016237)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Finnish study (n=333, baseline mean age 12.4, Grade 7→12): Grade-7 perceived competence predicted later physical activity (β = .28 total METs, .29 vigorous); Grade-7 autonomous motivation and enjoyment were *not* significant predictors.
      - Barnett et al. (2008): childhood object-control skill predicted adolescent activity (18% variance) and fitness (30%) ~6 years later; adding the perceived-competence path nearly doubled R².
      - Actual-vs-perceived (N=201, ages 6–9): actual motor competence explained 9–30% of activity variance vs 0–5% for perceived competence.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Skill predicts whether a child keeps going better than their motivation or their enjoyment does, and in the youngest kids what they can *actually* do beats what they think they can do. That is why we build on skill, and why asking a young child to rate themselves is weak.
   - **Link:** (Barnett/PASS and Finnish longitudinal as cited in the 2026-07-29 competence research report)

- **Source: Fresh 9–12 deep-research (2026-08-01): Mabbe et al. (2018); Patall et al. (2008); Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999); Skjesol et al. (2025); Childhood-and-Beyond cohort (2013)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Mabbe et al. (2018, n=110, mean age 10.71): competence satisfaction predicted challenge-seeking (b = .31, p < .001) while autonomy satisfaction did not (b = .01, ns); competence manipulation partial η² = .65 on competence satisfaction and .33 on intrinsic motivation, vs .07–.08 for the autonomy manipulation.
      - Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999): positive verbal reward/feedback effect on free-choice intrinsic motivation d = 0.11 (ns) for children vs d = 0.43 for college students; age difference Qb(1) = 5.14, p < .02.
      - Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008): choice raises intrinsic motivation d = 0.30 overall and d = 0.51 for primary-school students; a 2019 experiment (N=126, mean age 10.8) found the choice effect mediated *jointly* by autonomy and competence.
      - Skjesol et al. (2025, RCT n=175, ages 9–12): an 11-week competence-oriented soccer program raised neither perceived competence nor interest/value; interest/enjoyment declined significantly in both arms.
      - Childhood-and-Beyond (2013): child perceived competence predicted the *amount* of later participation, not *whether* the child continued; only parental influence predicted continuation.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The studies on 9 to 12 year olds split. Skill makes a child take on harder work, but telling a child they are skilled does not make them want to do it more, and giving them a choice does. So skill and choice run side by side and we need both. Skill's only advantage is that we can build it and measure it, and building it does not create lasting interest on its own.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2018_MabbeSoenensVansteenkiste_ExperimentalChildPsych.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint7040101

#### Subcategory 1.2: Why inner state cannot be read from the body

- **Source: Barrett et al. (2019), "Emotional expressions reconsidered"; EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) / Recital 44**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Emotion "cannot be reliably read from facial configurations"; variability is the norm.
      - The EU AI Act prohibits emotion inference in education (in force 2 February 2025).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Reading emotion off a face has no scientific basis, and in EU schools it is illegal, so we never do it.
   - **Link:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100619832930

- **Source: rPPG skin-tone bias (npj Digital Medicine, 2025); voice-stress literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Remote photoplethysmography mean absolute error ~4.23 bpm (Fitzpatrick I–V) rising to ~13.58 bpm (type VI), >2× degradation; "in the wild" correlation with true heart rate only r ≈ 0.58.
      - Voice-stress analysis is characterized as essentially pseudoscience.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Even when a body sensor partly works, it works worse on darker skin and falls apart outside the lab, and jumping from a heart rate to "stressed" has no support at all. So watching what a child does is the only thing we can defend.
   - **Link:** (npj Digital Medicine 2025 rPPG study, as cited in the 2026-07-21 burnout research report)

#### Subcategory 1.3: Why behaviour cannot name a motivational state

- **Source: Guay, Vallerand & Blanchard (2000); Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991); Fredricks, Blumenfeld & Paris (2004)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - On the free-choice measure, SDT's own authors: it "is unidimensional and consequently cannot assess other important motivational concepts derived from self-determination theory, namely identified regulation, external regulation, and amotivation."
      - Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991), three experiments: after **positive** feedback, ego-involved participants persisted **less** than task-involved ones; after nonconfirming or no feedback they persisted **more**; in neither case did they report interest, enjoyment or perceived choice.
      - Fredricks et al.: "no distinction is made between effort aimed merely at fulfilling behavioral expectations and that aimed at understanding the material and mastering the content."
      - Skinner et al. (2008, N = 805, grades 4–7): emotional components of engagement drove later changes in behavioural ones; feedback from behaviour to emotion was not as consistent.
      - Volpe, McConaughy & Hintze (2009, ages 6–11): for on-task behaviour, person variance was 10.6% and person-by-occasion plus residual 70.4%; ~14 sessions were needed for a dependable person-level estimate.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - What separates one kind of motivation from another is why a child acts, and the reason is not in the log. The field's one behavioural measure is documented as unable to tell them apart. Worse, a return means the opposite thing depending on what an adult said last, so praise flips our main signal, and we do not yet record what was said, so we cannot read a return in full. Watching also lags behind, because how a child feels moves first.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2000_GuayVallerandBlanchard_MO.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00995170

- **Source: Curran, Hill, Appleton, Vallerand & Standage (2015); Raedeke (1997); Smith, Smoll & Curtis (1979) and Barnett, Smoll & Smith (1992)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Meta-analysis of the passion literature, 94 studies and 1,308 effect sizes: harmonious and obsessive passion correlate with deliberate practice to a statistically indistinguishable degree (Hotelling's T = .48, ns); obsessive correlates **slightly more** with hours per week; obsessive passion is **unrelated to objective performance**. The only large clean separation is on activity dependence, itself a questionnaire.
      - Curran et al. (2011, n = 149 junior soccer players, mean age 16.2): harmonious and obsessive passion correlated **r = .73**, and obsessive passion was **unrelated to athlete burnout**.
      - The child version of the Passion Scale (Mageau et al. 2009, n = 163, mean age 11) ran α = .60 harmonious and .66 obsessive.
      - Raedeke (1997, 236 age-group swimmers): attraction ("I want to be here") and entrapment ("I have to be here") produce the same attendance record; entrapped clusters carried elevated burnout.
      - Coach Effectiveness Training (18 Little League coaches, ~ages 10–12): next-season attrition **5% in the trained group versus 26% in control**, with no difference in win-loss records; the largest self-esteem gains went to the children who started lowest.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Hours are the one thing an adult can see, and hours are exactly what cannot tell healthy from unhealthy, so any system built on intensity gets it wrong in both directions. What a program can do instead is run tests rather than wait for signals: take the activity away, and offer a real way out. It can also work on the adults, which is where the only controlled evidence on children sits, and where the reliable fix is taking things away rather than adding them.
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-015-9503-0 ; https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.19.4.396

### Category 2: Sustaining the child

#### Subcategory 2.1: Burnout as a measurable variable, and the "good stress" myth

- **Source: Salmela-Aro et al. (2009), School Burnout Inventory**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 10 items, 3 factors (exhaustion, cynicism, inadequacy); original N = 1,418 Finnish adolescents; three-factor fit RMSEA .06, CFI .96.
      - School burnout is associated with roughly 4× higher dropout risk.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - A short, tested questionnaire means we can track burnout as a real number instead of guessing, though it has only been validated down to about age 9 or 10, not 8.
   - **Link:** https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/abs/10.1027/1015-5759.25.1.48

- **Source: Yerkes-Dodson critique (Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2024); challenge/hindrance stress literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - The inverted-U "optimal arousal" law rests on a 1908 study using 2–4 mice that never measured arousal.
      - Both challenge and hindrance stressors relate negatively to psychological strain and physical health.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - We cannot design as if stress were good for kids. Rest and workload limits are required in a program this intense.
   - **Link:** (Yerkes-Dodson critique, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 2024, as cited in the 2026-07-21 burnout research report)

- **Source: Luthar & Becker (2002); Curran & Hill (2019); CBT/mindfulness burnout meta-analysis (5 RCTs)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Affluent/high-pressure youth run 2–3× population norms for anxiety, depression, and substance use.
      - Socially-prescribed perfectionism rose ~33% from the late 1980s to the late 2010s.
      - Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (ages 11–23): CBT/mindfulness reduced exhaustion d = −0.87, sport devaluation d = −0.77, reduced accomplishment d = −0.74; CBT outperformed mindfulness for exhaustion.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The risk is real, it is rising, and it responds to treatment, so we build the safeguards in from the start instead of adding them after a child gets hurt.
   - **Link:** https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000138.pdf

#### Subcategory 2.2: Long-term risks of early specialization, and what protects

- **Source: Frontiers in Sports & Active Living (2023), early- vs late-specialization review**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Predictors of rapid junior performance and of long-term senior performance are largely opposite; most elite athletes specialize in mid-to-late adolescence.
      - One specialization-exhaustion study (n=169, mean age 16.29) found higher emotional/physical exhaustion in specialized athletes (p = .030, d = −0.34).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Reaching the top does not require picking one thing early, so we keep the early start and design the lock-in out. One review disagrees and finds no direct link between specializing and burning out, which we read as the harm running through pressure rather than through the specializing itself.
   - **Link:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1175718/full

- **Source: Music-school dropout/motivation study; autonomy-supportive coaching SEM; Saarinen (2025)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Controlled motivation raised dropout (β = 0.19); autonomous motivation was protective (β = −0.17); encouraging parental involvement was the strongest protective factor (β = −0.39).
      - Autonomy-supportive coaching predicted psychological resilience (β = 0.522).
      - ~50% of musical participants drop out by age 17; parental over-involvement showed a U-shaped harm pattern (Saarinen 2025).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Whether a hard pursuit hurts a child depends on how much say they have and why they are doing it, not on how many hours they put in. Too little push from a parent hurts, and so does too much. That is why we teach in a way that leaves kids real choices, and why we screen families for the warm-but-demanding kind.
   - **Link:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1469029225000305

### Category 3: The record the child builds

#### Subcategory 3.1: How children and families experience evidence and credential systems

- **Source: Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Balci & Morris (2026) badge RCT; 38-study dashboard review (2023); 2025 learning-analytics meta-analyses**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Expected, contingent tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, and the effect is *more* severe for children than college students (Deci et al. 1999).
      - A digital-badge RCT (n=210) found badges reduced intrinsic motivation and satisfied no competence need (sample: college students).
      - A 38-study systematic review found *no evidence* dashboards improve academic achievement; 2025 meta-analyses find ES ≈ 0.36 (23 studies, 9,710 participants), heavily moderated by developmental level.
      - Motivational "task-value" prompts increased anxiety among already-anxious learners.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The usual badges-and-dashboard playbook backfires at this age, so our record holds back on motivational features and shows a child only their own progress.
   - **Link:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506824 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042

- **Source: MyReadscape (Nature HSS Communications); Molenaar et al. (LAK 2020); ICO Children's Code; COPPA**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - MyReadscape (N=30, ages ~7–9): a public leaderboard motivated 79% of interviewed children but fostered only rank-based competition; badges had the weakest motivational impact of all features.
      - Molenaar et al.: personalized trace-data visualizations improved accuracy and transfer for ages ~7–9.
      - COPPA governs under-13; the UK Children's Code (Age Appropriate Design Code) sets age-appropriate defaults.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Showing a child their own progress helps, and badges and competition do not. All of it sits inside child-data rules we have to follow. So we aim for a record a child can read and compare against themselves, never a ranking.
   - **Link:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-06750-x ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3375462.3375465 ; https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/

- **Source: Fresh deep-research (2026-08-01): CHI-2026 child-trust study; Agesilaou & Kyza (2022); Stoilova/Livingstone/Nandagiri (2020); Australian school-datafication focus groups; CDT K-12 AI survey**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - No located study tests a learning-record, digital-credential, data-provenance, or authorship-tracking UI with children aged 8–14 on legibility/motivation/stigma; the one AI-non-disclosure-shame study (survey n=97 + interviews) is on college students.
      - CHI-2026 child-trust study (N=289, ages 9–11): children engage with consent only when framed concretely ("asking before taking," not "data sharing") and object to systems "taking things without asking."
      - Agesilaou & Kyza (2022, n=63 fifth/sixth-graders): children misunderstood shared/platform ownership of their own self-generated data.
      - Australian secondary focus groups (n=62): students framed school datafication through "power, surveillance and affect," reporting "digital resignation" and perceived powerlessness; CDT survey: ~half of students said classroom AI use made them feel less connected to their teacher.
      - Disclosure UI: a simple AI-use label outperformed a detailed one (adolescent sample); cheating-accusation anxiety rises with grade level.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The gap in direct evidence is real, but nearby research on kids and data gives us something to work with: plain personal language, nothing that feels like being watched, simple disclosure, and readability treated as the designer's job. So this is a problem we have to design and test ourselves rather than copy from someone else.
   - **Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790765 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212868922000058 ; https://www.cogitatiopress.com/mediaandcommunication/article/view/3407

#### Subcategory 3.2: Verifying authorship in the AI era

- **Source: Weber-Wulff et al. (2023); Liang et al. (2023); Krishna et al. (2023); OpenAI/Turnitin/Vanderbilt**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 14 detectors across 754 cases were "neither accurate nor reliable"; none exceeded 80% accuracy.
      - Seven GPT detectors misclassified non-native (TOEFL) English at an average 61.3% false-positive rate; all seven flagged 19.8% of TOEFL essays.
      - Paraphrasing dropped DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% accuracy at a fixed 1% false-positive rate.
      - OpenAI's own classifier (26% true-positive, 9% false-positive) was discontinued; Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector (Aug 2023), estimating ~750 wrongly-flagged papers/year at claimed rates.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - AI text detectors are inaccurate, easy to beat, and worst for the students with the least protection, so one must never trigger any consequence for a child. The 2026 evidence says the same.
   - **Link:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z ; https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(23)00130-7

- **Source: Zhang et al. (2024, ICML); C2PA specification; interactive oral assessment (Dawson; Sotiriadou et al. 2020)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Strong watermarking of generative text is formally impossible under natural assumptions.
      - C2PA attests who signed, when, and with what tool, but "makes no judgment about whether provenance is 'true'" and does not attest human authorship.
      - Interactive oral assessment demonstrated resilience to misconduct including generative-AI misuse; the evidence base is mostly higher-education.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - No test on the finished file proves a human wrote it, so trust has to come from the process plus a spot-check where the child defends the work. That is the one thing a real school can do.
   - **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04378 ; https://philldawson.com/

#### Subcategory 3.3: The provenance stack and the deletion frontier

- **Source: Open Badges 3.0 / CLR 2.0 / W3C VC; in-toto/SLSA; Sigstore (Rekor/Trillian); Workflow-Run RO-Crate ↔ PROV; C2PA; Grammarly Authorship**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**

      - Grammarly Authorship ships per-sentence AI/human/web attribution plus writing-process replay (version-history, not cryptographic).
      - No existing product combines these into a single tamper-evident, content-addressed learning-provenance DAG; that integration appears novel.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Every building block is finished and reusable, so what sets us apart is putting them together for a child and pairing them with the in-person defense, not any single piece of crypto.

   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**

### Category 4: Proving the spike was worth building

#### Subcategory 4.1: What elite admissions rewards

- **Source: *SFFA v. Harvard* discovery. Reading Procedures, Class of 2023 (HARV00097939); Card expert report; Findings of Fact (D. Mass. 2019)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Verbatim extracurricular rating: "**1.** Unusual strength in one or more areas. Possible national-level achievement or professional experience… Truly unusual achievement. **2.** Strong secondary school contribution in one or more areas such as class president, newspaper editor, concertmaster… **3.** Solid participation but without special distinction." The scale enumerates **no** qualifying activity anywhere.
      - Card Exhibit 4, six years of domestic applicants: academic 1 with no other 1s → 68%; **extracurricular 1 with no other 1s → 48%**; four ratings of 2 → 68%; athletic 1 with no other 1s → 88%; **no rating of 1 or 2 on any dimension (n ≈ 56,000) → 0.1%**.
      - Card Exhibit 5: ~42% of applicants earn an academic 1 or 2, but fewer than 25% earn a 1 or 2 on each of the other three dimensions; only **7%** earn a 2 or better on at least three.
      - A separate **academic** rating rewards "possible national or international level recognition in academic competitions", so one academic-competition result scores on two axes.
      - Supplementary faculty review exists for **music, art, dance and academic** work only; MIT offers exactly four portfolios (Maker, Music & Theater Arts, Visual Arts & Architecture, Research). The Common App has 29 activity categories and none for games, puzzles or tabletop.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The rubric grades how good you got and never what you did, so there is no approved-subject list to satisfy. The 68 versus 48 gap is not breadth beating depth, because every row only counts kids with no other top ratings, which pits a spike from a kid *without* the grades against a well-rounded kid. Our profile is not in the table at all. Where the subject does matter is proof: some pursuits have a way to check the claim and some do not, and a record like ours supplies one.
   - **Link:** https://samv91khoyt2i553a2t1s05i-wpengine.netdna-ssl.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Doc-421-1-Card-Report.pdf

- **Source: Arcidiacono, Kinsler & Ransom (2022); Chetty, Deming & Friedman (*QJE*, 2025); Stu Schmill, MIT Admissions (2006)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Logit coefficients on admission: academic rating 1 → **4.094**; extracurricular rating 1 → **4.232**; academic 2 → 1.425; extracurricular 2 → 1.990. At the bottom of the scale the asymmetry reverses, and a weak academic rating is roughly three times as damaging as a weak extracurricular one.
      - Chetty et al., using waitlist variation across Ivy-Plus colleges: ~**30%** of the admissions advantage held by top-1% applicants is explained by stronger **non-academic** ratings; conditional on test scores, private-school students have **no higher academic ratings but much higher non-academic ratings**; non-academic ratings are uncorrelated or negatively correlated with post-college outcomes, while academic credentials predict them.
      - MIT's Dean of Admissions, in a post subtitled "You don't need to take up the crumhorn to look 'different'": "we don't value one type of activity over another… You don't have to set yourself apart from your classmates (common advice you might hear from college counselors)."
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Academics filter you in, and the activity rating sorts the kids who already passed that filter. The two add up rather than trade off, and that is the version of our thesis the data supports. Chetty is the uncomfortable half: part of what the non-academic rating measures is how well a school packages a student, and it does not predict how that student turns out.
   - **Link:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w29964 ; https://opportunityinsights.org/paper/diversifying-society-s-leaders/ ; https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/more_on_the_interview/

- **Source: Early-start evidence (chess) and the causal limits of the admissions literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - No study randomises or instruments an extracurricular pursuit, so there is no causal estimate of the effect of any activity on admission, for any activity.
      - Gobet & Campitelli (2007, N = 104) found the probability of reaching International Master was 1 in 4 for those starting serious chess by 12 versus 1 in 55 later, but the sample was recruited from a prestigious Buenos Aires club and is heavily title-enriched, so it is not a base rate for children.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The admissions data shows correlation, never cause. The chess numbers back an early start, but they come from a club full of titled players rather than from ordinary kids.
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.43.1.159
