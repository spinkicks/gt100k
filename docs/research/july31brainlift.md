# GT100K BrainLift: making it real by reading, sustaining, and proving the spike

## Owners

- Felipe Caicedo
- David Ordoñez


## Purpose

Based on our previous brainlifts, top school admissions demands three things. Every child clears an academic floor. Above that floor, a "spike," measured by whether the child comes back to it on their own. And that spike is proven out loud, defended in person, never merely handed in.

Simple enough on paper. Then one starts building, and the harder question surfaces: how does anyone run all of that against real children and real privacy law without wrecking the very thing they set out to protect?

That question is what follows. How does one tell what a child actually likes? How does one push hard without breaking them? How does one hand a child the record of their own work instead of quietly assembling a file on them? For each of these the market sells a shortcut, and each shortcut is seductive. Read the kid's face. Add badges. Assume hard work sorts itself out. Trust the log to explain why. All four are wrong. Most are wrong twice over, costing the school precisely as much as they cost the child.

Every SPOV below contradicts something the field currently believes, and survives anyway. The merely edgy ones were cut. So were the merely true.

### In scope

- Whether a child's interest will last, read from skill and from what they actually do. Never from what they say. Never from a face, a voice, or a pulse. And the limits of watching itself (SPOV 1, SPOV 2).
- What top colleges actually reward, taken from the court record rather than from consultant folklore (SPOV 6).
- Burnout as a number we watch and design against, never as the price of pushing kids hard.
- A record the child can read, wants to exist, and is never punished by.

### Out of scope

- The academic floor and its teaching, the case for a spike at all, and how families are chosen.
- Reading emotion off a child's face, voice, or pulse. Rejected on the evidence (SPOV 2), not explored.
- Final legal calls. The rules that bind the design are named here. Legal advice is not.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)


### On reading the child

- **Spiky POV 1: "Let them choose and watch what they pick" is a broken instrument at 9 to 12. And the tidy correction, "just build their skill," is broken too. Build skill because skill is the part one can build. Then watch a single thing: the child returning, unprompted.**
   - **Elaboration:** Consider the most intuitive instrument of all. Give the child a menu, then watch what they pick. Reasonable, is it not? Roughly 8,000 children across five countries were asked whether they played their sport by their own choice. More than 88% said yes. In every country. The researchers deleted the question outright. Why? Because it could not separate a child who adored the sport from one who would quit inside the month. The question measured nothing.

  What, then, does one measure? Skill. Skill, because it is the one thing an adult can actually build. Skill, because it can be judged without asking a child to narrate their own soul. And yet skill alone manufactures nothing; an 11-week program built precisely to raise it moved neither the children's competence nor their interest. The direct tests at this age split cleanly. Skill drives a child toward harder work, certainly. But telling a child they are good at something does not make them want it more, and simply letting them choose does. So the two sit side by side, neither above the other, differing only in what one can *do* with them. Skill is built and measured. Choice is protected. Watch the return, which skill feeds and control destroys.

  An objection, and a fair one: does this not condemn our own product? The discovery wall shows dozens of pursuits and invites a child to pick. It does not condemn it. The 88% finding rules out reading the pick; it says nothing whatsoever about offering one. A first click is exposure, nothing more, and it moves no estimate of ours. What moves the estimate is a child coming back days later with nobody asking them to, and a child canNOT come back to something they were never shown. The menu is the precondition. Mistake the thing they clicked for the signal, however, and the entire instrument collapses.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** two beliefs at once. That what a child picks reveals what they love. And the reflex correction, that skill-building is the master lever for interest that lasts.
   - **Backing:** Quested et al. (2013); Mabbe et al. (2018); Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008); Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Skjesol et al. (2025); the Childhood-and-Beyond cohort (2013); Review of Educational Research (2022) SDT meta-analysis. Honest limit: the two best direct tests at this age point in opposite directions. The negative claim, that free choice is a broken instrument, therefore stands on far firmer ground than any ranking of skill over choice.

- **Spiky POV 2: Behaviour is the only thing worth instrumenting. And behaviour still canNOT tell you *why*. So the software reports what it saw, then asks the guide for everything else.**
   - **Elaboration:** Here the design meets its most seductive temptation. If one cannot ask a child why, surely a camera can simply tell. It cannot. Emotion "cannot be reliably read from facial configurations," and since February of 2025 the EU AI Act has made the attempt illegal in schools outright. So we watch what a child does instead: what they come back to, how far they go, how long they stay. Measurable. Predictive of learning. Defensible.

  And most builders stop there, satisfied. They should not.

  Behaviour purchases far less than it appears to. It reveals what a child is drawn toward and nothing whatsoever about why, and the very authors who built these measures concede it of their own instrument. Worse follows. Praise inverts the signal. Adults with their ego on the line returned to a task *less* after being told they succeeded, and *more* after being told they failed, enjoying neither outcome. Nobody has run this on children, so we are extrapolating, and we say so plainly. But consider what it implies. A coach compliments a child on Tuesday. The child returns on Thursday. What does that return mean? Quite possibly the opposite of what we would assume. And we do not log what the adult said beside the return, so we canNOT see it happen. Recording it is the cheapest meaningful fix remaining.

  Then there is "over-motivated," the single state every operator most wants to catch. It does not appear in volume either. Across 94 studies, mostly of adults, healthy and unhealthy passion predict identical practice hours. Hours are the one quantity an adult can see. Hours are precisely the quantity that cannot separate the child to feed from the child to fear for.

  What survives is not a detector but a set of things one *runs*. Remove the activity and watch. Offer a genuine exit at no cost, which the attracted child declines and the entrapped child cannot even hear. And intervene on the adults, which is where the only controlled evidence on actual children sits: a workshop that changed how coaches behaved cut next-season dropout from 26% to 5%, and the teams won exactly as often.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** the wearable and camera pitch, that what a child feels can be sensed from their body. Then the harder one: that a detailed enough log of what a child did can reveal why they did it, which is the assumption sitting under most learning analytics. And the folk category "over-motivated," treated as one state you can spot.
   - **Backing:** Barrett et al. (2019); npj Digital Medicine (2025); EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f); Guay, Vallerand & Blanchard (2000); Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991); Fredricks, Blumenfeld & Paris (2004); Curran et al. (2015); Raedeke (1997); Smith, Smoll & Curtis (1979) and Barnett, Smoll & Smith (1992); Volpe, McConaughy & Hintze (2009). Honest limit: refusing the sensors rests on the firmest evidence in this document, and yet the sensors do work for narrow jobs under lab conditions. What breaks is the leap. From heart rate to emotion. From the lab to a real classroom. And excluding self-report is our own design choice rather than a finding, which is exactly what forces the software to say "here is what we saw, you decide."

### On sustaining the child

- **Spiky POV 3: Burnout is a number we watch and design against. "Good stress" is a myth. The early *start* is required. The early *lock-in* is not.**
   - **Elaboration:** The hothousing debate offers two answers, both bad. Push hard and damage the child, or keep them safe and mediocre. Neither survives contact with the evidence.

  Begin by separating what "early specialization" jams together. The early start holds, and we keep it. In chess, roughly 1 in 4 players who began serious study by 12 reached International Master, against 1 in 55 of those who began later. Music assumes the same, its graded ladders presupposing years of practice before the junior competitions open at 13.

  Early lock-in is another claim entirely, and it fails. What predicts rapid junior success sits near the *opposite* of what predicts senior success, and most elite performers do not narrow until mid-to-late adolescence. "Commit early or fall behind"? Unsupported. So: take the start, refuse the lock-in. This reverses a position we ourselves once held, and it is why the product carries several live hypotheses per child and why parking one is always reversible.

  Where does the damage come from, then? Not volume. Pressure. And a child whose sense of worth hangs on how they perform. In music schools, pressure raises dropout (β ≈ .19) while doing it for one's own reasons protects (β ≈ −.17). An encouraging parent is the strongest protection of all (β ≈ −.39), and coaching that leaves real choice predicts resilience (β ≈ .52).

  "Good stress" deserves its own burial. The famous inverted-U rests on a 1908 study of a handful of mice that never measured stress at all, and both challenge and hindrance stress track *worse* health. Hard-but-motivating is not free.

  What follows is operational, tracked like any other metric: validated burnout questionnaires (the School Burnout Inventory and its kin), a sleep floor of 9 to 12 hours for 6 to 12 year olds, practice distributed over time, scheduled light weeks, and teaching that leaves the child real choices. School burnout quadruples dropout risk. High-pressure environments run two to three times the population norms for anxiety and depression.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that the damage from intensity is either built in (so cap ambition) or trivial (so push harder). And that "good stress" is real at all.
   - **Backing:** Frontiers (2023) specialization review; Gobet & Campitelli (2007); Salmela-Aro et al. (2009); Luthar & Becker (2002); Curran & Hill (2019); the CBT/mindfulness burnout meta-analysis; the Yerkes-Dodson critique (2024). Honest limit: the chess figures come from a club stacked with titled players, so they are not the odds facing an ordinary child. No burnout questionnaire is validated at 8, meaning the youngest band needs a teacher or parent reporting instead. Borrowing "light weeks" from athletics and applying them to schoolwork is reasoning, not tested fact. And one review finds no direct link between specializing and burning out, which we read as the harm running through pressure rather than through workload.

### On the record the child builds

- **Spiky POV 4: The record must be legible to the child and wanted by them, or it is surveillance. Badges, points, and dashboards make it worse.**
   - **Elaboration:** A tamper-proof record of how a child actually worked: an asset, surely? Only if the child can read it and wants it to exist. Otherwise it is a file kept on them.

  The default EdTech answer is to gamify. Badge it, point it, dashboard it. On the evidence this backfires, and it backfires harder on children than on the college students these features were tested against. Rewards a child expects cut interest *more* in children than in adults. A controlled trial found digital badges *lowered* interest and met no need to feel capable whatsoever. A review of 38 studies found *no evidence* that dashboards raise achievement. Even encouraging messages *raised* anxiety in children already anxious.

  What helps, then? One thing. The child against their own past, specific enough to act on, never a leaderboard. Which rules out running totals, streaks, reward tokens, and every comparison between children.

  Nobody has tested a record like ours on this age group; we looked. But the adjacent research offers levers. Children of 9 to 11 grasp consent when it is put concretely and personally ("asking before taking," never "data sharing"), and they reason about their own records in terms of who holds power over them. Older students, left with the institution's default framing, describe being tracked as *surveillance* and lapse into resignation. That finding comes from secondary focus groups, so whether it reaches our band is untested. On disclosure, a simple AI-use label beat a detailed one, and the fear of being accused of cheating climbs with grade level.

  So the record is built to be understood and owned: plain words, self-comparison, disclosure that is easy and carries no shame, and nothing engineered to keep a child in the app. The same discipline that governs the signal layer.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that badges, points, leaderboards, and dashboards are safe, motivating defaults for children. And that a richer, more detailed record is automatically a more trustworthy one.
   - **Backing:** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Balci & Morris (2026); the 38-study dashboard review (2023) and the 2025 learning-analytics meta-analyses; MyReadscape; CHI-2026 child-trust study; Agesilaou & Kyza (2022); Stoilova, Livingstone & Nandagiri (2020); the Australian school-datafication focus groups; the ICO Children's Code and COPPA. Honest limit: not one study exists testing a learning record, a credential, or an authorship screen on 8 to 14 year olds. We looked. Everything above is borrowed from adjacent research, which makes this the one pillar the program will have to evidence itself.

- **Spiky POV 5: Every part of the EvidenceGraph is off the shelf. The moat is the composition, plus the in-person defense no AI-only competitor can run. And the hard problem is proving deletion, not proving nobody tampered.**
   - **Elaboration:** Two opposite assumptions, both wrong.

  The first: surely someone has built this. No one has. No product combines content-addressed provenance, signed attestations, transparency logging, and verifiable credentials into a single tamper-evident record of how a child learned. That composition appears genuinely novel.

  The second: surely the hard part is proving the work is real. It is not. That part is solved, commodity, free. W3C Verifiable Credentials, Open Badges 3.0 and CLR 2.0, in-toto and SLSA attestations, Rekor and Trillian transparency logs, RO-Crate mapped to W3C PROV, C2PA content credentials, sentence-by-sentence authorship replay from Grammarly. All mature. All reusable. And not one of them proves a human did the work unaided; C2PA says as much in its own specification. So trust rests where a real academy already stands: a five-minute defense, in person, on a sample. A remote competitor holding nothing but the finished file canNOT run it, and the check grows more valuable every year that AI makes files easier to fake.

  The genuinely hard problem inverts the crypto framing entirely. It is proving deletion from a log built never to change. Our answer keeps only fingerprints on the permanent log and puts the erasable child data in a separately keyed store.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that this is either already built, or that its hard part is proving the work authentic.
   - **Backing:** Open Badges 3.0 / CLR 2.0 / W3C VC; in-toto, SLSA, Sigstore; Workflow-Run RO-Crate ↔ PROV; C2PA 2.1; EDPB Guidelines 02/2025; Weber-Wulff et al. (2023); Liang et al. (2023); Zhang et al. (2024). Honest limit: we call it novel because we could not find it, which is an argument from absence and does not rule out a private build. And the evidence for oral defenses comes largely from universities rather than from schools.

### On proving the spike was worth building

- **Spiky POV 6: The spike does not beat the academic floor. It sorts the children who have already cleared it.**
   - **Elaboration:** The pitch-deck version insists a spiky profile outranks good scores. The litigation record says something narrower, and far more useful.

  Harvard scores four separate dimensions, and discovery lets one compare them. Applicants with a 2 across all four (academics, activities, personal, athletic) were admitted at 68%. Applicants with a top activity rating and nothing else top: 48%. Skim that and breadth appears to win.

  It does not. Every row conditions on "no other 1s," which pits a spike from a child *without* the grades against a well-rounded child. Our profile, strong academics plus one exceptional thing, never appears in the table at all. And on that profile the model is additive rather than substitutive: a top academic rating carries 4.094, a top activity rating 4.232, both enormous, and only 7% of the pool earns a 2 or better on three dimensions.

  Two findings outrank the admit rates entirely. First: the rubric grades level, never domain. It defines a 1 as "national-level achievement… truly unusual achievement" and enumerates no approved activity anywhere. The folk hierarchy of prestigious pursuits simply is not in the instrument. Second: domain does bite, but only at verification. Academic competitions trigger a second rating. Music, art, and making receive faculty portfolio review. Athletes get recruited. That machinery exists for some pursuits and not others. Prestige, then, is a question of who can check your claim. Not of what a reader is told to value. The EvidenceGraph closes exactly that gap.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** the admissions-consulting orthodoxy that a spike outranks a strong all-round profile. And the opposite reflex from school counselors, that activities are decoration on a transcript. And the assumption, widespread in this market, that a spike counts only if the subject is prestigious.
   - **Backing:** Harvard Reading Procedures, Class of 2023 (HARV00097939); Card expert report Exhibit 4; Arcidiacono, Kinsler & Ransom (2022); Chetty, Deming & Friedman (2025); Stu Schmill, MIT Admissions (2006). **Honest limit:** every figure here is a correlation. The rating is one reader's judgment of an entire application, not an attribute of a child, so improving a pursuit and improving the number a reader writes down are two different operations. No study establishes causation for any activity. And nothing in this literature observes anyone under 17, meaning nothing here supports the link from starting at six to standing out at seventeen. Which is the premise the whole program rests on.

## DOK 3: Insights

What we concluded after working through the sources. These connect the Knowledge Tree to the SPOVs.

### Reading the child

- **Insight 1:** Free choice is a near-constant, not a signal. When more than 88% of children endorse "this was my choice" regardless of whether they are about to quit, self-selection cannot rank anything. A program built on "watch what they pick" is measuring noise. Skill and the unprompted return are what remain: buildable, measurable, real. (→ SPOV 1)
- **Insight 2:** Skill and choice are jointly necessary, and at 9 to 12 neither outranks the other. They differ only in what one can do about them. Skill is built and assessed. Choice is protected, never manufactured. So the instrument aims at skill plus return under protected choice, rather than at drilling. Does building skill create interest? It does not, and an 11-week program proved it by moving neither. Skill "wins" for one reason only: we can build it and measure it. (→ SPOV 1, 3)
- **Insight 3:** In the youngest children, what a child can *actually* do predicts later engagement better than what they believe they can do. Asking a 9 year old to rate their own competence is therefore a weak proxy, and the signal should lean on observed performance instead. (→ SPOV 1, 2)
- **Insight 4:** Inner state cannot be read from the body, and in EU schools attempting it is illegal. Behaviour, however, is readable and predictive. The field discards "engagement detection" as a single idea when it is plainly two: faces and pulses (invalid) and what a child does (valid). We live on the valid side of that line. (→ SPOV 2)

### Sustaining the child

- **Insight 5:** The achievement case for locking in early is weaker than assumed, since what produces a fast junior sits near the opposite of what produces a great adult. So we keep the start, drop the commitment, and surrender nothing in achievement to do it. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 6:** Burnout runs through pressure and through a child tying their worth to results. Not through hours. Pressure raises dropout; doing it for one's own reasons protects. Which means a program can hold expectations high and still attack the channel that does the damage. The same choice-versus-control split decides whether a parent's involvement helps or harms. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 7:** Stress cannot be assumed good for children. The inverted-U is folklore, and both kinds of stress track worse health, so nothing permits us to excuse pressure by calling it motivating. Sleep, spacing, and light weeks are design elements. Not niceties. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 8:** Validated burnout questionnaires work from roughly 9 or 10 upward, and none work by self-report at 8, so the youngest band needs a parent or teacher reporting instead. And any single cutoff belongs only to the population it was built on, which is why we watch several sources rather than waiting for one alarm to sound. (→ SPOV 3)

### The record the child builds

- **Insight 9:** Gamification is a trap for this age band. Rewards cut interest *more* in children than in adults, badges can cut it outright, and dashboards raise achievement not at all. So the record restrains motivational features rather than piling them on. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 10:** Exactly one progress view helps: the child against their own past, specific enough to act on, never a leaderboard. And encouragement is not free either, since motivational prompts raise anxiety in children already anxious. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 11:** We searched, and no study exists testing a learning record, credential, or authorship screen on 8 to 14 year olds. So this remains an open design problem. The adjacent research on children and data still points somewhere. Speak concretely and personally ("asking before taking"). Expect the institution's default framing to read as *surveillance*, because children stop believing they have any say. Keep disclosure simple, since a plain AI-use label beat a detailed one. And treat readability as the designer's duty rather than something a teacher patches later. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 12:** The provenance stack is commodity; the integration is the novelty. Every layer already exists and is battle-tested. Ours is the composition for a child, paired with an in-person defense that a competitor holding only the finished artifact canNOT run. (→ SPOV 5)
- **Insight 13:** The hard problem is inverted from the marketing. Tamper-evidence is commodity. The hard part is *proving deletion from a log built never to change*, which our architecture aims at and has not yet built. The competitive scan confirms it as the frontier. (→ SPOV 5)

### Proving the spike was worth building

- **Insight 14:** The comparison everyone quotes is the wrong comparison. "Four 2s beat a single top activity rating" sounds like breadth beating depth. But every row in that table conditions on *no other top ratings*, so what it actually contrasts is a spike from a child without the grades against a well-rounded child. Our profile, grades plus one exceptional thing, never appears. And the coefficients make the two additive rather than substitutive. The spike sorts the children who already have the academics. (→ SPOV 6)
- **Insight 15:** The rubric grades *level, never domain*, and enumerates no approved activity anywhere. Domain prestige is real, certainly, but it lives in *who can check your claim* rather than in what a reader is told to value: a second rating axis for academic competitions, faculty portfolio review for music, art, and making, recruiting for athletes. Which turns the EvidenceGraph from a nice-to-have into the direct answer, since it supplies verification for pursuits that have none. (→ SPOV 6, SPOV 5)

### Reading motivation

- **Insight 16:** Refusing the sensors bought less than it appeared to. What we kept in their place, watching what a child does, *also* cannot name a motivational state, and the theory's own authors concede it of their own instrument. So the honest architecture is a division of labour rather than a better inference. The log carries behaviour. The guide carries everything else. And the software asks instead of concluding. (→ SPOV 2)
- **Insight 17:** Adult praise can invert our core signal. For the internally pressured child, returning rises after a setback and falls after success, which means the same return carries opposite meanings depending on what an adult said last session. A return canNOT be read without knowing what was said and what else the child could have chosen. So both are logged beside every return. (→ SPOV 2, SPOV 1)
- **Insight 18:** "Over-motivated" is not one state, and the axis an adult would use to detect it carries no information whatsoever. Healthy and obsessive passion track practice hours indistinguishably, obsessive marginally higher. So the deliverable a stakeholder asks for, a quadrant keyed on intensity, cannot be built. Worse, it would fail in a predictable direction: flagging the absorbed children, missing the pressured ones. What replaces it is probes the guide *runs*, plus intervention aimed at the adults. Which is also where the only controlled evidence on children sits. (→ SPOV 2, SPOV 3)

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

The sources, plus the facts and summaries the insights are built on. DOK 1 facts come straight from the source. DOK 2 summaries are in our own words.

### Category 1: Reading the child

#### Subcategory 1.1: Competence versus free choice as a durable-liking signal

- **Source: Quested et al. (2013), youth-sport motivation across five countries**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - N ≈ 8,000 youth players, mean age 11.56, five countries.
      - The free-choice "this is my choice" item was endorsed by more than 88% of children in every country and was dropped for non-discrimination.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - When nearly every child endorses the same item, the item separates nobody. It cannot distinguish the child who will stay from the child who quits within the month. Which is precisely why we treat free choice as a weak signal.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2013_Quested_et_al.pdf

- **Source: Meta-analysis, Review of Educational Research (2022), SDT needs and motivation**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 144 samples, 79,079 participants (sample mean age 16.55).
      - Relative weights for self-determined motivation: competence 43.21%, autonomy 34.49%, relatedness 22.31%; competence-IM and autonomy-IM correlations near-equal (ρ .58 vs .57).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Of the three needs, skill carries the largest share. Which supports leading with skill, while keeping choice close behind.
   - **Link:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8935530/

- **Source: Finnish 6-year longitudinal (J. Science and Medicine in Sport, 2015); Barnett et al. (2008); actual-vs-perceived competence (PubMed 29016237)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Finnish study (n=333, baseline mean age 12.4, Grade 7→12): Grade-7 perceived competence predicted later physical activity (β = .28 total METs, .29 vigorous); Grade-7 autonomous motivation and enjoyment were *not* significant predictors.
      - Barnett et al. (2008): childhood object-control skill predicted adolescent activity (18% variance) and fitness (30%) ~6 years later; adding the perceived-competence path nearly doubled R².
      - Actual-vs-perceived (N=201, ages 6–9): actual motor competence explained 9–30% of activity variance vs 0–5% for perceived competence.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Skill predicts whether a child keeps going better than motivation or enjoyment does, and in the youngest children what they can *actually* do beats what they believe they can do. Hence building on skill. Hence the weakness of asking a young child to rate themselves.
   - **Link:** (Barnett/PASS and Finnish longitudinal as cited in the 2026-07-29 competence research report)

- **Source: Fresh 9–12 deep-research (2026-08-01): Mabbe et al. (2018); Patall et al. (2008); Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999); Skjesol et al. (2025); Childhood-and-Beyond cohort (2013)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Mabbe et al. (2018, n=110, mean age 10.71): competence satisfaction predicted challenge-seeking (b = .31, p < .001) while autonomy satisfaction did not (b = .01, ns); competence manipulation partial η² = .65 on competence satisfaction and .33 on intrinsic motivation, vs .07–.08 for the autonomy manipulation.
      - Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999): positive verbal reward/feedback effect on free-choice intrinsic motivation d = 0.11 (ns) for children vs d = 0.43 for college students; age difference Qb(1) = 5.14, p < .02.
      - Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008): choice raises intrinsic motivation d = 0.30 overall and d = 0.51 for primary-school students; a 2019 experiment (N=126, mean age 10.8) found the choice effect mediated *jointly* by autonomy and competence.
      - Skjesol et al. (2025, RCT n=175, ages 9–12): an 11-week competence-oriented soccer program raised neither perceived competence nor interest/value; interest/enjoyment declined significantly in both arms.
      - Childhood-and-Beyond (2013): child perceived competence predicted the *amount* of later participation, not *whether* the child continued; only parental influence predicted continuation.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The direct tests at 9 to 12 split. Skill drives a child toward harder work; being told they are skilled does not raise their desire to continue, and being given a choice does. So the two run parallel, both necessary, neither above the other. Skill's sole advantage is that it can be built and measured, and building it manufactures no lasting interest on its own.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2018_MabbeSoenensVansteenkiste_ExperimentalChildPsych.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint7040101

#### Subcategory 1.2: Why inner state cannot be read from the body

- **Source: Barrett et al. (2019), "Emotional expressions reconsidered"; EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) / Recital 44**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Emotion "cannot be reliably read from facial configurations"; variability is the norm.
      - The EU AI Act prohibits emotion inference in education (in force 2 February 2025).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Reading emotion off a face has no scientific basis, and in EU schools it is illegal besides. So we never do it.
   - **Link:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100619832930

- **Source: rPPG skin-tone bias (npj Digital Medicine, 2025); voice-stress literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Remote photoplethysmography mean absolute error ~4.23 bpm (Fitzpatrick I–V) rising to ~13.58 bpm (type VI), >2× degradation; "in the wild" correlation with true heart rate only r ≈ 0.58.
      - Voice-stress analysis is characterized as essentially pseudoscience.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Even where a body sensor partly works, it works worse on darker skin and falls apart outside the lab. And the leap from a heart rate to "stressed" has no support whatsoever. Behaviour, then, is the only defensible instrument.
   - **Link:** (npj Digital Medicine 2025 rPPG study, as cited in the 2026-07-21 burnout research report)

#### Subcategory 1.3: Why behaviour cannot name a motivational state

- **Source: Guay, Vallerand & Blanchard (2000); Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991); Fredricks, Blumenfeld & Paris (2004)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - On the free-choice measure, SDT's own authors: it "is unidimensional and consequently cannot assess other important motivational concepts derived from self-determination theory, namely identified regulation, external regulation, and amotivation."
      - Ryan, Koestner & Deci (1991), three experiments: after **positive** feedback, ego-involved participants persisted **less** than task-involved ones; after nonconfirming or no feedback they persisted **more**; in neither case did they report interest, enjoyment or perceived choice.
      - Fredricks et al.: "no distinction is made between effort aimed merely at fulfilling behavioral expectations and that aimed at understanding the material and mastering the content."
      - Skinner et al. (2008, N = 805, grades 4–7): emotional components of engagement drove later changes in behavioural ones; feedback from behaviour to emotion was not as consistent.
      - Volpe, McConaughy & Hintze (2009, ages 6–11): for on-task behaviour, person variance was 10.6% and person-by-occasion plus residual 70.4%; ~14 sessions were needed for a dependable person-level estimate.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - What separates one kind of motivation from another is the reason for acting, and the reason is nowhere in the log. The field's one behavioural measure is documented as unable to tell them apart. Worse still: a return means the opposite thing depending on what an adult said last, so praise inverts our core signal, and we do not yet record what was said beside it. Behaviour also lags by construction. Feeling moves first.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2000_GuayVallerandBlanchard_MO.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00995170

- **Source: Curran, Hill, Appleton, Vallerand & Standage (2015); Raedeke (1997); Smith, Smoll & Curtis (1979) and Barnett, Smoll & Smith (1992)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Meta-analysis of the passion literature, 94 studies and 1,308 effect sizes: harmonious and obsessive passion correlate with deliberate practice to a statistically indistinguishable degree (Hotelling's T = .48, ns); obsessive correlates **slightly more** with hours per week; obsessive passion is **unrelated to objective performance**. The only large clean separation is on activity dependence, itself a questionnaire.
      - Curran et al. (2011, n = 149 junior soccer players, mean age 16.2): harmonious and obsessive passion correlated **r = .73**, and obsessive passion was **unrelated to athlete burnout**.
      - The child version of the Passion Scale (Mageau et al. 2009, n = 163, mean age 11) ran α = .60 harmonious and .66 obsessive.
      - Raedeke (1997, 236 age-group swimmers): attraction ("I want to be here") and entrapment ("I have to be here") produce the same attendance record; entrapped clusters carried elevated burnout.
      - Coach Effectiveness Training (18 Little League coaches, ~ages 10–12): next-season attrition **5% in the trained group versus 26% in control**, with no difference in win-loss records; the largest self-esteem gains went to the children who started lowest.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Hours are the one quantity an adult can see. Hours are precisely the quantity that cannot separate healthy from unhealthy. So any framework keyed on intensity misclassifies in both directions at once. What a program can do instead is run probes rather than await signals: remove the activity, offer a genuine exit. And intervene on the adults, where the only controlled evidence on children sits, and where the dependable change is subtraction rather than addition.
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-015-9503-0 ; https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.19.4.396

### Category 2: Sustaining the child

#### Subcategory 2.1: Burnout as a measurable variable, and the "good stress" myth

- **Source: Salmela-Aro et al. (2009), School Burnout Inventory**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 10 items, 3 factors (exhaustion, cynicism, inadequacy); original N = 1,418 Finnish adolescents; three-factor fit RMSEA .06, CFI .96.
      - School burnout is associated with roughly 4× higher dropout risk.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - A short, validated questionnaire means burnout becomes a number we track rather than a thing we guess at. Though its floor sits near 9 or 10, not 8.
   - **Link:** https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/abs/10.1027/1015-5759.25.1.48

- **Source: Yerkes-Dodson critique (Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2024); challenge/hindrance stress literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - The inverted-U "optimal arousal" law rests on a 1908 study using 2–4 mice that never measured arousal.
      - Both challenge and hindrance stressors relate negatively to psychological strain and physical health.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Stress cannot be assumed good. Rest and load management are requirements in a program this intense, never niceties.
   - **Link:** (Yerkes-Dodson critique, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 2024, as cited in the 2026-07-21 burnout research report)

- **Source: Luthar & Becker (2002); Curran & Hill (2019); CBT/mindfulness burnout meta-analysis (5 RCTs)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Affluent/high-pressure youth run 2–3× population norms for anxiety, depression, and substance use.
      - Socially-prescribed perfectionism rose ~33% from the late 1980s to the late 2010s.
      - Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (ages 11–23): CBT/mindfulness reduced exhaustion d = −0.87, sport devaluation d = −0.77, reduced accomplishment d = −0.74; CBT outperformed mindfulness for exhaustion.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The risk is real, rising, and treatable. So the safeguards are built in from the start rather than bolted on after a child is hurt.
   - **Link:** https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000138.pdf

#### Subcategory 2.2: Long-term risks of early specialization, and what protects

- **Source: Frontiers in Sports & Active Living (2023), early- vs late-specialization review**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Predictors of rapid junior performance and of long-term senior performance are largely opposite; most elite athletes specialize in mid-to-late adolescence.
      - One specialization-exhaustion study (n=169, mean age 16.29) found higher emotional/physical exhaustion in specialized athletes (p = .030, d = −0.34).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Reaching the top does not require picking one thing early, so we keep the start and engineer the lock-in out. One review disagrees, finding no direct link between specializing and burning out, which we reconcile as harm running through pressure rather than through the specializing itself.
   - **Link:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1175718/full

- **Source: Music-school dropout/motivation study; autonomy-supportive coaching SEM; Saarinen (2025)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Controlled motivation raised dropout (β = 0.19); autonomous motivation was protective (β = −0.17); encouraging parental involvement was the strongest protective factor (β = −0.39).
      - Autonomy-supportive coaching predicted psychological resilience (β = 0.522).
      - ~50% of musical participants drop out by age 17; parental over-involvement showed a U-shaped harm pattern (Saarinen 2025).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Whether an intense pursuit harms a child depends on how much say they have and why they are doing it. Not on hours. Too little push from a parent harms; so does too much. Which is the direct basis for teaching that leaves real choices, and for screening families for the warm-but-demanding kind.
   - **Link:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1469029225000305

### Category 3: The record the child builds

#### Subcategory 3.1: How children and families experience evidence and credential systems

- **Source: Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Balci & Morris (2026) badge RCT; 38-study dashboard review (2023); 2025 learning-analytics meta-analyses**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Expected, contingent tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, and the effect is *more* severe for children than college students (Deci et al. 1999).
      - A digital-badge RCT (n=210) found badges reduced intrinsic motivation and satisfied no competence need (sample: college students).
      - A 38-study systematic review found *no evidence* dashboards improve academic achievement; 2025 meta-analyses find ES ≈ 0.36 (23 studies, 9,710 participants), heavily moderated by developmental level.
      - Motivational "task-value" prompts increased anxiety among already-anxious learners.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The standard badges-and-dashboard playbook is counter-productive at this age, so the record restrains motivational features and shows a child only their own progress.
   - **Link:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506824 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042

- **Source: MyReadscape (Nature HSS Communications); Molenaar et al. (LAK 2020); ICO Children's Code; COPPA**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - MyReadscape (N=30, ages ~7–9): a public leaderboard motivated 79% of interviewed children but fostered only rank-based competition; badges had the weakest motivational impact of all features.
      - Molenaar et al.: personalized trace-data visualizations improved accuracy and transfer for ages ~7–9.
      - COPPA governs under-13; the UK Children's Code (Age Appropriate Design Code) sets age-appropriate defaults.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Self-comparison helps. Badges and competition do not. And all of it sits inside child-data rules that bind us. So legibility and self-comparison are the design targets, never ranking.
   - **Link:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-06750-x ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3375462.3375465 ; https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/

- **Source: Fresh deep-research (2026-08-01): CHI-2026 child-trust study; Agesilaou & Kyza (2022); Stoilova/Livingstone/Nandagiri (2020); Australian school-datafication focus groups; CDT K-12 AI survey**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - No located study tests a learning-record, digital-credential, data-provenance, or authorship-tracking UI with children aged 8–14 on legibility/motivation/stigma; the one AI-non-disclosure-shame study (survey n=97 + interviews) is on college students.
      - CHI-2026 child-trust study (N=289, ages 9–11): children engage with consent only when framed concretely ("asking before taking," not "data sharing") and object to systems "taking things without asking."
      - Agesilaou & Kyza (2022, n=63 fifth/sixth-graders): children misunderstood shared/platform ownership of their own self-generated data.
      - Australian secondary focus groups (n=62): students framed school datafication through "power, surveillance and affect," reporting "digital resignation" and perceived powerlessness; CDT survey: ~half of students said classroom AI use made them feel less connected to their teacher.
      - Disclosure UI: a simple AI-use label outperformed a detailed one (adolescent sample); cheating-accusation anxiety rises with grade level.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The gap in direct evidence is confirmed. But the adjacent research on children and data still yields levers: concrete personal language, nothing that reads as being watched, simple disclosure, readability as the designer's duty. Which is why this pillar is one we must design and measure ourselves rather than copy.
   - **Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790765 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212868922000058 ; https://www.cogitatiopress.com/mediaandcommunication/article/view/3407

#### Subcategory 3.2: Verifying authorship in the AI era

- **Source: Weber-Wulff et al. (2023); Liang et al. (2023); Krishna et al. (2023); OpenAI/Turnitin/Vanderbilt**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 14 detectors across 754 cases were "neither accurate nor reliable"; none exceeded 80% accuracy.
      - Seven GPT detectors misclassified non-native (TOEFL) English at an average 61.3% false-positive rate; all seven flagged 19.8% of TOEFL essays.
      - Paraphrasing dropped DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% accuracy at a fixed 1% false-positive rate.
      - OpenAI's own classifier (26% true-positive, 9% false-positive) was discontinued; Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector (Aug 2023), estimating ~750 wrongly-flagged papers/year at claimed rates.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - AI text detectors are inaccurate, trivially defeated, and worst for the students with the least protection. So one must never trigger a consequence for a child. The 2026 evidence reconfirms it.
   - **Link:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z ; https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(23)00130-7

- **Source: Zhang et al. (2024, ICML); C2PA specification; interactive oral assessment (Dawson; Sotiriadou et al. 2020)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Strong watermarking of generative text is formally impossible under natural assumptions.
      - C2PA attests who signed, when, and with what tool, but "makes no judgment about whether provenance is 'true'" and does not attest human authorship.
      - Interactive oral assessment demonstrated resilience to misconduct including generative-AI misuse; the evidence base is mostly higher-education.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - No test on the finished artifact proves a human wrote it. So trust rests on process plus a sampled defense, which is the one check an in-person academy is built to run.
   - **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04378 ; https://philldawson.com/

### Category 4: Proving the spike was worth building

#### Subcategory 4.1: What elite admissions rewards

- **Source: *SFFA v. Harvard* discovery. Reading Procedures, Class of 2023 (HARV00097939); Card expert report; Findings of Fact (D. Mass. 2019)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Verbatim extracurricular rating: "**1.** Unusual strength in one or more areas. Possible national-level achievement or professional experience… Truly unusual achievement. **2.** Strong secondary school contribution in one or more areas such as class president, newspaper editor, concertmaster… **3.** Solid participation but without special distinction." The scale enumerates **no** qualifying activity anywhere.
      - Card Exhibit 4, six years of domestic applicants: academic 1 with no other 1s → 68%; **extracurricular 1 with no other 1s → 48%**; four ratings of 2 → 68%; athletic 1 with no other 1s → 88%; **no rating of 1 or 2 on any dimension (n ≈ 56,000) → 0.1%**.
      - Card Exhibit 5: ~42% of applicants earn an academic 1 or 2, but fewer than 25% earn a 1 or 2 on each of the other three dimensions; only **7%** earn a 2 or better on at least three.
      - A separate **academic** rating rewards "possible national or international level recognition in academic competitions", so one academic-competition result scores on two axes.
      - Supplementary faculty review exists for **music, art, dance and academic** work only; MIT offers exactly four portfolios (Maker, Music & Theater Arts, Visual Arts & Architecture, Research). The Common App has 29 activity categories and none for games, puzzles or tabletop.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The rubric grades attainment, never activity type, so there is no approved-subject list to satisfy. And the 68 versus 48 gap is not breadth beating depth: every row conditions on "no other 1s," pitting a spike from a child *without* the grades against a well-rounded child. Our profile never appears. Where domain does bite is verification, since the machinery for confirming a claim exists for some pursuits and not others. An evidence record supplies it.
   - **Link:** https://samv91khoyt2i553a2t1s05i-wpengine.netdna-ssl.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Doc-421-1-Card-Report.pdf

- **Source: Arcidiacono, Kinsler & Ransom (2022); Chetty, Deming & Friedman (*QJE*, 2025); Stu Schmill, MIT Admissions (2006)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Logit coefficients on admission: academic rating 1 → **4.094**; extracurricular rating 1 → **4.232**; academic 2 → 1.425; extracurricular 2 → 1.990. At the bottom of the scale the asymmetry reverses, and a weak academic rating is roughly three times as damaging as a weak extracurricular one.
      - Chetty et al., using waitlist variation across Ivy-Plus colleges: ~**30%** of the admissions advantage held by top-1% applicants is explained by stronger **non-academic** ratings; conditional on test scores, private-school students have **no higher academic ratings but much higher non-academic ratings**; non-academic ratings are uncorrelated or negatively correlated with post-college outcomes, while academic credentials predict them.
      - MIT's Dean of Admissions, in a post subtitled "You don't need to take up the crumhorn to look 'different'": "we don't value one type of activity over another… You don't have to set yourself apart from your classmates (common advice you might hear from college counselors)."
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Academics filter. The activity rating sorts those who clear the filter. The two are additive rather than substitutive, which is the version of our thesis the data actually supports. Chetty is the uncomfortable half: part of what the non-academic rating measures is a school's skill at packaging a student, and it does not predict how that student turns out.
   - **Link:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w29964 ; https://opportunityinsights.org/paper/diversifying-society-s-leaders/ ; https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/more_on_the_interview/

- **Source: Early-start evidence (chess) and the causal limits of the admissions literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - No study randomises or instruments an extracurricular pursuit, so there is no causal estimate of the effect of any activity on admission, for any activity.
      - Gobet & Campitelli (2007, N = 104) found the probability of reaching International Master was 1 in 4 for those starting serious chess by 12 versus 1 in 55 later, but the sample was recruited from a prestigious Buenos Aires club and is heavily title-enriched, so it is not a base rate for children.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The admissions data is correlational, never causal. The chess figures support an early start, while cautioning that they come from a club stacked with titled players rather than from ordinary children.
   - **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.43.1.159
