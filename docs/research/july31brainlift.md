# GT100K BrainLift.3: Making It Real by Reading, Sustaining, and Proving the Spike at Scale

## Owners

- Felipe Caicedo
- David Ordoñez


## Purpose

BrainLift.1 argued the program's shape (the floor, the family, grouping, early specialization, friction). BrainLift.2 argued the two pillars beyond the floor: build a spike measured by voluntary return, and prove it from process plus a human defense. The family brainlift argued whom to admit. This one asks the question you only meet once you start building: **what does it take to run those convictions against real children, real scale, and real regulation without the design betraying them?**

The answer is a set of positions about *how*. How we read a child's interest. How we keep an intensive path from breaking them. How we make the evidence record theirs instead of a surveillance file, and how we select into the program and serve it at scale. The market sells a shortcut at every one of these: read the face, gamify the record, buy the biggest model, reach for a "fairer" test, trust that intensity regulates itself. Each is wrong on the evidence, and most are wrong twice, worse for the child and worse for the operator. The honest, autonomy-preserving path is also the cheap and legal one.

Each SPOV below breaks with a live consensus and holds up anyway. Where a claim was only edgy, or only true, we cut it.

### In scope

- Reading a durable-interest signal from **competence and behavior**, not from self-report or from a child's face, voice, or pulse.
- Treating **burnout as a monitored operating variable** an intensive program can measure and design against, not an accepted cost of ambition.
- Making the **evidence record legible, motivating, and non-punitive** to a child, and being honest about where the provenance stack ends.
- **Equitable identification** into the program, and the real **economics of serving AI tutoring** at 100,000-student scale.

### Out of scope

- The academic floor and its pedagogy (BrainLift.1), the two-pillar argument itself (BrainLift.2), and the family-ranking case (the family brainlift). We assume them and do not re-litigate them.
- Biometric affect/engagement detection as a signal. We reject it on the evidence (SPOV 2) rather than exploring it.
- Final legal or compliance determinations. We name the regulatory boundaries that bind the design; we do not issue legal advice.

## DOK 4: Spiky Points of View (SPOVs)

They group by pillar of the running system, with a unifying idea at the end.

### On reading the child

- **Spiky POV 1: "Give them free choice and watch what they pick" is a broken instrument at 9–12. The fix is not "competence beats autonomy"; competence is the lever you build, and voluntary return is the signal you watch.**
   - **Elaboration:** The intuitive read on a child's passion is "let them pick, and watch what they pick." BrainLift.2 replaced *stated* interest with *voluntary return*. The fresh evidence sharpens this against both the naive view and the tidy correction. First, free choice is a broken *instrument* at this age. In an ~8,000-child, five-country youth-sport study the "this is my choice" item drew endorsement from more than 88% of children in every country, so uniformly that the researchers dropped it: it could not tell a child who loved the sport from one about to quit. We then had to walk our own draft back. The reflex fix, "so just build competence, competence wins," does not hold at 9–12 either. The direct tests split. Felt competence, not autonomy, drove challenge-seeking in 10–11-year-olds (Mabbe 2018, competence b ≈ .31 vs autonomy b ≈ .01). Positive competence feedback does *not* raise children's free-choice intrinsic motivation the way it does adults' (Deci/Koestner/Ryan 1999, d = 0.11 ns vs 0.43). Free choice *does* raise intrinsic motivation at primary age (Patall 2008, d ≈ 0.51). And an 11-week competence-oriented program moved neither competence nor interest (Skjesol 2025 RCT, n = 175, interest fell in both arms). In the one cohort that tracked it, a child's perceived competence predicted the *depth* of later participation but not *whether* they continued; only parental influence did that. So the honest claim is narrower than "competence beats free choice." Competence and autonomy are parallel and jointly necessary, and they differ in what you can *do* with them. **Competence is the lever you build and can measure, autonomy is the condition you protect, and the durable signal to instrument is voluntary return, which competence feeds and control destroys.** Stop treating "we gave them options" as evidence of passion, and stop treating a skills drill as if it manufactures one.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** both "self-selection reveals the passion," and the SDT-lite reflex that competence-building is the master lever for durable interest.
   - **Backing:** Quested et al. (2013, N≈8,000, mean age 11.56) on free-choice non-discrimination; Mabbe et al. (2018, n=110, mean age 10.71) on competence over autonomy for challenge-seeking; Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008) on choice raising intrinsic motivation (primary-school d ≈ 0.51); Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) on competence feedback *not* enhancing children's free-choice motivation; Skjesol et al. (2025, RCT n=175, ages 9–12) null; the Childhood-and-Beyond cohort (child competence → depth, parents → continuation); the Review of Educational Research (2022) SDT meta (competence 43.2% / autonomy 34.5% / relatedness 22.3%, 144 samples, 79,079 participants). Honest limit: the fresh deep-research *weakened* this SPOV rather than strengthening it. The two best direct tests at 9–12 point in opposite directions, several key results are single-cohort, and "voluntary return as the signal" is a synthesis across sport, music, and classroom work, not a validated 9–12 discovery metric. What survives is the *negative* claim (free choice doesn't discriminate; skills drills don't manufacture interest); any positive *ranking* of competence over autonomy does not.

- **Spiky POV 2: You cannot read a child's inner state from their face, voice, gaze, or pulse, so we instrument behavior and never biometrics.**
   - **Elaboration:** The affective-computing pitch is that a camera or a wearable can sense a child's interest, engagement, or stress. It cannot, and in the EU it is illegal to try in a school. Emotion "cannot be reliably read from facial configurations"; variability is the norm. Remote photoplethysmography (reading pulse from video) degrades more than 2× on the darkest skin (mean absolute error ~4.2 bpm on lighter tones to ~13.6 bpm on Fitzpatrick VI) and correlates only ~0.58 with true heart rate "in the wild," and even a valid pulse is not a valid *stress* readout. Voice-stress analysis is, in the literature's words, pseudoscience. The EU AI Act prohibits emotion inference in education (Article 5(1)(f), in force 2 February 2025). So the signal layer reads what a child *does*: what they return to, how deep they go, how long they stay. Those we can measure, and they predict learning. What a child's body is said to broadcast, we leave alone. Of every claim in this document, this one rests on the firmest evidence.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** the wellness-wearable and engagement-camera premise that inner state is senseable from the body.
   - **Backing:** Barrett et al. (2019), *Emotional expressions reconsidered*; the npj Digital Medicine (2025) rPPG skin-tone-bias finding; EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) / Recital 44; behavioral-engagement evidence carried over from BrainLift.2 (Fredricks 2004; Saqr 2022, behavioral traces r ≈ .3–.5). Honest limit: these sensors *are* valid for narrow targets under controlled conditions (resting heart rate, cognitive load). The invalidity is the leap to emotion, stress, or interest and to real-world child conditions, the leap we refuse to make.

### On sustaining the child

- **Spiky POV 3: Burnout is a measurable operating variable we design against, "good stress" is a myth, and early specialization is not required for elite results.**
   - **Elaboration:** The hothousing debate is stuck in a false binary: intensity and damage, or safety and mediocrity. The evidence dissolves it. First, the achievement case for hothousing is weak on its own terms. The predictors of rapid *junior* success sit close to the *opposite* of long-term senior success, and most elite performers specialize in mid-to-late adolescence, so "specialize early or fall behind" is not what the data says. This is the honest correction to BrainLift.1's SPOV 4 and BrainLift.2's SPOV 3: take the early *start*, refuse the early *lock-in*. Second, the harm does not come from volume. It comes from **controlled motivation and contingent self-worth**. In music schools, controlled motivation raises dropout (β ≈ .19) while autonomous motivation protects (β ≈ −.17), and encouraging parental involvement is the single strongest protective factor (β ≈ −.39); autonomy-supportive coaching predicts resilience (β ≈ .52). Third, retire the folk model of "optimal arousal." Yerkes-Dodson rests on a 1908 experiment with a handful of mice that never measured arousal, and both "challenge" and "hindrance" stress relate *negatively* to strain, so hard-but-motivating is not free. What follows is operational: validated burnout instruments (the School Burnout Inventory and kin), sleep floors (9–12h for 6–12s), distributed practice, scheduled deloads, and autonomy-supportive delivery, monitored as first-class program metrics. School burnout quadruples dropout risk, and high-pressure environments run 2–3× the population norms for anxiety and depression.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that intensity's damage is either inherent (so cap ambition) or negligible (so push harder), and that "good stress" is real.
   - **Backing:** the Frontiers (2023) junior-vs-senior specialization review; Salmela-Aro et al. (2009) School Burnout Inventory; Luthar & Becker (2002) on high-achieving-school risk; Curran & Hill (2019) on socially-prescribed perfectionism rising ~33% since the late 1980s; the CBT/mindfulness burnout meta-analysis (5 RCTs, exhaustion d ≈ −0.87); the music-dropout motivation study; the Yerkes-Dodson critique (2024). Honest limit: there is no well-validated *self-report* burnout instrument at age 8 (most bottom out at 9–10), so the youngest band needs observer/parent/teacher input; the athletic "deload" transfer to child academics is a reasoned synthesis, not a validated protocol; and the sport literature has a live null (one Frontiers perspective finds no direct specialization→burnout link), best reconciled as harm running *through* psychosocial pressure rather than training load itself.

### On the record the child builds

- **Spiky POV 4: The evidence record has to be legible and wanted by the child, or it is surveillance. Badges, points, and dashboards make it worse.**
   - **Elaboration:** A tamper-evident graph of a child's real process is only an asset if the child can read it and wants it to exist. The default EdTech move is to gamify it, badge it, dashboard it. On the evidence that backfires, and it backfires harder on children than on the adults these features were tested on. Expected, contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation *more* in children than in college students. A controlled trial found digital badges *reduced* intrinsic motivation and satisfied no competence need. A 38-study systematic review found **no evidence** that learner dashboards improve achievement, and later meta-analyses find small, heavily-moderated effects. Motivational "task-value" prompts *raised* anxiety in already-anxious children. What helps is narrow: **self-referential** progress (you against your own past, never a leaderboard) grounded in self-regulated learning, and disclosure surfaces built so that asking for help, including declared AI help, carries no shame. Nobody has tested a learning-record UI with this age band, but adjacent evidence gives concrete design levers. Children 9–11 grasp data and consent through **concrete, relational** framing ("asking before taking," not "data sharing"), reason about their own records through an interpersonal-control frame, and, left to default institutional framing, experience school data tracking as *surveillance*, sliding into "digital resignation" rather than ownership. On disclosure, a **simple** AI-use label outperformed a detailed one, and cheating-accusation anxiety rises with grade level. So we design the record to be understood and owned: concrete language, self-comparison, low-friction and low-shame disclosure, not maximized time-in-app. This is the anti-gamification discipline BrainLift.2 applied to the signal, now applied to the evidence UI.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that gamification (badges, points, leaderboards) and progress dashboards are safe, motivating defaults for children, and that a richer, more detailed record is a more trustworthy one.
   - **Backing:** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) on reward-undermining being worse for children; Balci & Morris (2026) badge RCT (n=210); the 38-study dashboard review (2023) and the 2025 learning-analytics meta-analyses (ES ≈ 0.36); MyReadscape (N=30, leaderboards drove only rank-competition, badges weakest); on the record itself, the CHI-2026 child-trust study (N=289, ages 9–11) and Agesilaou & Kyza (2022, n=63) on concrete-vs-abstract data understanding, Stoilova/Livingstone/Nandagiri (2020) on legibility being a system-design duty, and the Australian school-datafication focus groups on "digital resignation"; the ICO Children's Code and COPPA as the binding UX constraints. Honest limit: a dedicated scan *confirmed* there is **no located study** testing a learning-record, credential, provenance, or authorship UI with 8–14s on legibility/motivation/stigma, and the one AI-non-disclosure-shame study is on college students. The design levers above are inferred from adjacent child-data and child-AI-trust research, not from anyone having built and measured this record for a child. The reward-undermining meta and badge RCT are also adult-sampled (the overjustification literature says children are *more* susceptible, so that risk direction is at least conservative). This remains the pillar with the thinnest direct evidence, and the one the program has to generate itself.

- **Spiky POV 5: Every part of the EvidenceGraph is off-the-shelf; the moat is the composition plus the in-person defense, and the unsolved problem is verifiable deletion, not tamper-evidence.**
   - **Elaboration:** Two opposite priors are both wrong. First: "surely someone has already built a learning EvidenceGraph." No. No existing product combines content-addressed provenance, attestation, transparency logging, and verifiable credentials into a single tamper-evident learning DAG, and that integration appears novel. Second: "the hard part is proving authenticity." No. That part is *solved and commodity*. W3C Verifiable Credentials, Open Badges 3.0 and CLR 2.0, in-toto/SLSA attestations, Rekor/Trillian transparency logs, RO-Crate mapped to W3C PROV, C2PA content credentials, and shipping per-sentence authorship replay (Grammarly Authorship) are all mature and reusable. The uncomfortable truth carried from BrainLift.2 is that **none of them proves a human authored the work unaided**. C2PA states that it does not attest human authorship. So the trust root is still the sampled five-minute defense an in-person academy can run and a remote artifact-only competitor cannot. The real frontier is the opposite of what the crypto framing suggests: **verifiable deletion on an append-only store holding child data**. Regulators are explicit that technical immutability cannot excuse non-compliance with the right to erasure, and crypto-shredding is a supplement, not a complete guarantee. This is where the novel engineering, and the hardest pre-live gate, lives.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that this is either already built, or that its hard problem is authenticity/tamper-evidence.
   - **Backing:** the competitive-landscape synthesis (no integrated product found; parts all mature); Open Badges 3.0 / CLR 2.0 / W3C VC; in-toto, SLSA, Sigstore (Rekor/Trillian); Workflow-Run RO-Crate ↔ PROV; C2PA 2.1; EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 on immutability vs erasure; the authorship-verification evidence from BrainLift.2 (Weber-Wulff 2023; Liang 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). Honest limit: the novelty verdict is an inference from the *absence* of a contradicting product across the surveyed corpus, not from an affirmative survey of the whole field, so a proprietary implementation cannot be ruled out; the EDPB guidance is a public-consultation draft; and the oral-defense evidence is still drawn largely from higher education, thin for ages 8–14.

### On selecting into the program

- **Spiky POV 6: Equity in identification comes from universal screening and local norms, not from a "fairer" test or a higher bar. The referral system is where the bias lives.**
   - **Elaboration:** When gifted programs under-represent poor and minority children, the reflex is to blame the test and reach for a nonverbal "culture-fair" one. That is the wrong lever. The bias enters upstream, at *referral and nomination*: qualified Black, Hispanic, low-income, English-learner, and female students go under-referred. Replace referral with **universal screening** and the picture changes. In Broward County, gifted placement rose from ~3.3% to ~5.5% overall, and the odds of identification rose **+174%** for disadvantaged students (Hispanic +130%, Black +80%) *with no change to eligibility standards*. Discretion is measurable too: Black students taught by a Black teacher were identified **3×** as often as similar-ability peers taught by others. And the "culture-fair" fix backfires: nonverbal tests showed a *larger* Black–White gap (d ≈ .63) than achievement tests (d ≈ .57) or teacher ratings (d ≈ .34). The family brainlift ranks on family commitment above two floors; this SPOV hardens the *floor* itself so it stops discarding the children the program exists to find.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that under-representation is a test-bias problem solved by nonverbal tests and defended by high national-norm cutoffs.
   - **Backing:** Card & Giuliano (2016, PNAS) on universal screening; Grissom & Redding (2016, AERA Open) on teacher discretion; Dixson et al. (2025) on instrument gaps; Ozen et al. (2025) CogAT validity meta-analysis (r ≈ .63). Honest limit: the strongest causal evidence is single-district (Broward; Dixson's ~25,000-student district), several key papers are working papers not yet peer-reviewed, and there is a genuine unresolved tension over how much residual under-representation is biased *procedure* versus upstream *opportunity gaps* once scores are in.

### The unifying idea

- **Spiky POV 7: The honest, autonomy-preserving path is also the cheap and legal one. You do not need surveillance or frontier models to run this at scale.**
   - **Elaboration:** Across every SPOV above, the disciplined choice and the cheap choice keep turning out to be the same choice. Refusing biometrics is both the lawful path and the free one. Refusing gamification protects the signal and saves the build. And on raw compute, where everyone assumes elite AI tutoring must be ruinously expensive, the measured numbers run the other way. Serving real-time Socratic tutoring at scale runs on the order of **a few to a few tens of dollars per student per year**. The strongest real anchor is Khan Academy, which bundles GPT-4-based Khanmigo into district licenses at roughly $10–$15/student/year and cut that price from $60 to $35 in late 2023, attributing the cut to engineering work that lowered its compute cost. The dominant cost lever is not GPU count but **model selection and batching**: continuous batching plus paged attention swings cost-per-token by ~23–44× and let one operator halve its serving fleet, and difficulty-routing keeps ~95–97% of frontier quality at ~15–30% of cost (RouteLLM: ~85% savings retaining ~95% of GPT-4 quality, needing the strong model on only ~14% of queries; MixLLM: 97.25% quality at 24.18% cost). Small fine-tuned models match year-old 70B models on targeted tasks. And this composes with BrainLift.2's friction thesis: a system engineered to *withhold* the answer and run Socratic dialogue does not want a frontier model dispensing solutions. Cheap, small, and disciplined is not a compromise here. It is the design.
   - **Consensus it breaks:** that scale forces a choice between cost and quality, and that elite AI tutoring needs frontier models and heavy GPU spend.
   - **Backing:** the infrastructure cost synthesis (Khanmigo/Khan Academy district pricing and the 2023 price cut; the ITAS four-agent study at ~$2.63–$4.79/student/semester); RouteLLM (ICLR 2025); MixLLM (NAACL 2025); vLLM/PagedAttention and continuous-batching throughput benchmarks; follows from SPOV 2 (no biometrics), SPOV 4 (no gamification), and BrainLift.2 SPOV 5 (friction is the product). Honest limit: no source benchmarks the actual target of 100,000 concurrent K-8 students, so every figure is a component that must be composed; the per-student band leans heavily on a single non-peer-reviewed preprint extrapolating from a ≤50-user *graduate* deployment, so "converge" would overstate it; a large share of the GPU economics comes from single-vendor blogs with commercial self-interest; the routing headline savings are largely *oracle-router* ceilings, and practical routers can barely beat a trivial baseline and depend on judge accuracy; and the deepest gap is that LLMs only marginally reproduce established intelligent-tutoring adaptivity in benchmarks, so whether a small model clears the pedagogical *and* child-safety bar for young children is unresolved and must be validated, not assumed.

## DOK 3: Insights

Original conclusions we reached after processing the sources. These bridge the Knowledge Tree to the SPOVs.

### Reading the child

- **Insight 1:** "Free choice" is a near-constant, not a signal. When >88% of children endorse "this was my choice" regardless of whether they are about to quit, self-selection cannot rank durable liking, so a program built on "watch what they pick" is measuring noise. The buildable, measurable levers are competence and voluntary return. (→ SPOV 1)
- **Insight 2:** Competence and autonomy are jointly necessary and, at 9–12, do *not* form a hierarchy, though they are not equally *actionable*. Competence can be built and assessed; felt volition has to be protected. So the instrument targets competence-plus-return under autonomy support, not competence drilling. The distinction matters because building competence does not by itself manufacture interest (an 11-week skills program raised neither competence nor interest). Competence "wins" only as the *measurable, buildable* lever, not by out-predicting autonomy for durable liking. (→ SPOV 1, 3)
- **Insight 3:** In the youngest children, *actual* skill can predict future engagement better than *self-perceived* skill, so a self-report competence question is a weak proxy at 9–10 and the signal should lean on observed performance and behavior. (→ SPOV 1, 2)
- **Insight 4:** Inner state is unreadable from the body and reading it is illegal in EU schools, but behavior is readable and predictive. The field's blanket dismissal of "engagement detection" conflates two different things: facial/physiological affect (invalid) and behavioral engagement (valid). The signal model lives on the valid side of that line. (→ SPOV 2)

### Sustaining the child

- **Insight 5:** The achievement argument for early lock-in is weaker than assumed: rapid junior success and long-term senior success have nearly opposite predictors. This lets us keep BrainLift.1/2's early *start* while dropping the early *commitment* with no achievement cost to defend. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 6:** Burnout harm runs through controlled motivation and contingent worth, not through hours. Controlled motivation raises dropout and autonomous motivation protects, so an intensive program can hold expectations high while attacking the damage channel, the same autonomy-vs-control mechanism that decides consent in the family brainlift. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 7:** "Good stress" is not a safe assumption. Yerkes-Dodson is folklore, and both challenge and hindrance stress relate negatively to strain, so a program cannot excuse pressure as motivating. Recovery (sleep floors, spacing, deloads) is a first-class design element, not a nicety. (→ SPOV 3)
- **Insight 8:** Burnout is monitorable with validated instruments above ~age 9–10, but not by self-report at age 8, so the youngest band needs observer/parent/teacher signal. Any single cutoff is population-specific, so monitoring must be multi-source and calibrated, not a threshold alarm. (→ SPOV 3)

### The record the child builds

- **Insight 9:** The gamification default is a trap for our age band: rewards undermine intrinsic motivation *more* in children, badges can reduce it, and dashboards do not raise achievement. So the evidence UI restrains motivational features rather than adding them. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 10:** The progress view that helps is self-referential and SRL-grounded: you against your own past, actionable, never a leaderboard. And motivational prompts can raise anxiety in anxious children, so "encouragement" is not free. (→ SPOV 4)
- **Insight 11:** A dedicated scan confirmed there is no located study testing a learning-record/credential/provenance/authorship UI with 8–14s, so this remains an open design problem. Adjacent child-data research points the way: use concrete, relational framing ("asking before taking"), expect the default institutional framing to read as *surveillance* (children slide into "digital resignation"), keep disclosure simple (a simple AI-use label beat a detailed one), and treat legibility as a UI-design duty rather than something teaching fixes. (→ SPOV 4, and BrainLift.2 SPOV 6)
- **Insight 12:** The provenance stack is commodity and the integration is the novelty. Every layer exists and is battle-tested; the differentiated asset is composing them for a child plus pairing them with the in-person defense that artifact-only competitors cannot run. (→ SPOV 5)
- **Insight 13:** The hard problem is inverted from the marketing. Tamper-evidence is solved; *verifiable deletion on an append-only child-data store* is not, regulators reject immutability as an excuse, and this is the binding pre-live gate, the same erasure problem BrainLift.2 flagged, now confirmed as the frontier by the competitive scan. (→ SPOV 5)

### Selecting and serving

- **Insight 14:** The bias in gifted identification is upstream of the test, in referral and discretion. Universal screening moves representation with no change to standards, while nonverbal "culture-fair" tests can widen the gap, so the fix is a process change, not a test swap. This hardens the family brainlift's child-side floor. (→ SPOV 6)
- **Insight 15:** Serving elite AI tutoring at scale is cheap when architected right: routing and batching, not GPUs, set the bill, and small models increasingly suffice. The *cost* objection to 100k-scale Socratic tutoring is an architecture problem the systems literature has addressed. The *fitness* objection is not: routing's best numbers are oracle ceilings, and no one has shown a small model clears the pedagogy-and-safety bar for young children at this scale. Cost is solved on paper; pedagogy at scale is not. (→ SPOV 7)
- **Insight 16:** The disciplined path and the cheap path coincide. Refusing biometrics, refusing gamification, and running small routed models under a friction design are each at once the more ethical, the more legal, and the less expensive choice. That unifying claim makes the whole system operable at scale rather than only defensible in principle. (→ SPOV 7)

## DOK 2: Knowledge Tree

Organized sources and the facts and summaries we built our insights on. DOK 1 facts are raw from the source; DOK 2 summaries are in our own words.

### Category 1: Reading the child

#### Subcategory 1.1: Competence versus free choice as a durable-liking signal

- **Source: Quested et al. (2013), youth-sport motivation across five countries**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - N ≈ 8,000 youth players, mean age 11.56, five countries.
      - The free-choice "this is my choice" item was endorsed by more than 88% of children in every country and was dropped for non-discrimination.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - A self-selection item so near-universally endorsed cannot separate durable enthusiasm from imminent dropout, which is the core evidence that "free choice" is a weak passion signal.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2013_Quested_et_al.pdf

- **Source: Meta-analysis, Review of Educational Research (2022), SDT needs and motivation**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 144 samples, 79,079 participants (sample mean age 16.55).
      - Relative weights for self-determined motivation: competence 43.21%, autonomy 34.49%, relatedness 22.31%; competence-IM and autonomy-IM correlations near-equal (ρ .58 vs .57).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Competence carries the largest share among the three needs for self-determined motivation, supporting a competence-first instrument while keeping autonomy close behind.
   - **Link:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8935530/

- **Source: Finnish 6-year longitudinal (J. Science and Medicine in Sport, 2015); Barnett et al. (2008); actual-vs-perceived competence (PubMed 29016237)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Finnish study (n=333, baseline mean age 12.4, Grade 7→12): Grade-7 perceived competence predicted later physical activity (β = .28 total METs, .29 vigorous); Grade-7 autonomous motivation and enjoyment were *not* significant predictors.
      - Barnett et al. (2008): childhood object-control skill predicted adolescent activity (18% variance) and fitness (30%) ~6 years later; adding the perceived-competence path nearly doubled R².
      - Actual-vs-perceived (N=201, ages 6–9): actual motor competence explained 9–30% of activity variance vs 0–5% for perceived competence.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Competence, and *actual* competence most of all in the youngest children, out-predicts autonomous motivation and enjoyment for later engagement, which is why competence is the durable, buildable signal and why self-report competence is a weak proxy at the youngest ages.
   - **Link:** (Barnett/PASS and Finnish longitudinal as cited in the 2026-07-29 competence research report)

- **Source: Fresh 9–12 deep-research (2026-08-01): Mabbe et al. (2018); Patall et al. (2008); Deci/Koestner/Ryan (1999); Skjesol et al. (2025); Childhood-and-Beyond cohort (2013)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Mabbe et al. (2018, n=110, mean age 10.71): competence satisfaction predicted challenge-seeking (b = .31, p < .001) while autonomy satisfaction did not (b = .01, ns); competence manipulation partial η² = .65 on competence satisfaction and .33 on intrinsic motivation, vs .07–.08 for the autonomy manipulation.
      - Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999): positive verbal reward/feedback effect on free-choice intrinsic motivation d = 0.11 (ns) for children vs d = 0.43 for college students; age difference Qb(1) = 5.14, p < .02.
      - Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008): choice raises intrinsic motivation d = 0.30 overall and d = 0.51 for primary-school students; a 2019 experiment (N=126, mean age 10.8) found the choice effect mediated *jointly* by autonomy and competence.
      - Skjesol et al. (2025, RCT n=175, ages 9–12): an 11-week competence-oriented soccer program raised neither perceived competence nor interest/value; interest/enjoyment declined significantly in both arms.
      - Childhood-and-Beyond (2013): child perceived competence predicted the *amount* of later participation, not *whether* the child continued; only parental influence predicted continuation.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - At 9–12 the direct tests split: competence drives challenge-seeking, but competence feedback does not raise children's free-choice motivation and free choice does. So competence and autonomy are parallel and jointly necessary rather than a hierarchy; competence's advantage is being buildable and measurable, and building it does not by itself manufacture durable interest.
   - **Link:** https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2018_MabbeSoenensVansteenkiste_ExperimentalChildPsych.pdf ; https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint7040101

#### Subcategory 1.2: Why inner state cannot be read from the body

- **Source: Barrett et al. (2019), "Emotional expressions reconsidered"; EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) / Recital 44**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Emotion "cannot be reliably read from facial configurations"; variability is the norm.
      - The EU AI Act prohibits emotion inference in education (in force 2 February 2025).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Reading emotion off a face is scientifically unfounded and, in EU schools, illegal, so the program never does it.
   - **Link:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100619832930

- **Source: rPPG skin-tone bias (npj Digital Medicine, 2025); voice-stress literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Remote photoplethysmography mean absolute error ~4.23 bpm (Fitzpatrick I–V) rising to ~13.58 bpm (type VI), >2× degradation; "in the wild" correlation with true heart rate only r ≈ 0.58.
      - Voice-stress analysis is characterized as essentially pseudoscience.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Even where a physiological sensor is partly valid it is biased by skin tone and brittle in real conditions, and the further leap to "stress" or "emotion" is unsupported, so behavioral signals, not biometrics, are the only defensible instrument.
   - **Link:** (npj Digital Medicine 2025 rPPG study, as cited in the 2026-07-21 burnout research report)

### Category 2: Sustaining the child

#### Subcategory 2.1: Burnout as a measurable variable, and the "good stress" myth

- **Source: Salmela-Aro et al. (2009), School Burnout Inventory**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 10 items, 3 factors (exhaustion, cynicism, inadequacy); original N = 1,418 Finnish adolescents; three-factor fit RMSEA .06, CFI .96.
      - School burnout is associated with roughly 4× higher dropout risk.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - A validated, short instrument means burnout can be monitored as a program metric rather than guessed at, though its validation floor sits around ages 9–10, not 8.
   - **Link:** https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/abs/10.1027/1015-5759.25.1.48

- **Source: Yerkes-Dodson critique (Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2024); challenge/hindrance stress literature**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - The inverted-U "optimal arousal" law rests on a 1908 study using 2–4 mice that never measured arousal.
      - Both challenge and hindrance stressors relate negatively to psychological strain and physical health.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - "Good stress" is not a safe design assumption; recovery and load management are required, not optional, in an intensive program.
   - **Link:** (Yerkes-Dodson critique, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 2024, as cited in the 2026-07-21 burnout research report)

- **Source: Luthar & Becker (2002); Curran & Hill (2019); CBT/mindfulness burnout meta-analysis (5 RCTs)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Affluent/high-pressure youth run 2–3× population norms for anxiety, depression, and substance use.
      - Socially-prescribed perfectionism rose ~33% from the late 1980s to the late 2010s.
      - Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (ages 11–23): CBT/mindfulness reduced exhaustion d = −0.87, sport devaluation d = −0.77, reduced accomplishment d = −0.74; CBT outperformed mindfulness for exhaustion.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The risk is real and rising, and it is treatable, which is why safeguards are built in as first-class features rather than bolted on after harm appears.
   - **Link:** https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000138.pdf

#### Subcategory 2.2: Long-term risks of early specialization, and what protects

- **Source: Frontiers in Sports & Active Living (2023), early- vs late-specialization review**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Predictors of rapid junior performance and of long-term senior performance are largely opposite; most elite athletes specialize in mid-to-late adolescence.
      - One specialization-exhaustion study (n=169, mean age 16.29) found higher emotional/physical exhaustion in specialized athletes (p = .030, d = −0.34).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Early single-domain lock-in is not required for elite outcomes, so the program keeps the early start and engineers out the lock-in, with the honest caveat that a competing perspective finds no direct specialization→burnout link, best reconciled as harm running through psychosocial pressure.
   - **Link:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1175718/full

- **Source: Music-school dropout/motivation study; autonomy-supportive coaching SEM; Saarinen (2025)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Controlled motivation raised dropout (β = 0.19); autonomous motivation was protective (β = −0.17); encouraging parental involvement was the strongest protective factor (β = −0.39).
      - Autonomy-supportive coaching predicted psychological resilience (β = 0.522).
      - ~50% of musical participants drop out by age 17; parental over-involvement showed a U-shaped harm pattern (Saarinen 2025).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Whether an intense pursuit harms depends on autonomy support and motivation quality, not on volume, and both too-little and too-much parental pressure hurt. That is the direct design basis for autonomy-supportive delivery and for the family brainlift's warm-demanding screen.
   - **Link:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1469029225000305

### Category 3: The record the child builds

#### Subcategory 3.1: How children and families experience evidence and credential systems

- **Source: Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); Balci & Morris (2026) badge RCT; 38-study dashboard review (2023); 2025 learning-analytics meta-analyses**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Expected, contingent tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, and the effect is *more* severe for children than college students (Deci et al. 1999).
      - A digital-badge RCT (n=210) found badges reduced intrinsic motivation and satisfied no competence need (sample: college students).
      - A 38-study systematic review found *no evidence* dashboards improve academic achievement; 2025 meta-analyses find ES ≈ 0.36 (23 studies, 9,710 participants), heavily moderated by developmental level.
      - Motivational "task-value" prompts increased anxiety among already-anxious learners.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The standard gamified/dashboard playbook is counter-productive for this age band, so the evidence UI restrains motivational features and shows only self-referential progress.
   - **Link:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1506824 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042

- **Source: MyReadscape (Nature HSS Communications); Molenaar et al. (LAK 2020); ICO Children's Code; COPPA**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - MyReadscape (N=30, ages ~7–9): a public leaderboard motivated 79% of interviewed children but fostered only rank-based competition; badges had the weakest motivational impact of all features.
      - Molenaar et al.: personalized trace-data visualizations improved accuracy and transfer for ages ~7–9.
      - COPPA governs under-13; the UK Children's Code (Age Appropriate Design Code) sets age-appropriate defaults.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Self-referential, SRL-grounded progress helps and competitive/badge features do not, inside binding child-data constraints, so legibility and self-comparison, not ranking, are the design targets.
   - **Link:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-06750-x ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3375462.3375465 ; https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/

- **Source: Fresh deep-research (2026-08-01): CHI-2026 child-trust study; Agesilaou & Kyza (2022); Stoilova/Livingstone/Nandagiri (2020); Australian school-datafication focus groups; CDT K-12 AI survey**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - No located study tests a learning-record, digital-credential, data-provenance, or authorship-tracking UI with children aged 8–14 on legibility/motivation/stigma; the one AI-non-disclosure-shame study (survey n=97 + interviews) is on college students.
      - CHI-2026 child-trust study (N=289, ages 9–11): children engage with consent only when framed concretely ("asking before taking," not "data sharing") and object to systems "taking things without asking."
      - Agesilaou & Kyza (2022, n=63 fifth/sixth-graders): children misunderstood shared/platform ownership of their own self-generated data.
      - Australian secondary focus groups (n=62): students framed school datafication through "power, surveillance and affect," reporting "digital resignation" and perceived powerlessness; CDT survey: ~half of students said classroom AI use made them feel less connected to their teacher.
      - Disclosure UI: a simple AI-use label outperformed a detailed one (adolescent sample); cheating-accusation anxiety rises with grade level.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - The direct evidence gap is confirmed, but adjacent child-data research yields concrete levers: concrete relational framing, surveillance-avoidance, simple disclosure, and legibility-as-UI-duty. That is why the evidence UI is a problem the program must design and measure itself, not copy.
   - **Link:** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790765 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212868922000058 ; https://www.cogitatiopress.com/mediaandcommunication/article/view/3407

#### Subcategory 3.2: Verifying authorship in the AI era

- **Source: Weber-Wulff et al. (2023); Liang et al. (2023); Krishna et al. (2023); OpenAI/Turnitin/Vanderbilt**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - 14 detectors across 754 cases were "neither accurate nor reliable"; none exceeded 80% accuracy.
      - Seven GPT detectors misclassified non-native (TOEFL) English at an average 61.3% false-positive rate; all seven flagged 19.8% of TOEFL essays.
      - Paraphrasing dropped DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% accuracy at a fixed 1% false-positive rate.
      - OpenAI's own classifier (26% true-positive, 9% false-positive) was discontinued; Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector (Aug 2023), estimating ~750 wrongly-flagged papers/year at claimed rates.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - AI-text detection is inaccurate, easily defeated, and biased against the most vulnerable students, so it must never trigger a consequence for a child. This is carried from BrainLift.2 and reconfirmed by the 2026 evidence.
   - **Link:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z ; https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899(23)00130-7

- **Source: Zhang et al. (2024, ICML); C2PA specification; interactive oral assessment (Dawson; Sotiriadou et al. 2020)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Strong watermarking of generative text is formally impossible under natural assumptions.
      - C2PA attests who signed, when, and with what tool, but "makes no judgment about whether provenance is 'true'" and does not attest human authorship.
      - Interactive oral assessment demonstrated resilience to misconduct including generative-AI misuse; the evidence base is mostly higher-education.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - No artifact-level test proves human authorship, so the trust root is process plus a sampled human defense, the check an in-person academy is built to run.
   - **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04378 ; https://philldawson.com/

#### Subcategory 3.3: The provenance stack and the deletion frontier

- **Source: Open Badges 3.0 / CLR 2.0 / W3C VC; in-toto/SLSA; Sigstore (Rekor/Trillian); Workflow-Run RO-Crate ↔ PROV; C2PA; Grammarly Authorship**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Open Badges 3.0 aligns to the W3C Verifiable Credentials data model and defines an Evidence class; CLR 2.0 wraps signed achievement credentials.
      - in-toto/SLSA bind signed metadata to artifacts by digest; Rekor/Trillian provide append-only transparency logs; Workflow-Run RO-Crate maps to W3C PROV; C2PA chains signed content provenance.
      - Grammarly Authorship ships per-sentence AI/human/web attribution plus writing-process replay (version-history, not cryptographic).
      - No existing product combines these into a single tamper-evident, content-addressed learning-provenance DAG; that integration appears novel.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Every building block is mature and reusable, so the differentiated asset is the composition for a child plus the in-person defense, not any single cryptographic layer.
   - **Link:** https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/ob/v3p0 ; https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.0/provenance ; https://www.grammarly.com/authorship

- **Source: EDPB Guidelines 02/2025; CNIL blockchain/GDPR guidance; crypto-shredding**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - EDPB (02/2025, public-consultation draft): technical immutability "cannot be invoked to justify non-compliance" with the right to erasure; crypto-shredding is a supplement, not a complete guarantee.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Verifiable deletion on an append-only child-data store is the unsolved problem and the binding pre-live gate, the opposite of the "authenticity is the hard part" framing.

### Category 4: Selecting and serving at scale

#### Subcategory 4.1: Equitable identification

- **Source: Card & Giuliano (2016), universal screening in Broward County**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Gifted placement rose ~3.3% → 5.5% overall; odds of identification rose +174% for disadvantaged students, +130% Hispanic, +80% Black, with no change to eligibility standards.
      - Newly identified "complier" students had IQ distributions similar to referral-identified peers.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Universal screening, not a different test, is what surfaces under-represented talent; the bias lives in referral, which screening removes.
   - **Link:** https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1605043113

- **Source: Grissom & Redding (2016); Dixson et al. (2025); Ozen et al. (2025)**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Black students taught by Black teachers were identified ~3× more often than similar-ability peers taught by other-race teachers (ECLS-K).
      - Nonverbal ability tests showed a *larger* Black–White gap (d ≈ .63) than achievement tests (d ≈ .57) or teacher ratings (d ≈ .34).
      - CogAT meta-analytic criterion validity r ≈ .63 (24 studies).
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Discretion injects measurable bias and "culture-fair" nonverbal tests can widen the gap, so the fix is a process change (universal screening, local norms), not a test swap. CogAT remains a valid instrument once the process around it is fixed.
   - **Link:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2332858415622175 ; https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai25-1118.pdf

#### Subcategory 4.2: Serving AI tutoring at scale

- **Source: Khanmigo / Khan Academy pricing (incl. 2023 price cut); ITAS four-agent tutoring study**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - Khanmigo consumer: $4/mo ($44/yr); Khan Academy Districts: ~$10–$15/student/year bundled (≥1,000 seats); standalone Student Tutor add-on list price up to $90; above 1,000 licenses is quote-only.
      - Khan Academy publicly cut the district Khanmigo price from $60 to $35/student/year (Nov 2023), attributing the cut to engineering work that reduced computational cost.
      - ITAS four-agent Gemini-2.5-Flash study (measured, 3,100 requests): ~7,438 tokens/interaction, ~$0.0039–$0.0071 each; a 675-question semester ≈ $2.63–$4.79/student (projection); median latency held flat 3.5–4.0s from 1–50 concurrent users on Priority tier.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Real-time Socratic tutoring at scale costs on the order of a few to a few tens of dollars per student per year, and a vendor cutting price while citing lower compute cost is the cleanest non-marketing signal that the trend is downward, though the per-student band still leans on one graduate-scale preprint and no source reaches the 100k-K-8 target.
   - **Link:** https://www.khanacademy.org/schools/pricing ; https://thejournal.com/articles/2023/11/16/khan-academy-cuts-district-price-of-khanmigo-ai-teaching-assistant.aspx ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.24110

- **Source: RouteLLM (ICLR 2025); MixLLM (NAACL 2025); continuous-batching benchmarks; small-model capability**
   - **DOK 1 - Facts:**
      - RouteLLM: ~85% cost savings retaining ~95% of GPT-4 quality, needing the strong model on only ~14% of queries.
      - MixLLM: 97.25% of GPT-4 quality at 24.18% of cost (~4× reduction).
      - Continuous batching yields 3–24× higher throughput; the cost-per-token swing scales with batch depth, ~23–44× at batch-128 (the fresh-scan figure) up to ~100× at batch-256 (~$258/M → ~$2.30/M on 8×H100 serving a 70B model).
      - Phi-4 (14B): 84.8 MMLU, 82.6 HumanEval, beating a prior-generation 70B on code.
      - Caveat from the fresh scan: the ~95–97% quality-at-15–30%-cost figures are largely *oracle-router* ceilings; practical routers can barely beat a trivial baseline and depend on judge accuracy, and benchmarks show current LLMs only marginally reproduce established intelligent-tutoring-system adaptivity.
   - **DOK 2 - Summary:**
      - Model routing and batching, not GPU count, set the bill, and small models increasingly suffice, so architecture, not spend, is the cost lever. But the routing gains are best-case and the pedagogical fitness of small models for young children at scale is unproven, so the cost case is far stronger than the pedagogy case.
   - **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/2603.04445v2 ; https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27457
